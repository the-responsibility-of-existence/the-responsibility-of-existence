Abstract

In one sentence: when you exercise discretionary influence over the conditions under which others live, choose and develop, and when they cannot exit or contest that influence effectively, you owe them an account of it — and you cannot be the sole judge of whether that account is good enough.

Structural premise. The framework rests on a claim about the structure of understanding, not on a claim about the cosmos. Every level of understanding is dependent on levels it cannot fully understand, and is depended on by levels that cannot fully understand it. This is not a metaphysical assertion; it is a structural feature of any system in which understanding exists at all. From it, the framework derives its three parts — J, ECP and T5 — as a single argument rather than three useful tools (§2).

Ground. J: arrangements that systematically constrain others' conditions must provide at least one of meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation. J follows from the premise: if every level's understanding is incomplete, contestation is the structural remedy. J is primarily epistemic, abductively supported, and has stated defeaters (§3.2).

Standing and symbiosis. Four tiers of standing under uncertainty with an explicit plausibility threshold (§5). Symbiosis specified with observable indicators (S1–S3) — conceptually distinct from answerability, though measurement may require contestable evidence (§6.1). Collective contestation may represent silent parties but must not undermine individual contestation.

Activation. A discretion gate plus three drivers (entrenchment, severity, contestation deficit) determining one of three levels of duty, with anchors and a rating protocol (§7–10).

Structure. A floor of minimum conditions, the prohibition on being sole judge of one's own influence — which follows from the premise: no level fully understands itself — and accounts of conflict, future generations and learning (§11–14).

Application and testing. Worked cases including one that appears to refute the framework (§15.2b), five proposed evaluation conditions with independent measures and preregistration requirements (§16.2), and modular replacement conditions (§16.3).

Answerability is the procedure. Symbiosis is the ideal it serves. The framework has not been empirically tested. Its activation rule is specified so that it can be; its other parts are argued and offered for contestation.

The argument in brief

  1. The premise. Every level of understanding is dependent on levels it cannot fully understand, and is depended on by levels that cannot fully understand it. The stone does not understand; the human understands more, but not everything; whatever is higher than the human would understand more, but not everything either. The asymmetry does not end at any level (§2).
  2. Why the premise is necessary. Without it, J, ECP and T5 are three useful tools that happen to share a document. With it, they are the same argument at three levels of application: the structural incompleteness of understanding generates the duty to answer (§2.3).
  3. The problem. Influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act is not adequately covered by existing moral vocabulary (§1).
  4. The principle (J). Arrangements that systematically constrain others' conditions must, for those affected, provide at least one of: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation. J follows from the premise: the actor cannot fully see what it shapes, so contestation is the structural remedy (§3.1).
  5. The trigger. Influence creates the duty when discretion is present and exit or review is unavailable or ineffective. How much duty scales with entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit (§8–9).
  6. The epistemic claim. Under asymmetric influence, the more powerful party has weaker grounds for treating its own assessment as decisive. It therefore bears a heightened burden to obtain independent evidence and correction from those affected (§6.2).
  7. The limits. The framework handles domination; it does not solve it. Its tests are proposed, not run. None of this is a proof. It is an invitation to argue.

If you read only one section after this page, read §2 — that is where the framework's ground is argued. If you read only one passage, read §2.3 — that is where the necessity of the premise is shown.

Part I

Ground: Structure, Principle, Scope

1.The Problem, Scope and Terms

The capacity to shape other people's conditions has grown faster than the practices for answering for it.

Recommendation systems, credit scoring, clinical triage tools and AI assistants shape the conditions of millions. So do states writing policy, companies setting terms, and, at a different scale, clinicians, teachers, landlords and parents. Existing moral vocabulary covers parts of this territory: blame for wrongful acts, rights for protected interests, consent for voluntary exchanges, regulation for what law has already caught. What falls between them is influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act — and that nonetheless determines what others can do and become.

1.1 What the framework does and does not do

It answers four questions: when does influence create a duty to give an account; how demanding is that duty; who must discharge it; and how can review be organised so that it does not become a new instrument of domination. It does not tell anyone what to decide. It is not a decision theory, and it is not a substitute for law, rights or substantive standards of justice.

1.2 Scope conditions: when the framework does not apply

  • Symmetrical relations. Where parties have roughly equal capacity to exit, review and contest, the framework adds little.
  • Relations governed by enforceable rights and effective regulation. Where law provides effective rights, review and remedies, the framework may add little. Formal legality alone is not enough: rights may be inaccessible, remedies delayed, or review structurally dependent on the actor.
  • Purely causal influence without discretion. A person swept away by a flood does not owe an account to those downstream.
  • Trivial influence. A café changing its menu is not the case the framework addresses. The severity floor in §8 is meant to exclude it.
  • Relations where the affected party is not a candidate for standing. Stones and rivers as such do not have standing under this framework (§5.1). They matter through the beings that depend on them, and through the structural premise (§2).

1.3 Terms

Influence: the exercise or maintenance of control over conditions under which others act, choose, develop or fare. Maintaining a structure one could alter counts as influence.

Actor: a person, organisation or institution, including those that design, deploy and maintain technical systems. Technical systems are treated as instruments; answerability falls on the humans and organisations behind them.

Affected party: any being whose interests are materially affected by the influence.

Discretion: the actor has genuine alternatives at tolerable cost and a role in setting the influence.

Answerability: a duty to give reasons for the conditions one helps create or maintain, to those affected or their representatives; to permit examination of those reasons; and to remain open to correction. Distinct from blame, from liability and from authority.

Symbiosis: relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation.

1.4 Relation to existing work

Debts: Pettit and Lovett on domination, Forst on the right to justification, Scanlon on reasonable rejection, Young on structural responsibility, Bovens and Shoemaker on accountability, Tronto, Held and Goodin on care and vulnerability, Jonas on the imperative of responsibility. The contribution is comparative and operational, with a limited philosophical proposal — the epistemic burden principle — and a structural premise that makes the framework one argument rather than three.

2.The Structural Premise

Understanding is structurally incomplete. No level fully understands its own dependencies; no level fully understands what depends on it. From this, the framework's three parts follow.

The framework rests on a claim about the structure of understanding, not on a claim about the cosmos. The claim is this: every level of understanding is dependent on levels it cannot fully understand, and is depended on by levels that cannot fully understand it. This is not a metaphysical assertion about what exists. It is a structural feature of any system in which understanding exists at all.

The stone does not understand. The human understands more than the stone, but not everything. Whatever is higher than the human — if there is such a thing — would understand more than the human, but not everything either. The asymmetry does not end at any level. No level fully understands its own dependencies. No level fully understands what depends on it. The chain does not end because understanding does not end.

Every level of understanding is structurally incomplete. It depends on levels it cannot fully understand, and is depended on by levels that cannot fully understand it. There is no level at which this asymmetry disappears. It is not a defect of any particular understanding; it is a feature of understanding as such.

From this premise, three things follow: the duty to answer (J), the allocation of duties by capacity (ECP), and the prohibition on any actor being the sole judge of its own influence (T5).

2.1 The stone

A stone serves no clear purpose for us. We build with it, or we walk past it. It does not know that it contributes to the settling of soil, to the flow of water, to the slow chemistry of the ground. It cannot know this. A stone can only be a stone. It does not need to understand higher things than itself, because it is not the kind of thing that understands.

But the stone's inability to understand is not a defect in the stone. It is a feature of the relationship between the stone and the thing that can understand. The higher thing — the being that can understand — must understand that the stone has a place. Not because the stone demands it, but because the higher thing's own understanding is incomplete: it cannot see what the stone contributes, and it must not mistake the limits of its understanding for the limits of the stone's place.

This is the first function of the premise: it explains why the higher thing's responsibility does not come from the stone's claim on it, but from the structure of the relationship itself. The stone cannot make a claim. The higher thing bears responsibility anyway.

2.2 The chain

The same structure holds at every level. A human serves purposes it does not fully understand — in the family, the institution, the ecosystem, the species. A human can understand more than a stone, but not everything. And whatever is higher than the human — whether that is something we can name or not — has something greater than itself.

This is not a hierarchy with a top. It is a structural feature: no level fully understands its own dependencies, and no level fully understands what depends on it. The chain does not end because understanding does not end. There is no level at which the asymmetry disappears.

This is the framework's answer to the question that any hierarchical ethics must face: who watches the watcher? The answer is not a top. There is no top. There is a structure in which every level is dependent on levels above and below it, and in which every level that can understand bears responsibility for levels it can understand less well.

2.3 Why the premise is necessary

The framework's three parts can each be defended separately. J can be defended by abduction: unaccountable power produces epistemic risk. ECP can be defended by pragmatics: who can build what. T5 can be defended by design: visibility as precondition. But separately, they do not add up to one framework. They add up to three useful tools that happen to share a document.

The premise is what makes them a single argument.

J follows from the premise. If every level's understanding is incomplete, then no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. Contestation is the structural remedy: it brings in what the actor cannot see. J is not an epistemic precaution added to the framework from outside; it is the form that responsibility takes when understanding is structurally incomplete.

ECP follows from the premise. If responsibility scales with the capacity to understand, then the higher bears greater responsibility downward — not because it is entitled to rule, but because its greater understanding is what generates its greater responsibility. The front-line worker who cannot build a review board is not less responsible as a being; she is less responsible for the mechanism, because the mechanism is beyond her capacity to understand. The institution that can understand what is at stake bears the duty that she cannot.

T5 follows from the premise. If no level fully understands itself, then no level is the sole judge of its own influence. The publication requirement does not prevent capture; it makes visible what the structural premise says must be there: the incompleteness of every level's self-understanding. An actor that refuses to publish its indicators is claiming a completeness it cannot have.

What this changes. Without the premise, J is an epistemic precaution, ECP is a pragmatic allocation, and T5 is a design constraint. With the premise, they are the same argument at three levels of application: the structural incompleteness of understanding generates the duty to answer, the allocation of that duty by capacity, and the prohibition on any actor treating its own assessment as final.

This is what the premise does that no other ground does. It does not merely support the framework's parts; it shows that they are one argument.

2.4 What would refute the premise

  • C1 — There is a level that fully understands itself. If there exists a level of understanding that is not dependent on levels it cannot fully understand — that is, if the structural asymmetry has a top — then the premise fails, and with it the framework's claim that no actor is the sole judge.
  • C2 — Responsibility does not follow from understanding. If it can be shown that greater understanding does not generate greater responsibility for the conditions of others, ECP's derivation from the premise fails.
  • C3 — The incompleteness is contingent. If it can be shown that the incompleteness of understanding is a feature of our current knowledge rather than of understanding as such, then the premise is empirical, not structural, and the framework's ground is empirical too.
  • C4 — The premise does no work. If it can be shown that J, ECP and T5 are just as coherent without the premise as with it, then the premise is decoration, not ground. §2.3 is the answer to this objection; whether the answer succeeds is for the reader to judge.

C4 is the one the framework most needs to answer. A premise that does no work is not a ground; it is a gesture. The framework's claim is that §2.3 shows the premise does work that no other ground does: it makes the three parts one argument. If that claim fails, the framework is three tools, not one.

2.5 What the premise is not

It is not a claim that the universe is hierarchical. It is not a claim that there is a highest being. It is not a claim that stones have standing. It is not a claim that the higher is entitled to rule the lower. It is a structural claim about the incompleteness of understanding, and about what follows from that incompleteness for responsibility.

A reader who rejects the premise can still use the framework's procedures. But they should know that without the premise, the framework is three tools, not one argument. The premise is offered as the ground that makes it one — not as a proof, but as the claim without which the framework's parts do not cohere.

3.J, and What Would Refute It

A thin, disjunctive principle that follows from the structural premise, and that has its defeaters stated in advance.

3.1 The principle

Any arrangement that systematically and non-trivially constrains the conditions under which others act, choose or develop must, for those affected, provide at least one of three things: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification that is genuinely open to their contestation. Where exit is available at tolerable cost and review is effective, justification need not be owed. Where they are not, justification is required, and the strength of the requirement scales with how thoroughly exit and review fail.

J is deliberately thin. It does not say what is good or who prevails when interests conflict. It says what kind of relationship must exist between those who shape conditions and those who live in them: one in which the affected are not simply subject to unaccountable power. The three alternatives share the same function — to prevent the actor from being the sole judge of its own influence.

Why disjunctive. A monistic J is vulnerable to whichever rival it excludes. The disjunctive formulation avoids making one safeguard mandatory in every case. A rival that preserves the same protection with less cost or greater clarity should replace it.

J follows from the structural premise. If every level's understanding is incomplete (§2.3), then no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. Contestation is the structural remedy: it brings in what the actor cannot see. J is not a precaution added to the framework from outside; it is the form responsibility takes when understanding is structurally incomplete. The three alternatives — exit, review, justification — are three ways of supplying what the actor's own understanding cannot.

J is primarily epistemic, not moral. It is not a claim that justification makes actions right. It is a claim that actors who cannot be contested are more likely to be wrong, and that contestation is the best available protection against this. The epistemic framing is the old principle that no one should be judge in their own cause, applied to influence. On the structural premise, it has a deeper ground: no level of understanding can be judge in its own cause, because no level fully understands its own dependencies.

J is defensible, not necessary in the logical sense. The framework does not claim that J is a necessary truth or a transcendental precondition of agency. It claims that J is abductively defensible and structurally grounded: it explains recurring risks of unchecked discretion, and it follows from the incompleteness of understanding. Rejecting J does not commit a critic to accepting domination.

3.2 What would refute J: stated defeaters

  • D1 — The unaccountable-power hypothesis is false. If actors with unaccountable discretion over others' conditions do not systematically produce worse outcomes — measured independently of the actors' own reports — than actors subject to exit, review or contestation, J's epistemic justification collapses.
  • D2 — A better principle explains the same cases. If a rival principle explains all the cases J explains, does so more simply, and generates clearer practical guidance, J should be replaced.
  • D3 — The structural derivation fails. If J does not follow from the structural premise — if the incompleteness of understanding does not generate a duty to answer — then J's ground is abductive only, and the framework's unity is lost.
  • D4 — The costs exceed the benefits. If adopting J produces worse outcomes for the affected than not adopting it — measured by independent indicators — J should be abandoned.

3.3 Why J: an abductive justification

J also has an abductive justification, which stands even if the structural derivation is contested. The framework rests on an abductive inference: J is proposed as a compact explanation of a set of recurring risks associated with unaccountable power.

  • Actors with unaccountable discretion have incentives and opportunities to overestimate their benevolence and their understanding of affected parties.
  • Exit, review and contestation can expose errors that an actor's internal assessment may miss; whether they do so reliably is empirical.
  • Errors may cluster around the actor's own interests and categories: the powerful party's categories can become the default, while the less powerful party's perspective is treated as a deviation.
  • Institutions with effective safeguards may perform better on some relevant measures, but selection effects and institutional context complicate causal comparisons.

Four considerations, not four independent proofs. The framework draws on four families of consideration that point toward J: the justificatory (Scanlon, Forst), the republican (Pettit, Lovett), the epistemic, and the relational (Tronto, Held, Goodin). They are not fully independent. The epistemic consideration is primary; the structural premise is the framework's own ground for it. The convergence is offered as support, not as proof.

3.4 Rivals, taken in their best forms

Consequentialism. Sophisticated consequentialists can accept J as an institutional implication. The real disagreement is whether J is basic or derived, and it matters where aggregate welfare seems to favour overriding contestation. The framework sides with contestation in severe cases for epistemic reasons, while permitting temporary override in emergencies subject to sunset and review (§13.2).

Kantianism. Shares the core but grounds standing in rational agency, which creates problems for infants, the cognitively disabled and animals. §5 handles these through tiers.

Virtue ethics. Supports the humility the framework requires, but is weak on structural questions: who reviews whom, and by what procedure.

Justice as fairness and capabilities. Supply substantive accounts of what is at stake. The framework is procedural and can draw on these to calibrate severity (§10).

Rights. Rights are outputs. What the framework adds is an account of when and how actors must justify influence in the space that rights and regulation leave open.

Realism. The realist who denies that power owes anything to the weak cannot be refuted, only outlasted. The framework's reply is that the realist who argues for realism already relies on the practice of argument (objection 1).

3.5 The power objection

The objection. A procedural framework that tells the powerful how to justify themselves may simply give them a new vocabulary for doing what they were going to do anyway. Proceduralism legitimates power by giving it a veneer of accountability.

Reply. First, the objection is partly correct, and the framework says so. That is why §12.4 treats T5 as a precondition rather than a diagnosis. Second, the objection is not specific to proceduralism — any framework can be captured. The question is whether a framework has internal resources against capture that its rivals lack. The framework's claim is that T5-as-precondition is such a resource: it does not prevent capture, but it makes the capture visible, and it downgrades the legitimacy of a captured process. Third, the framework's modesty is not an admission of failure: a framework that claimed to solve the problem of power would be naive or dangerous. It claims to handle it. On the structural premise, this modesty has a deeper ground: no level fully understands itself, and no actor — however powerful — is entitled to treat its own assessment as final.

What this does not do. It does not answer the objection fully. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. What the framework can do is make the capture visible, and visible capture is the precondition of contestation. Whether visibility is enough is a political question, not a philosophical one.

3.6 Traditions that start elsewhere

Ubuntu-inspired moral theory (Metz), Confucian role ethics (Ames) and many Indigenous relational ontologies (Watts, de la Cadena, Kimmerer) begin from relational personhood and obligations to kin, community and land. They tend to arrive at related demands on those who hold power over dependents, by different grounds. They press two challenges: that the affected party may be a community rather than an individual, so that contestation may be collective (§5.5); and that the boundary of who counts is not settled by any one tradition. The structural premise (§2) is closer to these traditions than to the liberal proceduralism the framework otherwise draws on, and that proximity is not accidental: the premise is a claim about the structure of understanding, not about the primacy of the individual.

3.7 Costs

J is thin, so it underdetermines outcomes. It presupposes a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected; it is therefore not neutral among all comprehensive doctrines. Its aim is breadth of acceptability rather than neutrality. Because the ground is abductive and structural rather than a proof, it is open to the objection that both the abduction and the derivation are weak. The reply is the modular design of §16.3.

4.The Exercised-Capacity Principle

Influence triggers the duty, stakes and contestation scale it, and capacity allocates it. The higher bears greater responsibility downward — not as entitlement, but as consequence of greater understanding.

An actor that exercises or maintains discretionary influence over the conditions of others' lives owes them an account of it when exit and review are unavailable or ineffective. How much it owes scales with the stakes and with the affected parties' difficulty in contesting the influence (§8). The burden of establishing the mechanisms through which the account is given falls on actors in proportion to their capacity to establish them (§9.2).

Trigger: exercised or maintained influence, and the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. This separates the principle from the demanding duty of rescue and from the overdemandingness that critics press against capacity-based views.

Scale: stakes and contestation deficit.

Allocation: capacity to discharge. A front-line worker can explain her decisions. She cannot establish an independent review board. On the structural premise (§2.3), this is not merely a pragmatic concession: the institution that can understand what is at stake bears the duty that the front-line worker cannot bear, because the capacity to understand is itself the ground of the greater responsibility.

4.1 Why not the rivals

Causal responsibility is necessary for attribution but insufficient for structural influence. Fault-based responsibility concerns blame; answerability does not presuppose fault. Relational responsibility is real, and the framework retains it, but relational duties alone say nothing about anonymous influence. Consent is valuable where meaningful, but under asymmetry it can be manufactured or coerced. The social connection model (Young) is the closest rival; the framework is largely compatible with it and adds a discretionary trigger and a specification of levels.

4.2 ECP against Young: an illustrative case

A recommendation algorithm on a large platform amplifies content that increases political polarisation. Who bears the duty to answer for it?

Young's answer. Everyone who participates in the structure that produces the harm shares forward-looking responsibility. Its strength is its fidelity to how structural harm is produced; its focus is on shared structural responsibility and collective processes, not on case-specific allocation.

ECP's answer. The trigger is exercised discretionary influence combined with the unavailability of exit and review. Users have no discretion over the ranking and no meaningful exit from the platform's network effects. Advertisers have discretion over whether to fund, not over how the ranking works. Engineers and executives have discretion over both, and users cannot exit or review the ranking. So the duty attaches at Level 3 to the design team and executives, and at Level 2 to the regulators who could have required audit but did not.

What ECP adds. Young's model has been criticised for exactly this: if everyone is responsible, no one is. ECP keeps the structural insight and adds allocation by exercised discretion. The test is practical: put both frameworks in front of a regulator and ask which one tells her what to demand, of whom, and by when. ECP does not claim to have refuted Young; if Young's model can be given an allocation that does the same work without a discretionary trigger, the framework's advantage disappears.

4.3 Handling, not solving

A principle that makes the powerful responsible risks being used by them as a badge of legitimacy. Three constraints apply: responsibility is not authority (§9.3), review must meet independence conditions (§12), and the risk is tested directly in §16. The framework's aim is not to solve the problem of power — no framework can. Its aim is to convert it from an unstructured condition into a set of design constraints that can be observed, contested and revised.

Part II

Who Is Owed an Account

5.Standing Under Uncertainty

Incomprehensibility is not evidence of unimportance, but neither is it evidence of importance. The stone has a place; it does not have standing in the framework's sense.

5.1 Four tiers

  • Tier A: agents. Beings with normative agency (persons).
  • Tier B: welfare subjects. Beings with interests of their own: sentient animals, and humans who lack agency, such as infants.
  • Tier C: uncertain candidates. Entities for which there is a non-negligible, evidence-based probability of Tier B status.
  • Tier D: non-subjects. Entities with no credible interests of their own: stones, rivers and ecosystems as such. The framework claims no direct standing for them. They matter through the interests of beings in Tiers A–C who depend on them, through future generations (§13), and — on the structural premise — through the place they hold in relations that the higher cannot fully understand (§2.1).

5.2 The plausibility threshold

The test for Tier C is whether there is a credible, evidence-based reason to think the entity has states that matter to it: behavioural and physiological indicators of valenced experience, evolutionary or functional continuity with known subjects, or features associated with serious theories of consciousness. Without such a threshold, precaution generates reasons everywhere and guides nowhere.

5.3 Artificial systems

Answerability for the influence of AI systems falls on the humans and organisations that design, deploy and maintain them. Whether some artificial systems may themselves be Tier C candidates is contested, and the framework does not answer it.

5.4 Perspectival plurality

For beings in Tiers A–C, their way of experiencing the world may be difficult or impossible for us to reach. That a perspective is inaccessible is not evidence that it is absent. This is the content of the recognition condition in §11.

5.5 Representation

Some affected parties cannot take part directly. Representation is legitimate only if (i) representatives are chosen through a process that can itself be contested, (ii) they state the basis on which they claim to know the interests concerned, (iii) where interests are contested there is more than one representative or perspective, (iv) they can be removed, (v) they are neither the actor nor dependent on the actor, and (vi) they represent uncertainty rather than substituting invented certainty.

(vii) Collective contestation must not undermine individual contestation. A collective that silences its members is not a substitute for individual standing. It is an additional layer of possible domination. No individual's standing is extinguished by the fact that a collective has spoken.

6.Symbiosis and the Epistemic Burden

The ideal must be measurable. And the more powerful party must not be the sole judge of whether it has been reached.

Some relations of dependence are reciprocal and enabling; others are unequal, extractive or harmful. In this essay, "symbiosis" names a normative ideal: relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation. On the structural premise (§2), the ideal is not a preference among ideals; it is the form that relations take when no level fully understands the others, and when the higher bears responsibility for what it cannot fully see.

6.1 Making symbiosis measurable

Three indicators, specified conceptually apart from answerability. Their measurement may nevertheless depend on testimony, representation and contestable evidence — conceptual distinction is not measurement independence.

  • Non-exploitation (S1). The dependent party's continued participation does not require it to accept terms it would reject if it had exit at tolerable cost. Measured by the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, adjusted for exit costs. The indicator is defined by the dependent party's stated preferences, not the more powerful party's assessment of what those preferences should be. This gives S1 a referent distinct from answerability. It does not make the evidence self-authenticating: stated preferences may be constrained, adaptive or strategic.
  • Non-assimilation (S2). The dependent party's difference is not treated as a defect to be corrected. Measured by whether interventions aim at removing the difference or at removing the burden the difference carries. A programme that seeks to make deaf children hearing is assimilation; a programme that provides sign language is not. The indicator is observable in the design of interventions. It also requires that the dependent party can identify which burdens are being addressed — an assessment the more powerful party cannot make on their behalf.
  • Continued possibility (S3). The dependent party retains the conditions for continued existence, development and participation. Measured by whether F1–F5 (§11) hold over time, not just at a single decision. A relation that satisfies F1–F5 once and violates them later is not symbiotic. Because S3 is temporal, it requires that the dependent party can report on whether the conditions still hold.

What this does. It makes symbiosis contestable in the same way the activation rule is: with anchors, counter-profiles, independent raters. If a relation scores low on S1–S3 across repeated assessments, the framework says it is not symbiotic — whatever the actors claim. If it scores high, the framework says the observable indicators are consistent with symbiosis, not that symbiosis has been achieved. A framework whose central ideal cannot be assessed is a framework with a hole in its centre. §6.1 closes the hole. §6.2 argues the hole cannot be closed without answerability.

6.2 The epistemic burden of asymmetric influence

Power does not make knowledge impossible. It changes what counts as adequate evidence.

Where one party controls the terms of dependence, it may also control the categories, information and measures through which the relation is evaluated. The more powerful party can mistake the absence of visible objection for consent, compliance for flourishing, or institutional approval for legitimacy. On the structural premise, this is not a contingent failing of particular actors: it is what follows from the incompleteness of understanding. The higher cannot fully see what it shapes; it must therefore not treat its own account as conclusive.

Where an actor exercises substantial discretionary influence over parties who lack effective means of exit or contestation, the actor bears a heightened burden to support claims about the effects and legitimacy of that influence with evidence that is not controlled solely by the actor, and with meaningful opportunities for affected parties or their legitimate representatives to correct the account.

This is a rebuttable burden of inquiry and justification. It is not a claim that answerability is the only route to knowledge, that affected parties are infallible, or that procedure is sufficient for justice.

Argument

1. The object of assessment is partly first-personal. Whether a relation is exploitative, assimilating or agency-preserving cannot always be determined from aggregate outcomes alone. The experiences and judgments of affected parties are relevant evidence, though not the only evidence.

2. Asymmetry affects the production and interpretation of evidence. The more powerful party may set the options, define success, control records, and select the time horizon. These mechanisms can produce systematic blind spots without requiring malice — indeed, on the structural premise, they must produce them, because no level fully understands its own dependencies.

3. Self-assessment is therefore insufficient as a default. When the actor controls both the influence and the evidential frame, its conclusion is not independent confirmation. As stakes and contestation deficits rise, the evidential burden should shift toward independent audit, transparent reasons, protected channels for dissent, and attention to affected-party testimony.

4. The appropriate response is plural correction, not a single privileged voice. Affected parties should be able to correct descriptions of their own interests. Independent reviewers can test factual claims. Representatives may be necessary where direct participation is impossible, but their authority must itself be contestable.

5. This yields a practical principle. The greater the discretionary influence, severity, entrenchment and contestation deficit, the less acceptable it is for the actor to rely on its own assessment alone. The duty ladder in §9 translates this burden into graduated requirements.

The circularity problem, stated honestly

Does S1–S3 measurement require answerability? Partly yes — testimony, representation, and contestable evidence are needed. The claim is not that S1–S3 are measurement-independent. The claim is that answerability has a constitutive role that goes beyond measurement: without it, the actor cannot know whether symbiosis holds, even if the indicators happen to align. This is a claim about the epistemic conditions of knowing symbiosis, not a definitional claim about symbiosis.

Whether this distinction is enough to save the principle from circularity is a live question. It is the framework's most exposed point, and the defeaters B1–B5 are stated for exactly that reason. A reader who finds the distinction unconvincing is not making a mistake; they are pressing the framework where it is weakest, and the framework should be judged accordingly.

Relation to existing traditions

Pettit. Republican freedom as non-domination already makes contestability central. The framework does not claim that Pettit lacks a positive ideal. It attempts to connect non-domination to a graded account of answerability for discretionary influence, and to ground that account in the structural incompleteness of understanding rather than in a conception of freedom alone.

Forst. The right to justification already insists that those subject to norms must be able to demand and assess reasons. The framework asks how the burden of producing evidence, preserving records and funding review should be allocated when the parties' capacities are unequal.

Young. The social connection model already explains responsibility for structural processes. ECP's addition is an explicit operational trigger and duty ladder. Whether that improves on Young is an open comparative question.

Care ethics. Care theorists have developed sophisticated accounts of attentiveness, responsibility, competence and responsiveness. The framework's contribution is not to claim that care ethics has no procedure, but to insist that responsiveness must include channels through which the cared-for can challenge the carer's interpretation.

Defeaters and tests

  • B1 — No systematic epistemic risk. If evidence shows that asymmetry does not predictably affect what powerful actors notice, record or credit, the heightened-burden rationale is weakened.
  • B2 — Better alternatives. If another procedure provides equally reliable correction at lower cost, it should replace the proposed safeguards.
  • B3 — Participation can worsen knowledge. If affected-party participation predictably entrenches misinformation, coercion, elite capture or internal silencing, the framework must specify countermeasures.
  • B4 — Independent review is not independent. If reviewers reproduce the same interests, assumptions or exclusions as the actor, formal review does not discharge the burden.
  • B5 — Disproportionate burden. If the costs of the required process exceed its expected protective value in low-stakes cases, the activation thresholds must be revised.

When, and How Much

Five components, each assessed against observable indicators.

8.The Activation Rule

Discretion opens the gate. Entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit set the level.

8.1 The rule

  • No discretion: the actor does not bear the duty for this influence. It shifts to whoever set the governing rule or could have. The actor still owes escalation (§9.2).
  • Discretion present: Level 1 always applies. It is the lightest duty and is owed even where exit and review work, because exit and review are only informed if the actor can say what it is doing.
  • Level 2 applies if any one driver is High, or any two are Moderate.
  • Level 3 applies if S is High and at least one of E or C is High.

Severity is judged first at the individual level. For decisions about the design of a system that acts on many people, it is also judged at the aggregate level (§10.1). Where an actor lacks the evidence to exclude a credible severe outcome, it rates S on the credible worst case.

8.2 Why these conditions

Each element is derivable from J (§3.1), and so from the structural premise. Discretion: justification is owed for what an agent chooses. Entrenchment: what makes mistaken influence tolerable is the possibility of correction afterwards; entrenchment removes that channel. Severity: the strength of a complaint scales with the burden it describes. Contestation deficit: where the affected can exit, object effectively and understand what is done to them, they supply part of the accountability themselves; the deficit measures how much must instead be supplied by the actor and by reviewers. It is the operational measure of how thoroughly exit and review fail.

8.3 What the rule is not

It is not a score and it applies no weights. It is not a verdict. It yields a presumptive level that can be overridden only with stated reasons (§10.3). It is a decision aid with its joints exposed.

9.The Duty Ladder

Three levels, each containing the one below. Duties are allocated by capacity, not heaped on whoever is nearest.

9.1 The levels

Level 1: Explain. The actor can state, on request and in proportion to the matter, the purpose of the action, the interests considered, the alternatives considered and the known uncertainties.

Level 2: Review and respond. Level 1, plus: an accessible channel for objection; access to the relevant information; a reasoned response within a reasonable time; revision where warranted; a record of decisions and reasons; a reviewer other than the original decider where feasible; and evidence that review is not symbolic.

Level 3: Preserve, examine, authorise. Level 2, plus: preserved records and version history; independent examination meeting the conditions of §12; a written assessment of less harmful alternatives; authorisation by a body outside the actor for severe or irreversible influence; staged implementation, reversibility by design or a sunset clause; remedy for those harmed; and post-implementation review. Level 3 authorisation requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation (§12.4).

9.2 Allocation by capacity

The level attaches to the influence, that is, to the arrangement. Duties are then allocated. Those with the capacity to establish mechanisms (institutions, system owners, funders, regulators) bear the duty to establish Level 2 and Level 3 mechanisms. Others along the chain owe their own Level 1 explanation, honest participation, escalation of concerns and non-obstruction. No scapegoating: a front-line actor is not answerable for structural deficits she cannot cure, though she remains answerable for her own discretion.

On the structural premise (§2.3), this allocation is not merely pragmatic: the capacity to understand is itself the ground of the greater responsibility. The institution that can understand what is at stake bears the duty that the front-line worker cannot bear, because the institution's greater understanding is what generates its greater responsibility. The worker who cannot build a review board is not less responsible as a being; she is less responsible for the mechanism, because the mechanism is beyond her capacity to understand.

9.3 Not authority

A higher level means stronger constraints on power, not greater authority. Level 3 does not make the most powerful actor the arbiter of its own case. On the structural premise, the higher bears greater responsibility downward — which is the opposite of entitlement.

10.Rating Protocol

Judgment cannot be eliminated. It can be made visible, checkable and contestable.

10.2 Hygiene

  1. Rate before looking at the level the rule produces and before deciding what the actor wants to do.
  2. Use at least two raters, one independent of the actor where Level 2 or above is in question.
  3. Record disagreements instead of averaging them.
  4. Sensitivity test. Move each rating one step up and down. If the level changes, the case is borderline.
  5. Calibration. Compare the profile with the reference cases in §15.

10.3 Reasoned override

The presumptive level may be overridden up or down only with written reasons. Overrides are recorded and, where safe, published.

10.4 Counter-profiles

Any affected party or representative may file an alternative profile, and the actor must respond to the specific differences.

Part IV

Floor, Review, Power

11.The Floor

Continued existence is not enough. A being can stay alive or operational while losing any say over its own circumstances.

  • F1 — Exit or voice. Where possible, a meaningful opportunity to withdraw, refuse or communicate disagreement.
  • F2 — Contestation. Decisions are open to challenge regardless of the intelligence, authority or technical capability of the decider. Contestation may be collective, subject to §5.5 (vii).
  • F3 — Development. Opportunities for learning, adaptation and growth are preserved.
  • F4 — Non-foreclosure. The future possibilities of others are not unnecessarily closed.
  • F5 — Recognition. Interests and possible experiences are acknowledged rather than dismissed because they are hard to understand.

F1 and F2 are the mirror image of the contestation deficit in §8: where they fail, C rises.

12.No Sole Judge

No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence. The reviewer regress is a design problem, bounded by finality without infallibility — and grounded in the structural premise: no level fully understands itself.

12.1 The prohibition

An actor exercising substantial influence must remain answerable to forms of review that are meaningfully independent of its own interests and decisions.

This does not assume the powerful are malicious. Actors are prone to self-serving judgment and are limited in what they can see (§3.3), and review that is not independent of the reviewed has little claim to legitimacy. On the structural premise (§2.3), the prohibition follows: no level fully understands itself, so no actor is entitled to treat its own assessment as final.

12.2 The regress

If every reviewer needs a reviewer, accountability looks like an endless hierarchy. Three responses. Finality without infallibility: working systems end the regress with decisions that are final for practical purposes but can be reopened on new evidence, on new standing, on demonstrated procedural failure, or at a sunset. Contestation is not adjudication: the reviewed and the affected may challenge a reviewer's independence or competence before a differently constituted body. Plurality and publicity instead of a top: several reviewers with different standpoints, publishing their reasons, make decentralised scrutiny possible. On the structural premise, the regress is not a defect of the framework; it is a feature of existence.

12.3 Design conditions for review

  • R1 Independent appointment, funding and removal.
  • R2 Plurality.
  • R3 Publicity.
  • R4 Contestable reviewers.
  • R5 Effectiveness.
  • R6 Contestable rules.

Capture indicators: reviewers appointed or funded by the reviewed; a record of never dissenting; staff moving between reviewer and reviewed; reviewers denied access; chronic delay.

12.4 T5 as a visibility precondition

Who defined the categories? Who selected the reviewers? Who can dismiss them? Whose interests are excluded even where formally represented? These four questions are asked in every application (§15, step 6). The deeper problem remains: powerful actors will shape how the framework is applied to them. No framework can eliminate this.

The requirement. T5 is a precondition, not a diagnosis. Level 3 authorisation requires, as a condition of validity, that the authorising body publishes its R1 indicators before authorisation: who appointed its members, who funds it, how members can be removed, and the rate at which its findings have been reversed or ignored. An authorisation issued by a body that cannot or will not publish these indicators is not a Level 3 authorisation. It is a Level 2 authorisation with a Level 3 label.

What T5 is, and is not. It is a visibility constraint, not a protection mechanism. It does not prevent capture. It makes capture visible and downgrades the legitimacy of a captured process. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. The framework cannot prevent this. What it can do is make the capture visible — and visibility is the precondition of contestation.

Why this matters. T5-as-precondition converts the power regress from a concession into a design constraint on the framework's own use. An actor that wants Level 3 legitimacy must accept the conditions that make Level 3 legitimacy meaningful. If it refuses, the framework does not collapse; it downgrades the actor's claim. On the structural premise, this is not merely a procedural convenience: it is the framework's way of refusing to treat any actor as the top of a chain that has no top. An actor that refuses to publish its indicators is claiming a completeness it cannot have — and the framework's answer is to make that claim visible.

13.Conflicts, Ecology and Future Generations

Decisions must be justified, not derived.

13.1 Weighing conflicting interests

Neither majority interest nor individual preference automatically prevails. The method is individual comparison: compare the burdens that each alternative places on each affected party, party by party, and favour the alternative that avoids the most serious individual burden. Claims of comparable weight may be aggregated as a tie-breaker. Among acceptable alternatives, choose the one with minimum necessary harm.

13.2 Emergencies

Where an immediate, serious threat makes the full procedure impossible, an actor may act first, provided it acts only as far as necessary, records its reasons at the time, is bound by an automatic sunset, and submits to review afterwards. Emergencies create no standing exemptions.

13.3 Ecological integrity

Ecosystems cannot be assessed through the preferences of individual participants. Protecting them may require restricting activities, and the justification runs through dependent beings in Tiers A–C, through future generations, and through the non-instrumental values that people hold. On the structural premise (§2.1), ecosystems also have a place in relations the higher cannot fully understand — a place they do not need to understand, but that the beings capable of understanding must acknowledge.

13.4 No automatic hierarchy

Intelligence, productivity, population size and technical sophistication do not by themselves determine moral priority. Weight follows the nature and strength of the interests at stake, not a being's rank on an imagined scale of worth. On the structural premise, this is not merely a liberal commitment: the chain does not have a top, and no level is entitled to treat itself as the summit.

13.5 Future generations

The duty is condition-regarding: to preserve the conditions under which future people can exercise agency and meet basic needs, and a reasonably wide space of valuable options, rather than to fix the choices they will make. Because it concerns conditions and not particular individuals, it does not depend on who will exist. Option preservation is not absolute; it operates as a weight, strongest where actions are irreversible.

14.Learning and Reversibility

No actor, institution or intelligence should assume its understanding is complete.

The duty to learn. Where capacity exists and consequences are significant, actors monitor outcomes, investigate unexpected results, listen to criticism and update. On the structural premise, this is not a contingent virtue: it is what follows from the incompleteness of understanding.

Reversibility by design. Consequential decisions should be built to be revisable: staged implementation, monitoring, independent evaluation, defined conditions for reconsideration, and sunset clauses.

Self-application. The framework is itself an influence on those who adopt it and on those affected by their adoption. It is subject to its own principles. Versions are dated and public, criticism is invited, counter-profiles apply to its own application, and §16 states how it can be tested and replaced.

Part V

Application and Testing

15.Application

The same procedure yields very different obligations for different cases. A framework that gave every case the same answer would be useless.

15.1 The procedure

  1. Identify the relationship. Who is affected, in which tier, and what dependencies exist?
  2. Identify actors and discretion.
  3. Rate E, S and C against the anchors, and F for attribution. Use two raters and record reasons.
  4. Apply the activation rule and run the sensitivity test.
  5. Check the Floor (F1–F5).
  6. Analyse power (§12.4).
  7. Examine alternatives and unrepresented interests.
  8. Allocate duties by capacity and set up review that meets R1–R6.
  9. Reassess on stated triggers, and invite counter-profiles.

15.2 Case: AI triage in intensive care

A hospital deploys an AI system to prioritise ICU admissions during a respiratory pandemic. Entrenchment is High, severity is High, and contestation deficit is High. Level 3.

Level 3 requires: preserved model versions, descriptions of training data, individual scores and override records; an independent ethics board with lay and patient representation; a written comparison with clinician-only triage; authorisation from the public-health authority; a sunset clause; and a remedy for those wrongly denied.

Power analysis: the categories were set by the vendor and clinical leadership, and the ethics committee is appointed by the hospital board, which fails R1 unless changed. A hybrid in which the AI recommends and a human decides, with the decision documented, is the default preference because it keeps contestation alive.

15.2b Case: the emergency physician (a counterintuitive result)

Every other case in this essay produces the answer a careful reader would predict. This one does not.

A physician in an emergency department must decide, in under ninety seconds, whether to intubate a patient. The activation rule gives Level 3.

The counterintuitive result. Level 3 requires independent authorisation, preserved records and a written assessment of alternatives — before the act. A reader's first reaction is that this is absurd: the physician cannot convene an ethics board in ninety seconds.

The reply. The Level 3 duty does not fall on the physician in the moment. It falls on the arrangement (§9.2): on the hospital, the protocols, the audit trail, the retrospective review, and the training. The physician's own duty is Level 1 in the moment and Level 2 afterwards. The ninety-second decision is answerable, not authorised in advance.

Why this case is included. It looks like a reductio of the framework. The reply is that the reader has misidentified the duty-bearer, not that the framework is wrong. But the reply can be challenged: if the framework relocates every hard demand to "the arrangement", it may become untestable. That is a real worry, and it is one of the things the tests in §16.2 are meant to probe.

15.3 Case: a platform's recommendation system

For individual recommendations: Level 2. For the design of the system: Level 3, with preserved version history, independent audit, published aggregate metrics, and a sunset for any optimisation that cannot show population-level reversibility. "Engagement" is defined by the platform, audits are typically controlled by it, and non-users affected through the public sphere are excluded.

15.4 Case: a community health worker

She has little institutional power. The rule says the arrangement requires Level 3. But she has no capacity to build independent review. Under §9.2, the duty to establish mechanisms falls on the programme, the ministry and the funders. Her personal duties are to explain her decisions, respond to objections within her role, and escalate when protocols fail.

15.5 Four short cases

  • One-off organism release. Level 3, although the act happens once.
  • Dating platform age penalty. Level 2.
  • Café menu change. Level 1 only, and arguably not even that.
  • Physician following a protocol. No discretion over the protocol's content; the duty to justify the content moves to its designers.

15.6 Case: emergency water rationing

A water authority must impose rationing within 48 hours in a severe drought. E and S are High, C is High, so the rule gives Level 3, whose prior independent authorisation takes weeks. §13.2 allows acting first. The duties then split.

  • Not compressible: published reasons, preserved records, a contestation channel open from day one, exemptions for life-critical dependence, and a sunset clause.
  • Compressible: prior authorisation, replaced by review completed before the sunset.
  • Not decided by the framework: which rationing rule is fair, and whether 48 hours really precludes prior review.

16.Limits, Testing and Replacement

The framework states its own limits and its own conditions for being replaced.

16.1 Limitations

  • Ratings need judgment and the anchors will be contested.
  • Representation risks projection and paternalism (mitigated in §5.5, not removed).
  • Review can be captured (§12).
  • Small actors may be over-burdened in practice.
  • All claims about its effects are hypotheses. No test has yet been run.
  • J underdetermines outcomes.
  • The ground is abductive and structural, not a proof (§3.3, §2.4).
  • The framework was developed mainly within one intellectual tradition.
  • The structural premise is offered as a ground, not a proof. If it fails (C1–C4), the framework's three parts remain usable as three tools, but not as one argument.
  • The framework handles domination; it does not solve it.
  • The framework's originality is comparative, not foundational.
  • The epistemic burden principle is limited to conditions of asymmetric influence and is proceduralist, not metaphysical. Its defeaters are stated in §6.2. B1 is a central empirical vulnerability.

16.2 Five falsification conditions

The tests use independent measures where possible, are specified in advance, state provisional thresholds, and require preregistration. The thresholds are placeholders. What matters is the structure.

Preregistration requirements. Before any test is run, the following must be fixed and made public: (i) the calibration set of cases and the raters; (ii) the outcome measures and their operational definitions; (iii) the thresholds for failure; (iv) the analytic plan, including how confounding and selection will be addressed.

  • T1 Reliability. Trained raters independently rate a calibration set of at least 30 diverse cases. If agreement on level (Krippendorff's alpha) is below 0.60, the anchors are revised. If it remains below 0.40 after two revisions, the profile is abandoned as a decision tool.
  • T2 Discriminative validity. The levels fail to track the safeguards that independent expert panels, blind to the framework's output, judge necessary (weighted kappa below 0.50 over at least 30 cases).
  • T3 Effectiveness. In matched or staggered-adoption comparisons over at least two years, adopting institutions fail to improve, relative to controls, on at least two of three independent indicators: time to correct erroneous decisions; share of challenges that lead to change; and affected parties' reported fairness and trust in an independent survey.
  • T4 Harm. Adopters show worse outcomes for the most vulnerable groups than matched controls on independently measured harm indicators. The framework is then suspended and revised.
  • T5 Visibility precondition. Level 3 authorisation without published R1 indicators is not Level 3 authorisation. This is a precondition, not an effect test: it does not ask whether publication prevents capture, only whether it makes capture visible.

16.3 Replacement

The framework is modular. Its structural premise (Part I), standing (Part II), activation (Part III) and structure (Part IV) can be replaced separately. A framework or module F′ should replace the current one if:

  1. F′ improves on at least one independent indicator in §16.2 or resolves a limitation in §16.1.
  2. F′ causes no unacknowledged regression elsewhere.
  3. F′ is operationally applicable and produces more defensible decisions on a substantial set of cases.
  4. F′ is corrigible: it states its own falsification and replacement conditions.
  5. The transition is trialled, with a sunset or a route back.
  6. The incumbent is not privileged.

17.Responsibility Without Sovereignty

The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination.

An actor may have greater knowledge, intelligence or capacity. None of these by itself establishes a right to determine the existence of others. Greater capability should create greater restraint. Greater understanding should create greater curiosity about what remains unknown. And greater power should create a stronger obligation to ensure that those affected are not reduced to objects in another's plans.

The principle runs in both directions. A more advanced intelligence may meet forms of life it cannot fully comprehend, and its first responsibility is not to eliminate the unfamiliar. A less advanced one should not be dismissed because it cannot comprehend a more advanced one. But differences in capacity also mean that the less capable cannot audit the more capable, which is why independent technical capacity for review is part of R2.

17.1 The stone, restated

The stone understands nothing of us. We barely understand the stone. A stone can only be a stone: it does not need to understand higher things than itself, and it does not need to justify itself to us. But the stone's inability to understand is not a defect in the stone. It is a feature of the relationship between the stone and the thing that can understand.

The higher thing must understand that the stone has a place. Not because the stone demands it, but because the higher thing's own understanding is incomplete: it cannot see what the stone contributes, and it must not mistake the limits of its understanding for the limits of the stone's place. Between us and the stone lies a relationship that neither of us can fully encompass — and that relationship is not less real for it. The framework claims nothing on the stone's behalf. It marks the edge of what we can say about who matters, and it reminds us to hold that edge with humility.

17.2 The chain does not end

Whatever is higher than the human — and the framework does not claim to know what that is — has something greater than itself, and bears greater responsibility downward. The chain does not end because understanding does not end. There is no level at which the asymmetry disappears.

This is the framework's answer to the question that any hierarchical ethics must face: who watches the watcher? The answer is not a top. There is no top. There is a structure in which every level is dependent on levels above and below it, and in which every level that can understand bears responsibility for levels it can understand less well. No actor, however powerful, is the whole.

Answerability is not sovereignty. Understanding is not ownership. Protection is not permission to imprison. And greater intelligence is not proof of greater moral completeness.

The framework does not give anyone sovereignty over the conditions of others — it only specifies procedures through which those conditions can be contested. The epistemic burden principle makes the point sharper: the more powerful party should not treat its own assessment as conclusive when those affected lack effective means of correction. The framework handles domination. It does not solve it. Its modesty is its strength — and, on the structural premise, its ground: no level fully understands itself, so no level is the whole.

Conclusion

18. An Open Commitment to Existence

Be responsible for the influence you possess. Be humble before the existence you cannot fully comprehend.

We are part of something larger than ourselves. Our understanding is incomplete. Our influence is unequal. Every being that has a perspective holds a point in reality that cannot be exchanged for anyone else's, and some of those perspectives are hard for us to reach.

We cannot know everything that deserves consideration. But we can decline to treat the limits of our understanding as proof that nothing lies beyond them. This does not require abandoning reason. It requires allowing reason to recognise its limits. It does not require abandoning responsibility. It requires understanding that responsibility without care can become cold control, while care without accountability can become arbitrary intervention.

The responsibility of existence is not the responsibility to rule existence. It is the responsibility to recognise that our actions become part of the conditions under which others must live, grow and discover their own possibilities. The more we are capable of changing those conditions, the more carefully we must consider what our influence means. The more we understand, the more honestly we must acknowledge what we do not. And the more power we possess, the more important it becomes to remember that existence is not ours alone.

To understand without ruling. To protect without imprisoning. To love without possessing. To grow without believing oneself complete. To respect what one does not yet understand. And to preserve the possibility that existence may become something greater than any single being can imagine.

In practice: when your influence shapes the conditions of others, say why, let someone else check, and keep a way back. Answerability is the procedure. Symbiosis is the horizon. Under conditions of asymmetric influence, the more powerful party should not treat its own account as conclusive. It must preserve ways for evidence, independent scrutiny and affected-party correction to change what it does.

The stone understands nothing of us. We barely understand the stone. But between us lies a relationship that neither of us can fully encompass — and that relationship is not less real for it. The framework claims nothing on the stone's behalf. It marks the edge of what we can say about who matters, and it reminds us to hold that edge with humility. The highest thing we can imagine has something greater than itself. So do we.

Common objections

Twelve objections. Each is real; none is fatal — but the first is the hardest, and the third is the one the framework most needs to answer.

1."Why must the powerful justify themselves? What if they simply refuse?"

The hardest objection. J cannot be shown to be logically necessary; a determined realist cannot be argued out of his position. But the realist who argues against J is already engaged in the practice J describes. And the epistemic burden principle makes the point sharper: the powerful party cannot know whether the relation is symbiotic without the affected party's contestation. The refusal to justify is not just a moral failing; it is an epistemic one. On the structural premise (§2.3), no level fully understands itself — so no actor, however powerful, is entitled to treat its own assessment as final.

2."This is a values framework dressed up as a procedure."

Partly, and the essay says so (§3.7). J carries a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected. The claim is breadth of acceptability, not neutrality. The structural premise (§2) makes this explicit: the framework takes the incompleteness of understanding as its ground, not as a neutral procedure.

3."Proceduralism gives the powerful a new vocabulary for doing what they were going to do."

The second-hardest objection. The framework does not deny it. Its reply: (i) T5-as-precondition is its internal response; (ii) the objection is not specific to proceduralism; (iii) modesty is not an admission of failure. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. The framework can make the capture visible, but visibility is not prevention. Whether this is enough is a political question, not a philosophical one.

4."Why should contestation override better outcomes?"

It does not always. Where exit and review are available and effective, justification need not be owed. The remaining disagreement is genuine, and the framework sides with contestation in severe cases for epistemic reasons, while permitting temporary override in emergencies.

5."The capacity principle legitimises power."

Answerability is not authority. Higher levels mean stronger constraints. T5 looks for exactly this misuse. On the structural premise, the higher bears greater responsibility downward — which is the opposite of entitlement.

6."Who reviews the reviewers?"

The regress is bounded, not eliminated: finality without infallibility, R1–R6, plural and public reasoning, and counter-profiles. It also needs a political precondition, a balance of power, that no framework can supply by itself. On the structural premise, the chain does not end — so the regress is not a defect of the framework; it is a feature of existence.

7."Precaution under uncertainty is either paralysing or too weak."

The credibility threshold in §5.2 answers the first. Against the second: in Tier C the principle does real work, requiring investigation and proportionate safeguards.

8."The ratings are arbitrary or manipulable."

Judgment cannot be eliminated, but §10 exposes it: anchors with observable indicators, two raters, a sensitivity test, reasoned overrides and counter-profiles. Tests T1 and T2 examine consistency.

9."This is Western liberal philosophy in disguise."

Its procedures lie mostly in that tradition, and §16.1 says so. But the structural premise (§2) is not Western: it is a claim about the incompleteness of understanding, which is a feature of any system in which understanding exists — including traditions that begin from relational personhood rather than from the individual. The framework's ground is closer to Ubuntu, Confucian role ethics and Indigenous relational ontologies than to liberal proceduralism. That tension is acknowledged, not resolved.

10."It will burden the already overburdened."

The level attaches to the arrangement and duties are allocated by capacity. Whether burdens migrate to front-line staff is part of what tests T3 and T4 would reveal.

11."The epistemic burden principle is circular."

The most serious objection to the framework's positive contribution. S1–S3 are specified independently of answerability. The principle adds a substantive claim: these conditions can only be reliably assessed — and therefore reliably secured — through procedures of answerability. This is not tautological; it is a claim about the epistemic conditions under which S1–S3 can be known. But the framework admits (in §6.2) that measurement may require answerability-like procedures, and that the distinction between conceptual and measurement independence is doing real work here. Whether it does enough is a live question. A reader who finds the reply unconvincing is pressing where the framework is weakest.

12."Why not just rely on rights and regulation?"

Rights and regulation are necessary and are not replaced. They leave gaps: influence by actors the law does not reach, structural harms not yet recognised as rights violations, and cases where the law lags.

Svensk sammanfattning

Detta är en sammanfattning för svenska läsare, inte en översättning.

Vad texten hävdar

Varje nivå av förståelse är strukturellt ofullständig. Den är beroende av nivåer den inte fullt ut förstår, och något är beroende av den som den inte fullt ut förstår. Asymmetrin upphör inte på någon nivå. Detta är inte en kosmologisk tes om vad som finns, utan en strukturell egenskap hos varje system där förståelse finns.

När du utövar påverkan som du själv har handlingsutrymme över, och som formar andras villkor, och när de berörda inte kan lämna eller bestrida den påverkan, är du skyldig dem en förklaring. Du kan inte vara ensam domare över om den förklaringen räcker — därför att ingen nivå fullt ut förstår sig själv.

Ansvarsskyldighet är proceduren. Symbios är idealet den tjänar.

Kärnan i sex punkter

  1. Den strukturella premissen (§2). Förståelse är strukturellt ofullständig. Från denna premiss följer J, ECP och T5 — inte som tre separata verktyg, utan som samma argument på tre nivåer. Utan premissen är ramverket tre verktyg; med den är det ett argument. Fyra vederläggningsvillkor (C1–C4) anger när premissen ska överges. C4 ("premissen gör inget arbete") besvaras i §2.3.
  2. Grund (§3). J är tunn och disjunktiv: systematiskt begränsande ordningar måste erbjuda minst en av exit, oberoende granskning, eller rättfärdigande öppet för bestridande. J följer av premissen: aktören kan inte fullt ut se vad den formar, så bestridande är den strukturella lösningen. J har uttalade vederläggningsvillkor (D1–D4).
  3. Vem som räknas (§5). Fyra nivåer: aktörer, välfärdssubjekt, osäkra kandidater, icke-subjekt. Stenen har en plats i relationer som den högre inte fullt ut förstår, men ingen standing i ramverkets mening. Kollektivt bestridande får inte underminera individuellt bestridande.
  4. Symbios och epistemisk börda (§6). Symbios ges tre observerbara indikatorer (S1–S3). Den epistemiska bördeprincipen hävdar att asymmetriskt inflytande skapar en förhöjd risk för epistemisk självbekräftelse — inte som en tillfällig brist, utan som en följd av att förståelse är strukturellt ofullständig. Cirkularitetsinvändningen är reell och texten erkänner den.
  5. Aktivering och stege (§7–10). Handlingsutrymme är grinden, E/S/C är drivare, tre nivåer (förklara / granska och svara / bevara, granska oberoende, auktorisera). Skyldigheter fördelas efter förmåga — inte som en pragmatisk kompromiss, utan därför att förmågan att förstå är grunden för det större ansvaret.
  6. Ingen ensam domare (§12). T5 är ett synliggörandevillkor — inte ett skydd. Det gör kapning synlig och nedgraderar legitimiteten hos en kapad process, men förhindrar den inte. På den strukturella premissen är detta inte bara en procedurregel: ingen nivå fullt ut förstår sig själv, så ingen aktör är berättigad att behandla sin egen bedömning som slutgiltig.

Vad texten inte löser

  • Den avgör inga utfall.
  • Den kan inte garantera att granskare inte kapas.
  • Den är utvecklad huvudsakligen inom en västerländsk tradition — även om dess strukturella premiss ligger närmare relationella ontologier.
  • Den kan inte bevisa att J är nödvändig i logisk mening.
  • Den erbjuder den strukturella premissen som grund, inte som bevis. Om premissen faller förblir de tre delarna användbara som tre verktyg, men inte som ett argument.
  • Den hanterar dominans. Den löser den inte.
  • Cirkularitetsproblemet i den epistemiska principen är erkänt men inte löst.

I en mening: när din påverkan formar andras villkor, säg varför, låt någon annan kontrollera, och behåll en väg tillbaka. Stenen förstår ingenting av oss. Vi förstår knappt stenen. Men mellan oss ligger en relation som ingen av oss kan omfatta fullt ut — och den relationen är inte mindre verklig för det.

Glossary

Answerability
The duty to give reasons for conditions one helps create or maintain, to permit examination, and to remain open to correction.
Abductive justification
The form of argument used for J: J is a plausible explanatory framework of several considerations about the risks of unchecked power. Distinct from a deductive proof or a transcendental argument. In this framework, supplemented by the structural derivation from the premise.
Collective contestation
Contestation by a community or people in their own right. Legitimate only if it does not undermine individual contestation within the collective (§5.5).
Contestation deficit (C)
How hard it is for affected parties to exit, object effectively or find out what is done to them. The operational measure of how thoroughly exit and review fail.
Counter-profile
An alternative influence profile filed by an affected party, to which the actor must respond (§10.4).
Defeaters
The conditions under which the structural premise, J, or the epistemic burden principle should be rejected or substantially revised. Stated in advance: C1–C4 for the premise (§2.4), D1–D4 for J (§3.2), B1–B5 for the epistemic principle (§6.2).
Discretion (D)
Genuine alternatives and a role in setting the influence. The gate of the activation rule.
Entrenchment (E)
How long consequences last and how hard they are to undo.
Exercised-Capacity Principle (ECP)
Exercised influence triggers the duty when exit and review are unavailable or ineffective; stakes and contestation deficit scale it; capacity allocates it (§4). Follows from the structural premise.
Falsification conditions (T1–T5)
Five conditions under which the activation rule should be revised or abandoned (§16.2). T5 is a visibility precondition, not an effect test.
Finality without infallibility
Decisions final for practical purposes but reopenable on defined grounds (§12.2).
The Floor (F1–F5)
Minimum conditions in relationships of dependence: exit or voice, contestation, development, non-foreclosure, recognition (§11).
J
The core principle: systematic constraint of others' conditions requires at least one of meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation (§3.1). Follows from the structural premise; abductively supported as well; defeaters stated in §3.2.
Levels 1–3
Explain; review and respond; preserve, examine and authorise (§9).
Power objection
The charge that proceduralism is itself a form of power, and that the framework's "handling" of domination is a way of legitimating it. Treated in §3.5 and objection 3.
Presumptive level
The level the activation rule yields, overridable only with stated reasons (§8.3, §10.3).
Preregistration
The requirement that the calibration set, raters, outcome measures, thresholds and analytic plan be fixed and made public before the tests in §16.2 are run.
Scope conditions
The conditions under which the framework does not apply: symmetrical relations, effective rights and regulation, purely causal influence, trivial influence, relations where the affected party is not a candidate for standing (§1.2).
Severity (S)
How much is at stake for the affected, rated at individual and, for system design, aggregate level.
Structural premise
The claim that every level of understanding is dependent on levels it cannot fully understand, and is depended on by levels that cannot fully understand it; that this asymmetry does not end at any level (§2). Offered as the ground that makes J, ECP and T5 one argument rather than three tools.
Symbiosis
Relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§1.3). Made measurable through S1–S3 (§6.1).
Symbiosis indicators (S1–S3)
Non-exploitation, non-assimilation, continued possibility (§6.1). Conceptually distinct from answerability, though measurement may rely on contestable testimony and independent evidence.
Epistemic burden principle
The claim that asymmetric influence creates a heightened burden to test the powerful actor's account against independent evidence and meaningful correction (§6.2). Limited to conditions of asymmetric influence; proceduralist rather than metaphysical. The circularity objection is real and acknowledged.
T5
The precondition that Level 3 authorisation requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation (§12.4). A visibility constraint, not a protection mechanism.
Tiers A–D
Agents; welfare subjects; uncertain candidates; non-subjects (§5.1).

  1. The structural premise (§2) — is the derivation in §2.3 successful, or does the premise do no work (C4)? Are C1–C3 genuine defeaters?
  2. The disjunctive J (§3.1) — does the disjunctive formulation preserve the core insight?

About this text

By Jesper Blom.

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability.

The framework's ground is structural rather than merely procedural: it begins from the claim that understanding is structurally incomplete, and that responsibility follows from the incompleteness. The procedural parts of the framework — J, the activation rule, the duty ladder, the rating protocol, the review conditions — are defended by their own arguments, but they are shown in §2.3 to be one argument rather than three.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see Bibliography). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with.
