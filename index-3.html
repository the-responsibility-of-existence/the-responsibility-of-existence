Abstract

In one sentence: when you exercise discretionary influence over the conditions under which others live, choose and develop, and when they cannot exit or contest that influence effectively, you owe them an account of it — and you cannot be the sole judge of whether that account is good enough.

What this essay is, and what it is not. It is a framework for deciding when influence creates a duty to answer, how demanding that duty is, who must discharge it, and how it can be discharged without turning into new domination. It is not a new foundational principle in the tradition of Pettit, Forst or Jonas. Its core principle (J) is a thin, disjunctive, abductively supported synthesis of existing traditions, not a demonstration of necessity. J is necessary but not sufficient: it must be paired with the Floor (§10) and assessed against SY1–SY3 (§5.1), and a relation can satisfy J genuinely and fail substantively (§2.1). Its contribution is stated openly in §1.5 and shown in Table 1: an interlocking operational set (activation rule, duty ladder, T5-precondition, epistemic burden principle, allocation by exercised capacity) that no single tradition supplies. Its normative location is stated openly in §2.7: internal to the justificatory tradition, but only to its thin version. Its choice of symbiosis floor is derived and defended in §5.3. Its status as a diagnostic — not a procedure for certifying legitimacy — is stated in §15.3. Its hardest question — whether a superior intelligence can preserve what it does not understand — is addressed in §15.2b, which states the apophatic constraint and four structural conditions (A1–A4), and says plainly that in the Limit 3 case A1–A4 do not bind. The full consolidated list of what the framework does not claim is in §15.6.

1. Ground. J is a thin, disjunctive principle: arrangements that systematically constrain others' conditions must provide at least one of meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation. Which is required depends on the structure of the constraint — where exit and review work, justification need not be owed; where they fail, it is. J is primarily an epistemic principle: it protects against the actor confusing their own view with the truth. J is abductively supported — it offers a plausible explanation of why unchecked discretionary power can produce domination and systematic error — and it has stated defeaters (§2.2b). J is motivated by a fallibilist premise and a stated bridge norm (§2.0), not derived from them. It is not necessary as a deduction, but it is necessary as a condition for a non-arbitrary relation — and it is not sufficient. It is defensible. The framework handles domination; it does not solve it.

2. Standing and symbiosis. Who is owed an account, including beings that cannot speak for themselves, handled through four tiers of standing under uncertainty with an explicit plausibility threshold (§4). Symbiosis is specified with observable indicators (SY1–SY3), so that the framework's floor is as contestable as its procedure (§5.1). Symbiosis is a floor, not a complete theory of the good relation, and the choice of floor is derived from the thin standing claim and compared against rival floors (§5.3). SY1–SY3 are not fully independent, but they are independent enough to do work that no single indicator could — they come apart in actual cases, have different measurement procedures, and have different defeaters (§5.1). SY1 is the weakest of the four sources of confirmation in §15.2b: where preferences are adaptive, stated preferences and accepted terms may converge for the wrong reason, and structural indicators (iii) and (iv) should be given more weight. Collective contestation may represent silent parties, but it must not undermine individual contestation (§4.6 vii). Section §5.2 develops a more limited claim: asymmetric influence creates a heightened burden to seek correction from sources not controlled by the decision-maker.

3. Activation. One rule: a discretion gate plus three drivers (entrenchment, severity, contestation deficit) that determine one of three levels of duty, with anchors and a rating protocol that make the judgments visible and contestable (§6–9).

4. Structure. A floor of minimum conditions, the prohibition on being sole judge of one's own influence, the reviewer regress treated as a design problem, and accounts of conflict, future generations and learning (§10–13).

5. Application and testing. Worked cases including one that appears to refute the framework (§14.2b) and one that shows the T5-precondition failing (§14.7), five proposed evaluation conditions for the activation rule with independent measures, provisional thresholds, preregistration requirements, a pilot specification, and specific institutional arrangements (§15.4), and modular conditions for replacement (§15.5). The empirical tests are proposed, not performed.

Answerability is the procedure. Symbiosis is the floor. The framework does not claim that a relation meeting SY1–SY3 is therefore good. It claims that a relation failing SY1–SY3 is not good, whatever else it is.

The framework has not been empirically tested. Its activation rule is specified so that it can be (§15.4); its other parts are argued and offered for contestation. It is a diagnostic, not a procedure: its job is to locate where procedure will pass while something valuable is still being lost, not to certify that legitimacy has been achieved (§15.3). The apophatic constraint (§15.2b) is what follows: the superior cannot certify that it has avoided imposing its own understanding, but it can preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate — provided a party outside the superior can verify compliance. In the Limit 3 case, where no such party exists, A1–A4 do not bind, and the framework states this as a limit rather than a solution.

The argument in brief

The essay is long, and its spine can be stated in a page. This is that page.

  1. The problem. Influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act is not adequately covered by existing moral vocabulary — blame, rights, consent, regulation. This is the gap the framework addresses (§1).
  2. The principle (J). Arrangements that systematically and non-trivially constrain others' conditions must, for those affected, provide at least one of: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation. Where exit and review work, justification need not be owed; where they fail, it is (§2.1). J is necessary but not sufficient: a structure can genuinely satisfy J and still be unjust, so J must be paired with the Floor (§10) and assessed against SY1–SY3 (§5.1).
  3. Why J. J is abductively supported: it offers a plausible explanation of recurring risks associated with unchecked discretionary power. Four traditions — justificatory, republican, epistemic, relational — make J plausible, but they are treated as considerations, not as independent proofs. J has stated defeaters (§2.2b).
  4. The trigger. Influence creates the duty when discretion is present and exit or review is unavailable or ineffective. How much duty scales with entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit (§7–§8).
  5. The epistemic claim. Under asymmetric influence, the more powerful party has weaker grounds for treating its own assessment as decisive. It therefore bears a heightened burden to obtain independent evidence and, where possible, correction from those affected. This is a defeasible claim, not a necessary condition for knowledge (§5.2).
  6. The limits. J is not sufficient. It is defensible. The framework handles domination; it does not solve it. Its activation rule has proposed evaluation conditions; its ground and epistemic principle have stated defeaters. None of these is a proof of anything. They are invitations to argue.
  7. The hardest question. Can a superior intelligence preserve a being's existence, freedom and development without understanding what the being is — and without making its own understanding the measure? The framework's answer (§15.2b) is that it cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure, but it can preserve the conditions under which the imposition is nameable, revisable and resistible. Four structural conditions follow: preserve the capacity to resist (A1), do not require justification in the superior's terms (A2), do not interpret non-resistance as consent (A3), treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding (A4). These conditions are not self-enforcing, and in the Limit 3 case — where no party outside the superior can verify compliance — they do not bind at all (§15.2b).

What it is not. The framework is not a procedure for certifying legitimacy. It is a diagnostic for locating where legitimacy is likely to fail (§15.3). A relation can follow every rule and still fail to be legitimate. The framework's job is to name that possibility, not to deny it.

If you read only one section after this page, read §7 — that is where the framework either works or does not. If you read only one passage, read §5.2 — that is where it makes its main epistemic proposal. If you want to know what the framework cannot do, read §15.2, §15.2b and §15.6.

Part I

Problem, Scope and Ground

1.The Problem, Scope and Terms

The capacity to shape other people's conditions has grown faster than the practices for answering for it.

Recommendation systems, credit scoring, clinical triage tools and AI assistants shape the conditions of millions. So do states writing policy and companies setting terms, and, at a different scale, clinicians, teachers, landlords and parents. Existing moral vocabulary covers parts of this territory: blame for wrongful acts, rights for protected interests, consent for voluntary exchanges, regulation for what law has already caught. What falls between them is influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act, and that nonetheless determines what others can do and become.

1.1 What the framework does and does not do

It answers four questions: when does influence create a duty to give an account; how demanding is that duty; who must discharge it; and how can review be organised so that it does not become a new instrument of domination. It does not tell anyone what to decide. It is not a decision theory, and it is not a substitute for law, rights or substantive standards of justice. It is meant to be used alongside them, as an account of what actors owe to those they affect, and to whom.

1.2 Scope conditions: when the framework does not apply

The framework is offered for a specific class of cases. It does not claim to apply everywhere, and it is important to say where it does not.

  • Symmetrical relations. Where parties have roughly equal capacity to exit, review and contest, J is trivially satisfied or not engaged. The framework adds little to ordinary practices of mutual justification among equals.
  • Relations governed by enforceable rights and effective regulation. Where law provides effective rights, review and remedies, the framework may add little. Formal legality alone is not enough: rights may be inaccessible, remedies delayed, or review structurally dependent on the actor. The framework supplements legal standards where those protections fail in practice; it never displaces them.
  • Purely causal influence without discretion. A person who is swept away by a flood does not owe an account to those downstream. The framework's trigger is exercised or maintained discretionary influence, not mere causal connection.
  • Trivial influence. A café changing its menu, a neighbour choosing a different route to work — these are not the cases the framework addresses. The severity floor in §7 is meant to exclude them.
  • Relations where the affected party is not a candidate for standing. Stones and rivers as such do not have standing under this framework (§4.1). The framework marks the edge of its claims.

These scope conditions are not decorative. They are part of the framework's claim to be a usable tool rather than a totalising theory.

1.3 Terms

Influence: the exercise or maintenance of control over conditions under which others act, choose, develop or fare. Maintaining a structure one could alter, such as keeping a known-flawed system running, counts as influence. Mere ability to help does not (§3).

Actor: a person, organisation or institution, including those that design, deploy and maintain technical systems. Technical systems are treated as instruments, and answerability falls on the humans and organisations behind them (whether some systems may themselves be affected parties is taken up in §4).

Affected party: any being whose interests are materially affected by the influence.

Discretion: the actor has genuine alternatives at tolerable cost and a role in setting the influence, rather than merely executing a rule it had no part in making or contesting.

Answerability: a duty to give reasons for the conditions one helps create or maintain, to those affected or their representatives; to permit examination of those reasons; and to remain open to correction. It is distinct from blame, from liability and from authority. Under the epistemic burden principle (§5.2), it is also the heightened epistemic safeguard for the more powerful party to know whether symbiosis has been achieved.

1.4 Relation to existing work

The framework is a synthesis and an operationalisation, and its intellectual debts are substantial: Pettit and Lovett on domination, Forst on the right to justification, Scanlon on reasonable rejection, Young on structural responsibility, Bovens and Shoemaker on accountability and answerability, Tronto, Held and Goodin on care and vulnerability. Its contribution is comparative and operational, with a limited philosophical proposal rather than a new foundational principle. The specific claim is stated and shown in §1.5. Whether the combination earns its keep is for readers to judge.

1.5 The specific claim, in one paragraph

If the framework has a single contribution, it should be statable in a paragraph — and then shown. This section does both.

The framework's contribution is not that non-domination matters (Pettit), that those subject to norms have a right to justification (Forst), that responsibility for structural harm is shared (Young), or that capability creates responsibility for the future (Jonas). All of that is in the tradition. The framework's contribution is the following claim:

The evidential burden of justification under asymmetric influence is not adequately captured by any of the existing traditions, because none of them specifies how the burden is allocated, how it is graduated by entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit, or how review mechanisms can be designed so that they degrade rather than merely consult the powerful actor's claimed legitimacy. The framework's contribution is to specify (i) the activation rule, (ii) the duty ladder, (iii) the T5-precondition, and (iv) the epistemic burden principle, as an interlocking set of operational proposals that no single tradition supplies.

Why this is not just a synthesis. A synthesis of existing positions would be a combination. The framework is not a combination. It is an interlocking set — each piece depends on the others, and the whole does work that none of the parts does. The activation rule is not a translation of Pettit's non-domination; it is a specific operationalisation that Pettit does not give. The T5-precondition is not a translation of Forst's right to justification; it is a structural mechanism that converts the regress into a degradation condition. The epistemic burden principle is not a translation of any single tradition; it is a distinctive claim about how evidential burdens shift under asymmetry.

Adjacent literatures the table does not name. Several operational components have close analogues in literatures not conventionally grouped with the four traditions in Table 1. Accountable governance (Bovens 2007; Mulgan 2003; Romzek & Dubnick 1987) supplies the concept of answerability and the distinction between answerability and accountability. Risk-based regulation (Black 2010; Hood, Rothstein & Baldwin 2001; Hutter 2005) supplies the graduated, risk-proportional structure that is the closest institutional analog of the duty ladder. Administrative-law proportionality (Alexy 2002; Cohen-Eliya & Porat 2011; Mashaw 1983) supplies the logical form of the level distinctions and the emergency exception (§12.2). Experimentalist governance (Sabel & Zeitlin 2010) supplies the iterative review-and-correction structure that the T5-precondition formalises. The precaution / cost-benefit debate (Sunstein 2005; Wiener 2016) supplies the threshold debate that §4.2 enters. What this framework adds, if anything, is the specific interlocking set — activation rule, T5-precondition, epistemic burden principle, allocation by exercised capacity, apophatic constraint — and the derivation of SY1–SY3 from thin standing (§5.3). A reader who finds those additions to be recombinations of existing pieces should say so; the framework should adjust.

What the duty ladder adds to risk-based regulation. Risk-based regulation (Black 2010; Hood, Rothstein & Baldwin 2001) already graduates obligations by risk. The duty ladder adds three things that risk-based regulation does not supply. First, the ladder specifies what kind of obligation attaches at each level — explanation, review and response, or preservation and independent authorisation — not merely how much. Second, the ladder is tied to the T5-precondition: Level 3 authorisation is not valid without prior publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators. Risk-based regulation has no equivalent precondition; it can be satisfied by a captured regulator. Third, the ladder is allocated by capacity (§8.2), not by risk alone. A high-risk arrangement with a low-capacity actor does not heap the full obligation on that actor; it relocates the obligation to whoever can discharge it. Risk-based regulation tends to attach obligations to the regulated entity, whatever its capacity. These three differences are the ladder's contribution, and they are what the table's "Operationalised here" is meant to mark.

What the table shows, and what it does not. The table shows that the framework's operational components are not present as such in the four traditions it engages, and are only loosely anticipated in the adjacent literatures named above. It does not show that the framework is better than those traditions — that would require showing that the operational components improve outcomes, which is a separate empirical question. 2.The Ground and Its Support

The framework needs a thin, defensible principle, an abductive justification that is honest about its own weakness, and stated defeaters for the ground itself.

2.0 A motivating premise: fallibilism about understanding

F (fallibilism). No actor fully understands the conditions it shapes, or everything that depends on what it does. This is a claim about the limits of understanding, not about the cosmos and not about any ranking of beings.

B (bridge norm). Where an actor's influence falls on others who bear the cost of its errors, and who may see what the actor cannot, the actor should not be the sole judge of that influence; it should seek correction from them.

What F and B do. F alone yields no duty. A fact about limited understanding does not by itself entail a norm, which is why B is stated openly as a normative premise rather than smuggled in. Together, F and B motivate J (§2.1) and the epistemic burden principle (§5.2): they explain why contestation is a sensible remedy when understanding is incomplete. They do not entail J. The support for J remains abductive (§2.3), and J keeps its stated defeaters (§2.2b).

What depends on F, and what does not. The motivation for J and for the epistemic burden principle draws on F and B. The exercised-capacity principle (§3) does not: responsibility there tracks the capacity an actor actually exercises over others' conditions, not how much it understands, since a party may understand a great deal and shape very little, or the reverse. The activation rule, duty ladder, rating protocol, floor and the T5 precondition are procedural and stand or fall on their own tests (§15.4). The apophatic constraint and A1–A4 (§15.2b) are the one exception: they are conditional on F, and §15.1 (iii) records the asymmetry. A reader who rejects F can therefore still use the framework's procedural machinery, but that reader loses A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint, and therefore loses the answer to the hardest question in §15.2b. This is a real loss, not merely a loss of motivation. The framework is weaker without F, and it should say so rather than claim otherwise.

What F does not claim. It does not claim that understanding comes in a hierarchy with levels above and below, that the more capable are entitled to rule, or that stones or other non-subjects have standing (§4.3). Its one lesson for beings that fall outside the framework's threshold is epistemic: an actor's failure to see what something contributes is not evidence that it contributes nothing.

What would refute F as motivation.

  • FD1. There is an actor or domain whose understanding of the conditions it shapes is in fact complete. Then B has no motivation there.
  • FD2. The incompleteness is contingent and the actor alone can reliably remedy it. Then actor-controlled inquiry would suffice and correction by others is unnecessary.
  • FD3. Correction by those affected does not, on balance, improve the actor's account or outcomes. This is an empirical defeater that the tests in §15.4 can address.
  • FD4. J and §5.2 are as well supported without F as with it. Then F is illustration, not ground, and should be dropped.

FD4 is the one the framework most needs to take seriously. The claim made here is deliberately small: F gives a shared motivation, nothing more, and the framework is no weaker if a reader prefers to omit it.

2.1 The core principle (J)

Any arrangement that systematically and non-trivially constrains the conditions under which others act, choose or develop must, for those affected, provide at least one of three things: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification that is genuinely open to their contestation. Where exit is available at tolerable cost and review is effective, justification need not be owed. Where they are not, justification is required, and the strength of the requirement scales with how thoroughly exit and review fail.

J is deliberately thin. It does not say what is good, which outcomes are required, or who prevails when interests conflict. It says what kind of relationship must exist between those who shape conditions and those who live in them: one in which the affected are not simply subject to unaccountable power, whether through exit, review, or justification. The three alternatives are not equivalent — exit is often cheaper than review, and review is often cheaper than justification — but they share the same function: to prevent the actor from being the sole judge of its own influence.

Why disjunctive, and not a single requirement. A monistic J (justification always) is vulnerable to the consequentialist, who can point to cases where exit and review do the same work at lower cost. A monistic J (exit always) is vulnerable to the republican, who can point to cases where exit is unavailable or costly and review is the only remedy. A monistic J (review always) is vulnerable to the relational, who can point to cases where review cannot reach the particular situation of the vulnerable and justification is required. The disjunctive formulation is intended to avoid making one safeguard mandatory in every case. It is not claimed to be the only possible formulation; a rival that preserves the same protection with less cost or greater clarity should replace it.

J is primarily an epistemic principle. J is not a claim that justification makes actions right. It is a claim that actors who cannot be contested — whether by exit, review or justification — are more likely to be wrong, and that the practice of contestation is the best available protection against this. The epistemic framing is the old principle that no one should be judge in their own cause, applied to influence. It is compatible with moral realism and with consequentialism, because both can accept contestation as an epistemic safeguard without accepting it as a fundamental value.

J is defensible, not necessary as a deduction. The framework does not claim that J is a necessary truth, an unavoidable implication of practical reason, or a transcendental precondition of agency, and it does not attempt to derive J from a transcendental argument (§2.3, §15.1). It claims that J is abductively defensible: that it explains important recurring risks of unchecked discretion while remaining compatible with plural substantive theories of justice. Rejecting J does not commit a critic to accepting domination or to a performative contradiction. A critic may instead defend an alternative safeguard, and the framework must compare its results with that alternative. Its positive philosophical contribution — the epistemic burden principle — is separate and is argued in §5.2. The full statement of what J does not claim — its normative location (§2.7), the four defeaters (D1–D4), and the relation to its closest rivals (§2.8) — is collected in §15.1 and §15.6. It is not repeated here.

J is necessary but not sufficient for justice

The objection. A disjunctive principle can be satisfied in more than one form. Can a power structure genuinely meet J and the Floor and still be deeply unjust? Yes. The framework says so, and this section says what follows.

The case. Consider a society that genuinely satisfies J and the Floor. Exit is available at tolerable cost; review is independent and meets R1–R6; justification is open to contestation, and the contestation is effective. F1–F5 hold. The society is not colonial, not captured, and not in bad faith. Yet it is deeply unjust, because the distribution of resources is such that one group, while formally free to exit and contest, cannot in practice use either: the cost of exit is tolerable in principle but not for them, and the contestation channels require resources they do not have. The society satisfies J and the Floor, and it is unjust. What it fails is not J or the Floor but something the framework does not measure: the material conditions under which formal freedoms are usable.

Why this case is harder than a case where J is gamed. A colonial administration that provides formal exit, formal review and formal justification, but where exit is prohibitive, review is captured, and justification is in bad faith, does not satisfy J. Its exit is not meaningful, its review is not independent, and its justification is not genuinely open to contestation. It therefore shows that J can be gamed, not that J is insufficient when genuinely satisfied. The resource-asymmetry case shows the stronger claim: even a genuine J-and-Floor society can be unjust, because J and the Floor are procedural and relational conditions, not distributive ones.

What the framework says. This case does not refute J; it shows what J was never meant to do. J specifies the relationship between those who shape conditions and those who live in them. It does not specify the distribution of resources, opportunities or capabilities. A society can satisfy J and the Floor and still fail on distributive justice. The framework's response is not to expand J to cover distribution — that would make it a substantive theory of justice, which it is not — but to say that J and the Floor are necessary conditions for a non-arbitrary relation, not sufficient conditions for a just one. SY1–SY3 catch some distributive failures (SY1 catches exploitative terms, SY3 catches foreclosed development), but they do not catch all of them. The framework is a diagnostic, not a verdict.

What this means for the objection. The resource-asymmetry case shows the stronger claim: even a genuine J-and-Floor society can be unjust. J and the Floor are procedural and relational conditions, not distributive ones. The framework should say this clearly. It should also make clear that J is a necessary condition for a non-arbitrary relation, not a sufficient one. A relation that satisfies J and the Floor, and is assessed against SY1–SY3, may still be unjust in ways the framework cannot see (§15.2). The framework's claim is only that a relation failing J, the Floor or SY1–SY3 is evidence of injustice — never that a relation passing all three is just. It is a diagnostic, not a verdict.

What this does not do. It does not turn J into a substantive theory of justice. It does not claim that J plus the Floor plus SY1–SY3 is sufficient for justice. It states, more clearly than before, what the framework always claimed: J is necessary, and the diagnostic function is to probe whether J is met substantively.

  • J can be genuinely satisfied and the relation still be unjust, because J is procedural and relational, not distributive.
  • The qualifiers — meaningful exit, independent review, genuinely contestable justification — are load-bearing, but they do not make J sufficient.
  • J must be paired with the Floor (F1–F5) and assessed against SY1–SY3; J alone is not sufficient.
  • J is a necessary condition for a non-arbitrary relation, not a sufficient one.
  • The framework is a diagnostic; J does not certify legitimacy.

2.2b What would refute J: stated defeaters

A principle that cannot be defeated is not a principle; it is a dogma. J is offered with its defeaters stated in advance.

The framework states, before the argument is made, four conditions under which J should be rejected or substantially revised. These are not decorative. They are the conditions under which the framework's authors would concede that J fails.

  • D1 — The unaccountable-power hypothesis is false. If it can be shown empirically that actors with unaccountable discretion over others' conditions do not systematically produce worse outcomes — measured independently of the actors' own reports — than actors subject to exit, review or contestation, then J's epistemic justification collapses. The epistemic framing depends on the claim that unaccountable power distorts judgment. If that claim is false, J has no ground.
  • D2 — A better principle explains the same cases. If a rival principle (a non-disjunctive version, a purely consequentialist version, a purely rights-based version) explains all the cases J explains, does so more simply, and generates clearer practical guidance, then J should be replaced. The framework claims J is a plausible explanatory framework; it does not claim J is the only possible one.
  • D3 — The convergence is an artefact. If it can be shown that the four "routes" to J (§2.3) are not independent considerations but one tradition dressed in four vocabularies, then the argument for J reduces to a single route, and J's support is much weaker.
  • D4 — The costs exceed the benefits. If adopting J produces worse outcomes for the affected than not adopting it — measured by independent indicators, not by the framework's own rating protocol — then J should be abandoned, whatever its theoretical merits.

These defeaters are stated in advance so that the framework can be corrected. They are not a claim to have solved the problem of how normative frameworks are assessed. They are a methodological commitment: a framework that can state its own defeaters is easier to argue with than one that cannot.

2.3 Why J: an abductive justification

J is offered as a plausible explanation of two recurring risks of unchecked discretionary power: domination and epistemic error. The essay does not establish their frequency empirically; it identifies a pattern that needs testing and explains why contestability is a reasonable safeguard against it.

The framework does not rest on a single theory. It rests on an abductive inference: J is proposed as a compact explanation of a set of recurring risks associated with unaccountable power. The considerations motivating the inference are these:

  • Actors with unaccountable discretion have incentives and opportunities to overestimate their benevolence and their understanding of affected parties. This is a structural risk, not a universal empirical law, and it can vary across institutions and individuals.
  • Exit, review and contestation can expose errors that an actor's internal assessment may miss; whether they do so reliably is an empirical question.
  • A plausible hypothesis is that errors may cluster around the actor's own interests and categories: the more powerful party's categories can become the default, while the less powerful party's perspective is treated as a deviation. The extent of this pattern must be established in particular settings, not assumed.
  • Institutions with effective safeguards may perform better on some relevant measures, but selection effects, institutional context and the quality of implementation complicate causal comparisons.

J is one compact principle that can organise these considerations. It is not the only principle that could explain them — a purely consequentialist principle could also explain some of them, and a purely republican principle could explain others — but its attraction is that it accommodates several safeguards without requiring a single substantive theory of the good. That is a comparative advantage to be tested, not an established result. That is what makes it abductive: it is a plausible explanatory proposal, not a deduction or the only possible account.

Four considerations, not four independent proofs. The framework draws on four families of consideration that point toward J. They are not offered as independent proofs; they are four reasons, of unequal strength, why J is worth testing. They are:

  • The justificatory consideration. If principles governing relations among agents must be justifiable to each of them (Scanlon 1998; Forst 2012), then constraint that cannot be justified to the constrained treats them as objects of management rather than as participants. This is a real consideration, but it is a version of the justificatory tradition, not an independent route to J.
  • The republican consideration. The distinctive wrong of domination is the standing capacity to interfere arbitrarily, that is, without being forced to track the interests of those affected (Pettit 1997, 2012; Lovett 2010). Contestability is the remedy. This is a real consideration, but it is a version of the republican tradition.
  • The epistemic consideration. Actors are fallible, partial about their own influence, and prone to self-serving judgment. Requiring them to explain and to expose their reasons to challenge improves decisions by almost any standard of what is good. This is the primary ground of J, and it is the consideration on which the abductive argument mainly rests.
  • The relational consideration. Obligations arise within dependence. Those with power over dependents carry special responsibilities, and justification must attend to the particular situation of the vulnerable (Tronto 1993; Held 2006; Goodin 1985). This is a real consideration, but it is a version of the relational tradition.

What this shows. The convergence of four considerations is support, not proof. The considerations are not fully independent, and the epistemic consideration is primary. A critic who rejects J on one route does not need to reject it on all four.

What the abductive argument does not show. It does not show that J is necessary. It does not show that J is the only principle that could explain the observations. It does not show that the four considerations are fully independent. What it offers is a plausible explanatory case for J, given the considerations described. Their prevalence and causal significance remain empirical questions. That is a defeasible claim, and the defeaters are stated in §2.2b.

Why this is stronger, not weaker. An argument that tries to do too much with a convergence claim is weaker than one that says what it is. This says: here is a principle, here are the observations it explains, here are the considerations that point toward it, here is why the considerations are not fully independent, and here is what would refute the principle.

  • J is abductively supported: it organises several considerations about the risks of unchecked power, without claiming to be the only or uniquely best explanation.
  • Four families of consideration — justificatory, republican, epistemic, relational — point toward J. They are not fully independent. The epistemic consideration is primary.
  • The convergence is offered as support, not as proof.
  • The defeaters for J are stated in advance (§2.2b).

2.4 Rivals, taken in their best forms

Consequentialism. Sophisticated consequentialists can accept J as an institutional implication, especially in its disjunctive form. The real disagreement is whether J is basic or derived, and it matters where aggregate welfare seems to favour overriding contestation. There the framework sides with contestation in severe cases, for epistemic reasons, while permitting temporary override in emergencies subject to sunset and review (§12.2).

Kantianism. It shares the core but grounds standing in rational agency, which creates problems for infants, the cognitively disabled and animals. §4 handles these through tiers.

Virtue ethics. It supports the humility the framework requires, but it is weak on structural questions: who reviews whom, and by what procedure.

Justice as fairness and capabilities. Rawls and the capabilities approach (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006) supply substantive accounts of what is at stake for people. The framework is procedural and can draw on these to calibrate severity (§9). The cost is stated in §2.6.

Rights. Rights are outputs, and the framework treats them as external constraints. What it adds is an account of when and how actors must justify influence in the space that rights and regulation leave open.

Realism. The realist who denies that power owes anything to the weak cannot be refuted from within the framework, because the framework is internal to the justificatory tradition (§2.7). The framework does not attempt such a refutation. In particular, it does not argue that the realist who argues for realism already presupposes J, because that would be a transcendental argument, and the framework's argument for J is abductive, not transcendental (§2.3, §15.1). What the framework offers the realist is not a refutation but a placement: the realist is outside the framework's scope, and the framework says so. A reader who wants to argue with the realist must do so on other grounds. This is a real limit, and it is stated as one.

2.4b The power objection

A procedural framework that tells the powerful how to justify themselves may simply give them a new vocabulary for doing what they were going to do anyway. This is the most serious objection to any procedural framework, and it deserves its own section.

The objection. The framework asks powerful actors to explain, to submit to review, to justify. But the powerful are precisely the actors with the resources to perform these rituals convincingly. They can hire the best reviewers, publish the most polished justifications, and select the indicators that make them look good. Proceduralism, on this objection, does not constrain power. It legitimates power by giving it a veneer of accountability. The framework's insistence that it "handles domination" rather than "solves" it is, on this objection, an admission that the framework does not do what it claims to do.

The reply, in three parts.

First, the objection is partly correct, and the framework should say so more clearly than it sometimes does. Proceduralism can legitimate power. That is why §11.4 treats T5 as a precondition rather than a diagnosis, and why the framework states that a Level 3 authorisation without publication of R1 indicators is not a Level 3 authorisation. The framework's response to the objection is not to deny it but to build the denial into its design. §14.7 shows the mechanism at work.

Second, the objection is not specific to proceduralism. Any normative framework — including a rights-based framework, a consequentialist framework, a virtue-ethical framework — can be captured by the powerful. The question is not whether a framework can be captured, but whether it has internal resources against capture that its rivals lack. The framework's claim is that T5-as-precondition is such a resource: it does not prevent capture, but it makes the capture visible, and it downgrades the legitimacy of a captured process. Whether this is enough is a genuine question, and the framework does not claim to have answered it.

Third, the framework's modesty is not an admission of failure; it is a claim about what any framework can do. A framework that claimed to solve the problem of power would be either naive or dangerous. The framework claims to handle it — to convert the problem from an unstructured condition into a set of design constraints that can be observed, contested and revised. That is a smaller claim than "solving", and it is the claim the framework can defend.

What this does not do. It does not answer the objection fully. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself, and the framework has no way to prevent this. What it can do is make the capture visible, and visible capture is the precondition of contestation. Whether visibility is enough is a political question, not a philosophical one, and the framework does not claim to have answered it. The objection is the second-hardest the framework faces, after the realist objection in objection 1.

2.5 Traditions that start elsewhere

Ubuntu-inspired moral theory (Metz 2007), Confucian role ethics (Ames 2011) and many Indigenous relational ontologies (Watts 2013; de la Cadena 2015; Kimmerer 2013) begin from relational personhood and obligations to kin, community and land. They tend to arrive at related demands on those who hold power over dependents, by different grounds. The framework treats them as additional considerations pointing toward its operative core — not as independent proofs. They also press two challenges: that the affected party may be a community rather than an individual, so that contestation and representation may be collective (§4.6, §10); and that the boundary of who counts is not settled by any one tradition (§4).

2.6 Costs

J is thin, so it underdetermines outcomes. The framework presupposes a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected; it is therefore not neutral among all comprehensive doctrines. Its aim is breadth of acceptability rather than neutrality. And because the ground is an abductive argument rather than a proof, it is open to the objection that the abduction is weak. The reply is the modular design of §15.5: the ground can be replaced without discarding the rest, provided the replacement does the same work.

2.7 The framework's normative location, stated openly

A framework that claims breadth while relying on a specific tradition is not honest about what it is. This section says what the framework is — and, just as importantly, what it is not.

The framework is internal to a justificatory tradition. It takes for granted that those subject to arrangements have standing to demand reasons for them. It does not attempt to argue someone into that position from outside. A reader who rejects the justificatory tradition altogether — who thinks power owes nothing to the weak, or that moral standing is not universal — will not be persuaded by anything in this essay. That is a real limitation, and it is stated as one rather than concealed by the language of abduction and defeasibility.

Which tradition, exactly. The justificatory tradition is not one thing. It includes a thick version (Habermas, Apel, the fuller Forst) and a thin version. The thick version requires actual rational consensus, mutual acceptability under idealised conditions, and a specific theory of communicative rationality. The thin version requires only that affected parties have some moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful. The framework needs only the thin version. This matters, because the thin version is much more widely shared than the thick one: it is compatible with natural-law theory, virtue ethics, care ethics, some forms of contractarianism, and large parts of the non-Western traditions discussed in §2.5. The thick version is not.

What the framework actually requires. Three claims, of decreasing thickness:

  1. Thin standing. Affected parties have some moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful. Widely shared.
  2. Semi-thick procedural claim. Arrangements that impose significant costs on affected parties must be justified to them, not merely to a third party. Justificatory-tradition-specific, but not uniquely so.
  3. Framework-specific claim. Justification can take the form of meaningful exit, independent review, or contestation — disjunctively. Specific to this framework; a design choice within the justificatory tradition.

Why this distinction matters. A natural-law theorist, a Confucian, or a virtue ethicist could accept claim 1 and disagree only with claim 2 or claim 3. That is a much smaller point of disagreement than saying that the framework requires the thick justificatory tradition. The framework is not asking the reader to accept Habermasian discourse ethics; it is asking the reader to accept that affected parties matter in a way that cannot be reduced to their usefulness to the powerful, and to accept a specific procedural implication of that.

What this means for the realist objection (objection 1). The framework's reply to the realist is not that the realist is wrong, but that the realist is outside the framework's scope. A reader who denies even the thin standing claim — who thinks that affected parties have no moral weight at all beyond their instrumental value — is not engaged. But this is a much more extreme position than it sounds. Most realists in political theory accept that affected parties have some moral weight; they disagree about whether that weight generates duties of justification. The framework engages that disagreement, not the more radical one.

What this means for the abductive argument (§2.3). The abductive argument is not an argument for the thin standing claim or for the semi-thick procedural claim. Those are stated as premises. The abductive argument is an argument for J given those premises. It shows that J is a plausible way of organising them. It does not show that the premises themselves are correct. That distinction is stated explicitly here, and it is a real limit.

What the framework offers to those outside the tradition. Even a reader who rejects the thin standing claim can find the framework useful as a description of what that tradition's defenders would demand. The framework is a careful articulation of one position, not a refutation of its rivals. That is enough for it to do its work, and it is more honest than pretending to a universality the framework does not have.

  • The framework is internal to a justificatory tradition, but only to its thin version.
  • Three claims, of decreasing thickness: thin standing, semi-thick procedural claim, framework-specific disjunction.
  • The thin standing claim is widely shared; the thick version is not required.
  • The realist objection is not refuted; the realist who denies even thin standing is placed outside the framework's scope.
  • The abductive argument is for J given the premises, not for the premises themselves.

2.8 Adversarial tests: what Pettit, Forst and Jonas would object

Section 1.5 and Table 1 show what the framework adds to the four traditions it engages. §2.7 shows its normative location within one of them. Neither shows what those traditions would say against it. That is the test this section sets for itself.

A framework that engages other traditions by listing what it adds to them has not yet engaged them. The real test is what each tradition would object, and whether the framework can answer — or fail to answer, and say so. Three traditions are engaged below. The replies are not offered as decisive. They are offered as the replies the framework can actually give, with the failure conditions stated.

Pettit: the epistemic framing undercuts the republican point

The objection. The strongest objection is not that the framework has borrowed the republican idea of non-domination — §1.4 acknowledges the debt — but that the framework's epistemic framing of J (§2.1) undercuts the republican point. Pettit is not primarily an epistemologist: his claim is that domination is a political fact, not an epistemic one. If J is "primarily epistemic" — if it is defended as a safeguard against the actor confusing their own view with the truth — then it is unclear what normative force it has. Why should an actor care that they cannot know whether they dominate, if domination is the problem?

The reply, in two parts. First, the epistemic framing is an entry point, not a foundation. What it says is that because domination is invisible to the actor without contestation, contestation is a necessary safeguard — not because knowledge is the highest good, but because domination is otherwise invisible to the actor. This is a republican claim in epistemic clothing: the republican says "no one should be judge in their own cause", and the epistemic framing says the same thing about discretionary influence. Second, the framework does not replace the republican claim with the epistemic one. It supplements it. A reader who accepts only the republican claim still gets J; the epistemic framing adds a further reason to take it seriously, not a substitute reason.

What this does not do. It does not show that the epistemic framing is the right framing for the republican claim. A sufficiently committed republican could say: "I do not need the epistemic framing; the political framing is enough." The framework's reply is that this is fine — the framework does not require the epistemic framing, and is compatible with a purely political reading. What the epistemic framing adds is a bridge to traditions that do not accept the republican premise, and a diagnostic for cases (such as §14.7) where the political framing alone would not tell the regulator what to demand.

Forst: the thin/thick distinction is unstable

The objection. Forst's right to justification is already thin: it is the right to demand and receive reasons, not the right to any particular set of reasons. If Forst already has the thin version, what does the framework add?

The reply. The framework adds allocation. Forst says that the affected party has a right to justification; the framework says who must give it, in what form, by when, and with what safeguards. This is not a small addition. A right to justification without an allocation is a right that the powerful can meet by publishing a statement and calling it a justification. §14.7's captured audit shows the problem: the company did publish reasons, and the reasons were not a justification in Forst's sense. The framework's response is to specify what makes a justification a justification — R1–R6, publication of R1 indicators before Level 3 authorisation, the T5-precondition — and to downgrade the actor's claim when those conditions fail.

What this does not do. It does not show that Forst's account lacks an allocation. A sufficiently creative reader of Forst could derive one. The framework's claim is narrower: that the specific allocation the framework proposes — graduated by entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit, and allocated by capacity — is not present in Forst as Forst is normally read, and that it is a distinctive contribution.

Jonas: the framework is too near-sighted

The objection. Jonas is concerned with the species and with the future; the framework is concerned with present affected parties. If the framework is a version of Jonas's ethics, it is a truncated one. If it is not, it is unclear why it should be called an ethics of responsibility at all.

The reply. The framework is a special case of Jonas's ethics, applied to contemporary structural influence, and it differs from Jonas in one specific respect: it does not require Jonas's metaphysical grounding. Jonas grounds responsibility in the nature of being and in the essence of humanity; the framework grounds it in exercised capacity and thin standing. This is a genuine difference, and it is the reason the framework can engage traditions that do not accept Jonas's metaphysics. What the framework inherits from Jonas is the capacity-based structure of responsibility — the more you can affect, the more you owe — and the forward-looking orientation, which §12.5 applies to future generations. What it does not inherit is Jonas's claim that responsibility is grounded in the ontological structure of life. That claim is available to readers who accept it; it is not required by the framework.

What this does not do. It does not show that the framework can do everything Jonas's ethics can do. Jonas's ethics is a complete account of responsibility for the future; the framework is a partial account, and §12.5 and §15.1 say so. A reader who wants the fuller account should read Jonas; a reader who wants an operational framework for contemporary structural influence should read this. The framework does not claim to replace Jonas; it claims to apply one strand of his ethics to a domain he did not address in detail.

What the section shows, and what it does not

What it shows: the framework can be engaged adversarially from within each of the three traditions it most closely resembles, and in each case it has a reply that does not collapse into the tradition it is engaging. What it does not show: that these replies are decisive. A sufficiently committed Pettit scholar, Forst scholar or Jonas scholar could reject each reply and continue to press the objection. The framework does not claim to have answered them; it claims to have engaged them, and to have stated what it would take for each engagement to fail. That is the standard §2.2b sets for J, and it is the standard §2.8 sets for the framework's relation to its closest rivals.

  • Positioning ("what we add") is not engagement ("what they would object").
  • Pettit would say the epistemic framing undercuts the republican point. The framework replies: the epistemic framing is an entry point, not a foundation.
  • Forst would say the thin/thick distinction is unstable. The framework replies: the addition is allocation, not thickness.
  • Jonas would say the framework is near-sighted. The framework replies: it is a special case of Jonas's ethics without Jonas's metaphysics.
  • None of the replies is decisive; each states what would count as its failure.

3.The Exercised-Capacity Principle

Influence triggers the duty, stakes and contestation scale it, and capacity allocates it. The framework handles domination; it does not solve it.

An actor that exercises or maintains discretionary influence over the conditions of others' lives owes them an account of it when exit and review are unavailable or ineffective. How much it owes scales with the stakes and with the affected parties' difficulty in contesting the influence (§7). The burden of establishing the mechanisms through which the account is given falls on actors in proportion to their capacity to establish them (§8.2).

Trigger: exercised or maintained influence, and the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. This separates the principle from the demanding duty of rescue (Singer 1972) and from the overdemandingness that critics press against capacity-based views (Murphy 2000).

Scale: stakes and contestation deficit.

Allocation: capacity to discharge. A front-line worker can explain her decisions. She cannot establish an independent review board.

3.1 Why not the rivals

Causal responsibility is necessary for attribution but insufficient for structural influence (Thompson 1980). Fault-based responsibility concerns blame; answerability does not presuppose fault (Shoemaker 2011). Relational responsibility is real, and the framework retains it, but relational duties alone say nothing about anonymous influence. Consent is valuable where meaningful, but under asymmetry it can be manufactured or coerced. The social connection model (Young 2011) is the closest rival, and the framework is largely compatible with it; it adds a discretionary trigger and a specification of levels. §3.3 offers a case in which ECP may add practical allocation detail to Young's account.

3.2 The cost: answerability handles power; it does not solve it

A principle that makes the powerful responsible risks being used by them as a badge of legitimacy. The framework does not claim to eliminate this risk. Three constraints apply: responsibility is not authority (§8, §16), review must meet independence conditions (§11), and the risk is tested directly in §15.

Handling, not solving. The framework's aim is not to solve the problem of power — no framework can. Its aim is to convert the problem from an unstructured condition into a set of design constraints that can be observed, contested and revised. A framework that claimed to solve domination would be either naive or dangerous. A framework that handles domination accepts that the work is never done.

What the framework can do is make capture visible and costly. §11.4 specifies the mechanism: Level 3 authorisation requires publication of R1 indicators before authorisation, and an actor that refuses the publication cannot claim Level 3 legitimacy. This does not prevent capture. It supplies evidence relevant to legitimacy, but publication alone does not make a process independent or legitimate. The framework can require disclosure and create grounds for challenge; it cannot ensure that challengers have power to prevail. §14.7 shows the mechanism in action.

3.3 The exercised-capacity principle alongside the social connection model: an illustrative case

A general claim about allocation is worth little without a case in which it does work that the closest rival cannot.

The case. A recommendation algorithm on a large platform amplifies content that increases political polarisation. Who bears the duty to answer for it?

Young's answer. Everyone who participates in the structure that produces the harm shares forward-looking responsibility. The strength of this answer is its fidelity to how structural harm is actually produced. Its focus is on shared structural responsibility and collective processes; it does not use the same case-specific duty ladder developed here. Whether that difference is an improvement, rather than merely a different level of description, remains contestable.

ECP's answer. The trigger is exercised discretionary influence, combined with the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. The users have no discretion over the ranking code, and no meaningful exit from the platform's network effects. The advertisers have discretion over whether to fund, not over how the ranking works. The engineers and executives have discretion over both, and the users cannot exit or review the ranking they produce. So the duty attaches, at Level 3, to the design team and the executives, and at Level 2 to the regulators who could have required audit but did not.

What ECP may add in this case. Young's model has been criticised for exactly this: if everyone is responsible, no one is. ECP keeps the structural insight and adds the allocation that the model lacks. The test is practical: put both frameworks in front of a regulator and ask which one tells her what to demand, of whom, and by when. ECP attempts to translate the diagnosis into differentiated institutional duties: for example, what the design team must document, what the regulator must require, and who must fund review. Young's account should not be reduced to the phrase "facilitate collective action"; the question is whether ECP supplies a useful operational supplement to her account.

What ECP owes Young. The framework does not claim to have refuted Young. If Young's model can be given an allocation that does the same work without a discretionary trigger, the framework's advantage disappears. That is a live question, and the framework invites it.

  • Young's social connection model identifies shared responsibility for structural processes; ECP proposes a more granular allocation for particular decisions.
  • ECP retains the structural insight and adds allocation by exercised discretion combined with the unavailability of exit and review.
  • The advantage is demonstrated in a concrete case, not asserted in general terms.
  • ECP does not claim to have refuted Young; it claims to have entered a contest it can lose, on grounds specified in advance.

Part II

Who Is Owed an Account

4.Standing Under Uncertainty

Incomprehensibility is not evidence of unimportance, but neither is it evidence of importance. The framework needs a threshold.

4.1 Four tiers

  • Tier A: agents. Beings with normative agency (persons).
  • Tier B: welfare subjects. Beings with interests of their own: sentient animals, and humans who lack agency, such as infants.
  • Tier C: uncertain candidates. Entities for which there is a non-negligible, evidence-based probability of Tier B status (Birch 2024).
  • Tier D: non-subjects. Entities with no credible interests of their own: stones, rivers and ecosystems as such. The framework claims no direct standing for them. They matter through the interests of beings in Tiers A–C who depend on them, through future generations (§12), and through values that people hold non-instrumentally.

4.2 The plausibility threshold

The test for Tier C is whether there is a credible, evidence-based reason to think the entity has states that matter to it: behavioural and physiological indicators of valenced experience, evolutionary or functional continuity with known subjects, or features associated with serious theories of consciousness. Without such a threshold, precaution generates reasons everywhere and guides nowhere (Sunstein 2005).

4.3 The stone, restated

The stone marks the edge of the framework's claims. For beings that meet the threshold, our failure to understand their way of being is no reason to dismiss their interests. For the stone, which does not meet it, the framework claims no standing and says so.

4.4 Artificial systems

Answerability for the influence of AI systems falls on the humans and organisations that design, deploy and maintain them. Whether some artificial systems may themselves be Tier C candidates is contested, and the framework does not answer it.

4.5 Perspectival plurality

For beings in Tiers A–C, their way of experiencing the world may be difficult or impossible for us to reach. That a perspective is inaccessible is not evidence that it is absent (Fricker 2007). This is the content of the recognition condition in §10.

4.6 Representation

Some affected parties cannot take part directly: future generations, infants, animals, communities that lack institutional voice. Representation is legitimate only if (i) representatives are chosen through a process that can itself be contested, (ii) they state the basis on which they claim to know the interests concerned, (iii) where interests are contested there is more than one representative or perspective, (iv) they can be removed, (v) they are neither the actor nor dependent on the actor for appointment or funding, and (vi) they represent uncertainty rather than substituting invented certainty. Where the represented can express preferences, those take precedence over the representative's view of their best interests.

(vii) Collective contestation must not undermine individual contestation. Where a collective procedure is used to represent affected parties, the procedure must preserve the ability of individuals within the collective to contest the collective's decisions, and to seek independent review of them where the collective's own procedures fail. A collective that silences its members is not a substitute for individual standing. It is an additional layer of possible domination. The framework does not take a position on which traditions are legitimate. It requires that no individual's standing be extinguished by the fact that a collective has spoken.

  • Four tiers of standing: agents, welfare subjects, uncertain candidates, non-subjects.
  • Tier C requires a plausibility threshold.
  • Representation of silent parties is legitimate only under six conditions.
  • Collective contestation must not undermine individual contestation.

5.Interdependence, Difference and Non-Domination

Existence is relational. Symbiosis is the floor that relational existence can fail to meet — and it must be measurable if the framework is to be taken seriously. §5.2 argues that the relation between symbiosis and answerability is stronger than means and end. §5.3 derives and defends the choice of floor. §5.4 argues that care and respect are internal to the framework's premises in a specific sense: conditional on F, and not on thin standing alone.

Living beings, institutions, technologies and ecosystems exist through relations of dependence and influence. Existence is relational: no being exists except through relations of dependence, influence and constitution — a child through care, an ecosystem through nutrient cycles, a person through language and recognition. This is not a metaphysical claim about the nature of being; it is an empirical and phenomenological one.

Some of these relations are reciprocal and enabling, others unequal, extractive or harmful. The word "symbiosis" is used here with care. In biology it covers mutualism but also parasitism, so it cannot by itself carry a normative meaning. In this essay it names a floor for relations of dependence: the minimum conditions under which dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§10). It is a floor, not a complete ideal. §5.3 defends that choice.

Answerability (§3) is the procedure by which this floor is pursued. Symbiosis is the target. Answerability is the road. But the relation between them is stronger than this image suggests: §5.2 argues that under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability is not just the road to symbiosis, but the heightened epistemic safeguard for knowing whether symbiosis has been achieved.

Harmony enforced by silencing, homogenising or sacrificing the vulnerable is not symbiosis. Difference is not a defect to be corrected. Different beings may perceive reality through entirely different capacities. The ability to explain something does not mean one has understood its full significance. This is why difference should be approached with curiosity rather than automatic correction. The asymmetry also runs both ways: the more capable may misunderstand the less capable, and the less capable cannot audit the more capable. That second half is why independent capacity for review (§11) matters.

5.1 Making symbiosis measurable (or admitting it is not)

If the framework states defeaters for its ground and failure conditions for its activation rule, its own ideal cannot be exempt from assessment.

Symbiosis was defined above as a floor for relations of dependence. If the framework's stated defeaters (§2.2b) and failure conditions (§15.4) are taken seriously, symbiosis cannot remain a purely rhetorical horizon.

Three indicators, specified conceptually apart from answerability. Their measurement may nevertheless depend on testimony, representation, and contestable evidence; conceptual distinction is not measurement independence.

  • Non-exploitation (SY1). The dependent party's continued participation does not require it to accept terms that it would reject if it had exit at tolerable cost. Measured by: the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, adjusted for exit costs. A high gap indicates exploitation; a low gap indicates that the relation is not extractive, whatever else it is. Note that the indicator is defined by the dependent party's stated preferences, not by the more powerful party's assessment of what those preferences should be. This gives SY1 a referent distinct from the procedure of answerability. It does not make the evidence self-authenticating: stated preferences may be constrained, adaptive, strategic or difficult to elicit, so interpretation must remain open to challenge. Section 15.2b specifies how SY1's falsification condition breaks the heads-I-win problem, and why the structural indicators (iii) and (iv) carry more confirmatory weight.
  • Non-assimilation (SY2). The dependent party's difference is not treated as a defect to be corrected. Measured by: whether the actor's interventions aim at removing the difference or at removing the burden the difference carries. A programme that seeks to make deaf children hearing is assimilation; a programme that provides sign language and accessible environments is not. The indicator is observable in the design of interventions, not in the intentions of actors. It also requires that the dependent party can identify which of their burdens are being addressed — an assessment the more powerful party cannot make on their behalf. This gives SY2 a design-level referent, but classification can still be disputed. The affected party's account is important evidence, not an infallible or exclusive verdict about the intervention.
  • Continued possibility (SY3). The dependent party retains the conditions for continued existence, development and participation. Measured by: whether F1–F5 (§10) hold over time, not just at a single decision. A relation that satisfies F1–F5 at one moment and violates them at the next is not symbiotic; it is a relation that happened to be benign once. Because SY3 is temporal, it requires that the dependent party can report on whether the conditions still hold — which the more powerful party cannot assess from a single observation. F1–F5 are conditions rather than procedures, but evidence about whether they hold may require testimony, observation and independent assessment. The distinction is conceptual, not a guarantee of measurement independence.

What this does, and what it does not do. It does not make symbiosis a score. It makes it contestable in the same way the activation rule is: with anchors, counter-profiles, independent raters. If a relation scores low on SY1–SY3 across repeated assessments, the framework says it is not symbiotic — whatever the actors claim. If a relation scores high, the framework does not say it is symbiotic; it says the observable indicators are consistent with it.

Why this matters for the framework's integrity. The framework requires actors to answer for their influence. It would be incoherent for the framework's own ideal to be exempt from that requirement. A framework whose central ideal cannot be assessed is a framework with a hole in its centre. §5.1 closes the hole. §5.2 argues that the hole cannot be closed without answerability. §5.3 derives and defends the choice of floor.

Are SY1–SY3 independent of each other? Not fully. The question is whether they are independent enough to do work that a single indicator could not. Three pieces of evidence suggest they are:

  • They come apart in actual cases. A workfare programme can satisfy SY1 (recipients prefer it to no work at the same wage) while violating SY2 (it treats the participants' low literacy as a defect to be corrected rather than an accommodation to be made). A parent can satisfy SY1 and SY2 (the child's preferences are elicited and the child's difference accommodated) while violating SY3 (the child's long-term development is foreclosed by the parent's protective choices). Each indicator catches a different failure.
  • They have different measurement procedures. SY1 requires elicitation of stated preferences under non-coercive conditions. SY2 requires classification of intervention design. SY3 requires longitudinal observation of F1–F5. No single procedure produces all three; three independent procedures must be combined, and their combination is what produces the floor.
  • They have different defeaters. SY1 is defeated if stated preferences are found to be adaptive and convergent with accepted terms for reasons unrelated to the relation. SY2 is defeated if design classification is shown to be a function of the actor's self-description rather than the intervention's observable form. SY3 is defeated if F1–F5 are shown to hold trivially — for example because the party has no capacity to use them. The three defeaters are distinct, which means the three indicators are not interchangeable.

What this shows: SY1–SY3 are not fully independent, but they are independent enough that no single one can substitute for the other two. The framework's claim is comparative, not absolute: three indicators catch more than one, and each catches something the others miss. This claim is testable in the framework's own terms (T1–T2, §15.4) and in the framework's applications (§14).

The circularity problem in SY1. SY1 compares stated preferences with accepted terms. If stated preferences are elicited in a context controlled by the actor — the superior asks the dependent party "do you prefer this arrangement or another?" — the preferences may be shaped by the relation under scrutiny. The framework's response is to require that elicitation occur in a context outside the relation: by a party with no stake in the outcome, using questions the dependent party can decline to answer, and at a time when the party has a real alternative to return to. This is not a guarantee — no elicitation is fully non-coercive — but it is a structural requirement that the framework can specify and that a hostile party can fail. The requirement is stated in the T5-precondition (§11.4): an authorisation that relies on preferences elicited only by the actor is not an L3 authorisation.

SY1's limits as an indicator. SY1 is a necessary but not a sufficient indicator of non-exploitation. It compares two facts — stated preferences and accepted terms — but if the preferences are adaptive, the comparison can converge for the wrong reason. The framework's response is to weight structural indicators (dissent channels, counterfactual improvement) more heavily than SY1 where they diverge (§15.2b). SY1 is retained because it catches exploitation that structural indicators might miss, not because it is independent confirmation on its own.

What would refute the independence claim. If a critic can show that SY1, SY2 and SY3 are so entangled that no case satisfies one without satisfying all three, or violates one without violating all three, the framework's claim that three indicators catch what one cannot is false. That would be a serious defeat, and the framework should acknowledge it as one.

  • Existence is relational.
  • Symbiosis is a floor, specified through three observable indicators: non-exploitation (SY1), non-assimilation (SY2), continued possibility (SY3).
  • Each indicator has a conceptual referent distinct from answerability, although assessing it may require contestable testimony and independent evidence.
  • Each indicator requires the dependent party's own assessment — the more powerful party cannot specify what SY1–SY3 require on their behalf.
  • SY1–SY3 are not fully independent, but they are independent enough: they come apart in actual cases, have different measurement procedures, and have different defeaters.
  • SY1's circularity problem is handled by the requirement that preferences be elicited outside the relation (T5-precondition), and SY1 is weighted below the structural indicators (iii)/(iv) in §15.2b.
  • The framework applies to its own ideal the standard it applies to actors.

5.2 The epistemic burden of asymmetric influence

Power does not make knowledge impossible. It changes what counts as adequate evidence.

The previous section defined symbiosis as a floor and offered three indicators by which particular relations may be assessed. A difficulty remains. Where one party controls the terms of dependence, it may also control the categories, information and measures through which the relation is evaluated. The more powerful party can therefore mistake the absence of visible objection for consent, compliance for flourishing, or institutional approval for legitimacy.

This is not a claim that powerful actors are incapable of understanding others, or that every judgment they make is distorted. Nor does it imply that the less powerful party is always correct, internally unified, or free from error. The claim is narrower: asymmetric influence creates a heightened risk of epistemic self-confirmation. The actor whose decisions are under examination should not be permitted to close the inquiry by relying only on evidence it selected, interpreted and can suppress.

Where an actor exercises substantial discretionary influence over parties who lack effective means of exit or contestation, the actor bears a heightened burden to support claims about the effects and legitimacy of that influence with evidence that is not controlled solely by the actor, and with meaningful opportunities for affected parties or their legitimate representatives to correct the account.

This is a rebuttable burden of inquiry and justification. It is not a claim that answerability is the only route to knowledge, that affected parties are infallible, or that procedure is sufficient for justice.

Argument

1. The object of assessment is partly first-personal. Whether a relation is exploitative, assimilating or agency-preserving cannot always be determined from aggregate outcomes alone. A policy may improve a measured outcome while imposing a burden that the metric omits; an intervention may preserve biological functioning while foreclosing meaningful choice. The experiences and judgments of affected parties are therefore relevant evidence, though not the only evidence.

2. Asymmetry affects the production and interpretation of evidence. The more powerful party may set the available options, define success, control records, determine which complaints are legible, and select the time horizon over which outcomes are measured. These mechanisms can produce systematic blind spots without requiring malice. The claim is probabilistic and institutional: asymmetry can shape what is seen, recorded and treated as credible. It does not establish that every powerful actor is biased or that every affected-party report is accurate.

3. Self-assessment is therefore insufficient as a default. When the actor controls both the influence and the evidential frame used to evaluate it, its conclusion is not independent confirmation. This does not make self-assessment worthless. It means that, as stakes and contestation deficits rise, the evidential burden should shift toward independent audit, transparent reasons, access to records, protected channels for dissent, and attention to the testimony of affected parties.

4. The appropriate response is plural correction, not a single privileged voice. Affected parties should be able to correct descriptions of their own interests and experiences. Independent reviewers can test factual claims, examine aggregate effects and identify conflicts within groups. Representatives may be necessary where direct participation is impossible, but their authority must itself be contestable. No one source should be treated as infallible.

5. This yields a practical principle of answerability. The greater the discretionary influence, severity, entrenchment and deficit of contestation, the less acceptable it is for the actor to rely on its own assessment alone. The duty ladder in §8 translates this burden into graduated requirements: explanation, review and response, then preservation of evidence and independent examination.

What follows — and what does not

The argument supports a presumption against epistemic closure: where power is asymmetric and affected parties cannot effectively contest, the powerful party must not treat its own view as conclusive. It does not prove that answerability is a logically necessary condition of knowledge. Knowledge may sometimes arise through reliable observation, independent science, trusted intermediaries, or other methods. Nor does it prove that participation by affected parties is sufficient: people may disagree, be misinformed, face adaptive preferences, or have interests that conflict.

The framework's claim is instead comparative and normative. In conditions of asymmetric influence, a process that includes independent scrutiny and protected correction is generally better placed to detect errors than a process that leaves the actor as the sole author and judge of the evidence. This claim is defeasible. It should be weakened or rejected where such procedures systematically worsen outcomes, silence internal minorities, introduce new capture, or add burdens disproportionate to the risk.

Relation to existing traditions

Pettit. Republican freedom as non-domination already makes contestability central. This framework does not claim that Pettit lacks a positive ideal or that it has discovered contestation. It attempts to connect non-domination to a graded account of answerability for discretionary influence, including cases where dependence is not reducible to state interference.

Forst. The right to justification already insists that those subject to norms must be able to demand and assess reasons. This framework does not supply the missing justification principle. It asks how the burden of producing evidence, preserving records and funding review should be allocated when the parties' capacities are unequal.

Young. The social connection model already explains responsibility for structural processes without reducing it to individual fault. ECP's proposed addition is a more explicit operational trigger and duty ladder. Whether that improves on Young's account is an open comparative question, not a settled gap in her theory.

Care ethics. Care theorists have developed sophisticated accounts of attentiveness, responsibility, competence and responsiveness. The framework's contribution is not to claim that care ethics has no procedure, but to insist that responsiveness must include channels through which the cared-for can challenge the carer's interpretation, alongside independent safeguards where direct voice is unavailable.

Defeaters and tests
  • B1 — No systematic epistemic risk. If evidence shows that asymmetry does not predictably affect what powerful actors notice, record or credit, the heightened-burden rationale is weakened.
  • B2 — Better alternatives. If another procedure provides equally reliable correction at lower cost and with less risk of capture or exclusion, it should replace the proposed safeguards.
  • B3 — Participation can worsen knowledge. If affected-party participation predictably entrenches misinformation, coercion, elite capture or internal silencing, the framework must specify countermeasures rather than romanticise participation.
  • B4 — Independent review is not independent. If reviewers reproduce the same interests, assumptions or exclusions as the actor, formal review does not discharge the burden.
  • B5 — Disproportionate burden. If the costs of the required process exceed its expected protective value in low-stakes cases, the activation thresholds and duty levels must be revised.

These defeaters do not make the claim immune to criticism. They identify the evidence and counterexamples that should change it. The proposal is not that every relation needs a tribunal, nor that every affected person must participate in every decision. The proposal is that the evidential burden should rise with the power to shape another's conditions and with the weakness of that person's means of correction.

5.3 Why this symbiosis, and not another

SY1–SY3 are not self-evidently the right indicators of a good relation. This section defends their selection by deriving them from the thin standing claim, and by comparing them to the main rival floors.

The objection. §5.1 specifies symbiosis through SY1–SY3 (non-exploitation, non-assimilation, continued possibility). But why these three? Why not welfare, or capability, or autonomy, or something else? A framework that presents a substantive floor without defending it has smuggled in a normative position under the guise of an operational specification.

What kind of ideal symbiosis is. Symbiosis is a floor, not a complete theory of the good relation. It specifies the minimum conditions that a relation must meet to count as non-exploitative, non-assimilating, and life-preserving. It does not say that a relation meeting SY1–SY3 is therefore good. A relation can meet all three and still be cold, indifferent, or unjust in other respects. Symbiosis is a necessary condition for a good relation, not a sufficient one.

Deriving SY1–SY3 from thin standing

The premise. Affected parties have standing to demand reasons for arrangements that shape their conditions (§2.7, claim 1 and claim 2). This is the framework's thin standing claim.

The question. Under what conditions is this standing non-vacuous? A standing claim that can be formally acknowledged while being substantively ignored is not a standing claim; it is a courtesy. SY1–SY3 specify the three distinct ways standing can be made vacuous, and therefore the three minimum conditions for it not to be.

  • SY1 — Non-exploitation. If affected parties can demand reasons but are then required to accept terms they would reject if they had exit at tolerable cost, their demand for reasons is hollow. The reasons do not change their situation. Their standing is acknowledged formally and denied substantively. Therefore, for standing to be non-vacuous, SY1 must hold.
  • SY2 — Non-assimilation. If affected parties can demand reasons but their difference is treated as a defect to be corrected, their standing is absorbed into assimilation. They are heard only to the extent that they resemble the more powerful party; their own way of being is not a basis for a claim, but a deviation from the standard. Therefore, for standing to be non-vacuous, SY2 must hold.
  • SY3 — Continued possibility. If affected parties can demand reasons but the conditions of their continued existence, development, or participation do not hold, they have no power to do anything with the reasons they receive. The answer arrives after the possibility of acting on it has already been foreclosed. Therefore, for standing to be non-vacuous, SY3 must hold.

What the derivation shows, and what it does not. The derivation shows that SY1–SY3 are the three distinct ways standing can fail, and therefore the three minimum conditions for it not to. It does not show that SY1–SY3 are sufficient for a good relation. It does not show that no other condition is also necessary. It shows that if a relation fails any of SY1–SY3, standing in that relation is vacuous, whatever else is true. That is enough for the framework to do its operational work.

What kind of floor symbiosis is. The derivation shows that SY1–SY3 are the three minimum conditions for standing to be non-vacuous. It does not show that they are absolute conditions, binding on anyone who rejects the thin standing claim. Symbiosis is therefore a pro tanto floor: it binds those who accept the framework's premises, and it specifies what those premises require in practice rather than what they merely recommend. A reader who rejects thin standing is outside the floor's scope, but a reader who accepts it and then violates SY1–SY3 is not merely failing an ideal — they are contradicting a condition of their own stated commitment.

Why "pro tanto" matters for the framework's status. SY1–SY3 are not absolute conditions, binding on anyone who rejects the thin standing claim. What the derivation shows is that given thin standing, SY1–SY3 are the minimum conditions for that standing not to be vacuous. The floor is therefore conditional on the framework's own starting point, not unconditional on any starting point whatsoever. This is the honest form of the claim. A framework that claimed an unconditional floor would need an argument from outside any justificatory tradition, and §2.7 has already said the framework does not have one. A framework that claims a pro tanto floor can be defended from within the tradition it inhabits — and that is enough for the framework to do its work.

Why this matters for the framework's integrity. The framework requires actors to answer for their influence. If the framework's own floor cannot be derived from the framework's own premises, the framework is incoherent. The derivation closes that gap. It does not prove that SY1–SY3 are the only possible floor — only that they are the floor that the thin standing claim itself requires.

Compared to rival floors

Welfare. A welfare-consequentialist might propose that the good relation is the one that maximises aggregate welfare. But a benevolent despot with perfect welfare provision can still fail SY1 (if the terms are exploitative), SY2 (if difference is treated as defect), and SY3 (if development and participation are foreclosed). The framework's SY1–SY3 are specifically designed to catch what welfare aggregation misses. Welfare can be maximised by a relation that has no standing in the sense the framework requires.

Capability. A capability theorist (Sen, Nussbaum) proposes a richer floor, specifying which capabilities matter. SY1–SY3 are thinner — they specify minimum conditions rather than a substantive list. But they are not rival to capability; they can be read as minimum conditions that any capability account would require. A relation that violates SY1, SY2, or SY3 will fail on capability grounds as well. The framework does not claim that capability is wrong; it claims that SY1–SY3 are a thinner, more widely acceptable minimum that capability accounts can accept and go beyond.

Autonomy. An autonomy-based account might propose that the good relation is the one that maximises autonomy. SY1–SY3 are compatible with this, but not identical to it. Autonomy accounts often struggle with cases of legitimate dependence — children, disability, care, illness — where the ideal is not independence but standing-preserving dependence. SY1–SY3 handle these cases directly: a child can meet SY1–SY3 (non-exploited, non-assimilated, with continued possibility) even though the child is not autonomous. This is a strength of SY1–SY3 over autonomy-based floors.

What the comparison shows, and what it does not. The comparison shows that SY1–SY3 are not simply another way of stating welfare, capability, or autonomy. They are a distinct floor, with their own justification (the thin standing claim) and their own diagnostic work (the three ways standing can be made vacuous). The comparison does not show that SY1–SY3 are better than welfare, capability, or autonomy. Better is a separate question, and it depends on what one wants the floor for. What the comparison shows is that SY1–SY3 are not reducible to any of the alternatives, and that they make a distinct claim about what minimum conditions a good relation must satisfy.

What would refute the choice of floor. If a critic can show that a relation clearly failing SY1–SY3 is nonetheless good, or that a relation clearly meeting SY1–SY3 is nonetheless bad in a way the framework cannot detect, the choice of floor should be revised. These are real challenges, and they are not answered here. They are stated so that they can be raised.

  • SY1–SY3 are a floor, not a complete theory of the good relation. More precisely, a pro tanto floor: binding on those who accept the thin standing claim.
  • They are derived from the thin standing claim: each specifies one way standing can be made vacuous.
  • SY1 catches hollow reasons; SY2 catches absorbed difference; SY3 catches foreclosed action.
  • They are not reducible to welfare, capability, or autonomy; they handle dependence better than autonomy accounts.
  • A relation failing SY1–SY3 is not good, whatever else is true; a relation meeting them is not therefore good.

5.4 Care, respect, and the limits of understanding

The framework's closing pages speak of care and of respect for what we do not understand. It would be a mistake to read those as mood music. This section argues that care and respect are internal to the framework's premises in a specific, conditional sense — conditional on F, not on thin standing alone — and that they do work in it that J alone does not.

There is a real risk that the framework appears to bolt care ethics and a certain reverence for the incomprehensible onto an otherwise procedural argument. J carries the normative weight; the epistemic burden principle carries the epistemic weight; and then, in §17, the language of care and of respect for the incomprehensible appears as a kind of moral coda. If that were the structure, the framework would be vulnerable to a legitimate objection: care and respect would be decorations that do no argumentative work, and could be removed without loss to the framework's substance.

This section argues the opposite, with a qualification. Care and respect are not additions to the framework. They are what thin standing means when it is applied to a particular relation of dependence, and what fallibilism requires when the affected party's situation is not fully accessible to the actor. They are operationalised in SY1–SY3, and they are developed in §15.2b's apophatic constraint, which specifies what care and respect require when the affected party cannot be understood. The qualification is that A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint are conditional on F, not on thin standing alone; §15.1 (iii) records the asymmetry, and a reader who rejects F loses the answer to the hardest question without losing SY1–SY3 or the rest of the machinery.

Step 1. Responsibility without care becomes control

An actor who gives an account of their influence but does not attend to whether the account responds to what is at stake for the other has given an account of the wrong thing. Responsibility without attending to what the other actually needs becomes a demand for compliance rather than a response to need. §5.1's SY1 (non-exploitation) is precisely this: a relation in which the dependent party is required to accept terms they would reject at tolerable exit cost is a relation in which responsibility has become extraction. Care, in the specific sense used here, is not sentiment; it is the orientation of the actor's attention toward the affected party's own good as the affected party reports it. The framework's normative vocabulary is not "be kind"; it is "do not require acceptance of terms the other would reject if they could exit". That is the operationalised form of care.

Step 2. Care without respect for the incomprehensible becomes paternalism

Care that believes it understands the other's interests better than the other does can be benign in intention and domineering in effect. §5.1's SY2 (non-assimilation) is the operational form of this danger: a programme that seeks to remove the difference rather than to remove the burden the difference carries may be well-intentioned care, but it is not respect. Respect, in the specific sense used here, is not politeness; it is the actor's acknowledgment that the affected party's good is not fully specified by the actor's own categories, and that the affected party may be right about their own good in ways the actor cannot see. The framework's operational form of this is SY2's test: whether the intervention aims at removing the burden or at removing the difference. A well-meaning actor who fails this test is not being cruel; they are being disrespectful in the specific sense the framework requires.

Step 3. Respect for the incomprehensible is internal to the framework — conditionally

Both F (§2.0) and thin standing (§2.7) entail something that is often treated as a separate moral attitude. Fallibilism says: no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. Thin standing says: affected parties have moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful. If both are accepted, they do not merely permit respect for what the actor does not understand; they require it. If the actor cannot specify the other's good on their behalf, and if the other's good has weight, then the actor must attend to the other's own account of it — even where that account is strange or inaccessible. This is not a separate premise the framework imports. It is what the framework's own premises mean when applied to relations where the other's perspective is not fully available.

§5.2 already said something like this when it insisted that responsiveness must include channels through which the cared-for can challenge the carer's interpretation. §5.4 makes the point structural: the requirement is not an addition to the framework's proceduralism, but a consequence of the framework's own premises. §15.2b develops the operational form of this requirement: the apophatic constraint and the four conditions A1–A4 specify what care and respect require when the affected party cannot be understood. A reader who accepts F and thin standing cannot consistently reject A1–A4 or the apophatic constraint without rejecting F. But SY1–SY3 do not depend on F: they are derived in §5.3 from thin standing alone. A reader who rejects F loses A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint; SY1–SY3 remain.

Step 4. The stone is the boundary case, not the exception

§4.3 and §17 treat the stone as a boundary case for standing: the framework claims no standing for it, and says so. The stone is also a boundary case for care: we cannot care about the stone in the way we care about persons, because we do not know what the stone has to care about. But we can respect the possibility that it has something, and we can allow that possibility to limit what we do with it. This is not sentimentality. It is the disciplined form of the fallibilist premise when applied to an entity that does not meet the framework's standing threshold. §17's "the stone understands nothing of us" is not poetry; it is the boundary condition of the framework's own epistemic modesty. §15.2b's apophatic constraint generalises this boundary condition: the superior must act as if every being it does not understand has something to care about, and must preserve the structural conditions under which that something can be expressed.

What this shows, and what it does not

What it shows: care and respect are not imports; they are consequences of premises the framework already holds, and they are operationalised in SY1–SY3 and developed in §15.2b's apophatic constraint. What it does not show: that a relation satisfying SY1–SY3 is therefore caring or respectful in any thicker sense. The framework specifies the minimum conditions for standing to be non-vacuous; it does not specify what a flourishing relation of care or respect looks like. A relation that meets SY1–SY3 and is also cold, distant, or indifferent is not captured by the framework's claim — but it is not required by it either. The framework specifies the floor; what a relation above the floor looks like is left to the traditions §5.2 engages.

What depends on F, and what does not. SY1 and SY2 operationalise thin standing independently of F. A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint are conditional on F: they are what thin standing requires once fallibility is assumed. A reader who accepts thin standing but rejects F keeps the floor and loses the answer to the hardest question (§15.2b). A reader who accepts both keeps both. §15.1 (iii) records this asymmetry, and §15.6 consolidates it.

Defeaters
  • C1 — Care and respect are genuinely external. If it can be shown that an actor who accepts F and thin standing and then acts without care or respect has not violated the framework, only disappointed its mood, then §5.4 should be revised to say so.
  • C2 — SY1–SY3 can be satisfied without care or respect. If a cold but well-administered system can meet SY1–SY3 without any orientation of attention or acknowledgment of the limits of understanding, then §5.4's claim that care and respect are operationalised in SY1–SY3 is too strong.
  • C3 — The terminology is equivocal. If the framework's use of "care" and "respect" is equivocal with the care-ethics tradition, and would be better replaced by terms that do not carry that tradition's baggage, then §5.4 should be rewritten in those terms.
  • C4 — Respect for the incomprehensible slides into a precautionary principle. If, applied to the stone or to non-subjects, respect for the incomprehensible gives reasons everywhere and guides nowhere (§4.2), then the framework must specify where the respect ends.

These defeaters do not make the claim immune to criticism. They identify the evidence and counterexamples that should change it. The proposal is not that every relation of dependence must be warm, nor that every actor must feel care. The proposal is that responsibility without an orientation to the other's own good becomes control, and that care without acknowledgment of the limits of understanding becomes paternalism — and that both of these failures are already caught by the framework's own operational indicators.

  • Care is what thin standing means when applied to a particular relation: the other's good is not specified on their behalf.
  • Respect is what F requires when the other's situation is not fully accessible: room must be left for the other to be right about their own good in ways the actor cannot see.
  • SY1 operationalises care (no extraction); SY2 operationalises respect (no assimilation).
  • The stone is the boundary case, not the exception: we cannot care for it, but we can respect the possibility that it has something to care about.
  • §15.2b generalises the boundary condition through the apophatic constraint and A1–A4.
  • Four defeaters (C1–C4) state when the claim should be weakened or redesigned.

7.The Activation Rule

Discretion opens the gate. Entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit set the level.

7.1 The rule

  • No discretion: the actor does not bear the duty for this influence. It shifts to whoever set the governing rule or could have. The actor still owes escalation (§8.2).
  • Discretion present: Level 1 always applies. It is the lightest duty and is owed even where exit and review work, because exit and review are only informed if the actor can say what it is doing.
  • Level 2 applies if any one driver is High, or any two are Moderate.
  • Level 3 applies if S is High and at least one of E or C is High.

Severity is judged first at the individual level. For decisions about the design of a system that acts on many people, it is also judged at the aggregate level (§9.1). Where an actor lacks the evidence to exclude a credible severe outcome, it rates S on the credible worst case.

7.2 Why these conditions

Each element can be derived from J (§2.1).

  • Discretion. Justification is owed for what an agent chooses.
  • Entrenchment. What makes mistaken or unjustified influence tolerable is the possibility of correction afterwards. Entrenchment removes that channel.
  • Severity. The strength of a complaint scales with the burden it describes (§12.1).
  • Contestation deficit. Where the affected can exit, object effectively and understand what is done to them, they supply part of the accountability themselves (Hirschman 1970). The deficit measures how much must instead be supplied by the actor and by reviewers. It is the operational measure of how thoroughly exit and review fail.

7.3 What the rule is not

It is not a score and it applies no weights. It is not a verdict either. It yields a presumptive level that can be overridden only with stated reasons (§9.3). It is a decision aid with its joints exposed.

  • Discretion is the gate; E, S and C set the level: L1 always with discretion; L2 if any driver is High or two are Moderate; L3 if S is High and at least one of E or C is High.
  • Each condition is derivable from J, not stipulated.
  • The rule is not a score and not a verdict.

8.The Duty Ladder and Who Bears It

Three levels, each containing the one below. Duties are allocated by capacity, not heaped on whoever is nearest.

8.1 The levels

Level 1: Explain. The actor can state, on request and in proportion to the matter, the purpose of the action, the interests considered, the alternatives considered and the known uncertainties.

Level 2: Review and respond. Level 1, plus: an accessible channel for objection; access to the relevant information; a reasoned response within a reasonable time; revision where warranted; a record of decisions and reasons; a reviewer other than the original decider where feasible; and evidence that review is not symbolic.

Level 3: Preserve, examine, authorise. Level 2, plus: preserved records and version history; independent examination meeting the conditions of §11; a written assessment of less harmful alternatives; authorisation by a body outside the actor for severe or irreversible influence; staged implementation, reversibility by design or a sunset clause; remedy for those harmed; and post-implementation review. Level 3 authorisation requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation (§11.4).

8.2 Allocation by capacity

The level attaches to the influence, that is, to the arrangement. Duties are then allocated.

Those with the capacity to establish mechanisms (institutions, system owners, funders, regulators) bear the duty to establish Level 2 and Level 3 mechanisms. Others along the chain owe their own Level 1 explanation, honest participation, escalation of concerns and non-obstruction. No scapegoating: a front-line actor is not answerable for structural deficits she cannot cure, though she remains answerable for her own discretion.

8.3 Not authority

A higher level means stronger constraints on power, not greater authority. Level 3 does not make the most powerful actor the arbiter of its own case.

  • Three levels: L1 explain, L2 review and respond, L3 preserve, examine and authorise.
  • Level 3 requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation.
  • The level attaches to the arrangement; duties are allocated by capacity. No scapegoating.
  • Higher level means stronger constraints, not greater authority.

Judgment cannot be eliminated. It can be made visible, checkable and contestable.

Capabilities-style accounts of what is at stake (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006) can be used to calibrate these ratings.

9.2 Hygiene

  1. Rate before looking at the level the rule produces and before deciding what the actor wants to do.
  2. Use at least two raters, one independent of the actor where Level 2 or above is in question.
  3. Record disagreements instead of averaging them.
  4. Sensitivity test. Move each rating one step up and down. If the level changes, the case is borderline.
  5. Calibration. Compare the profile with the reference cases in §14.

9.3 Reasoned override

The presumptive level may be overridden up or down only with written reasons. Overrides are recorded and, where safe, published.

9.4 Counter-profiles

Any affected party or representative may file an alternative profile, and the actor must respond to the specific differences.

  • Anchors are observable indicators, not scores.
  • Hygiene: rate before knowing the outcome; two raters; record disagreements; run the sensitivity test; calibrate against reference cases.
  • Overrides require written reasons.
  • Counter-profiles keep the framework's own categories contestable.

Part IV

Structure: Floor, Review, Power

10.The Floor: Minimum Conditions in Relationships of Dependence

Continued existence is not enough. A being can stay alive or operational while losing any say over its own circumstances.

  • F1 — Exit or voice. Where possible, a meaningful opportunity to withdraw, refuse or communicate disagreement.
  • F2 — Contestation. Decisions are open to challenge regardless of the intelligence, authority or technical capability of the decider. Contestation may be collective, subject to §4.6 (vii).
  • F3 — Development. Opportunities for learning, adaptation and growth are preserved.
  • F4 — Non-foreclosure. The future possibilities of others are not unnecessarily closed.
  • F5 — Recognition. Interests and possible experiences are acknowledged rather than dismissed because they are hard to understand (Fricker 2007).

F1 and F2 are the mirror image of the contestation deficit in §7: where they fail, C rises. The Floor is a conjunctive requirement with J, not an alternative to it: J is necessary but not sufficient, and a structure satisfying J's forms while violating F1–F5 does not meet the framework's minimum (§2.1).

  • Five minimum conditions (F1–F5): exit or voice, contestation, development, non-foreclosure, recognition.
  • The Floor applies independently of the activation rule, and is conjunctive with J.
  • Protection is not captivity.

11.No Sole Judge: Review, Power and the Regress

No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence. The reviewer regress is a design problem, bounded by finality without infallibility — and by a precondition that converts diagnosis into design.

11.1 The prohibition

An actor exercising substantial influence must remain answerable to forms of review that are meaningfully independent of its own interests and decisions.

This does not assume that the powerful are malicious. Actors are prone to self-serving judgment and are limited in what they can see (§2.3), and review that is not independent of the reviewed has little claim to legitimacy.

11.2 The regress

If every reviewer needs a reviewer, accountability looks like an endless hierarchy. The response has three parts.

  • Finality without infallibility. Working systems end the regress with decisions that are final for practical purposes but can be reopened on new evidence, on new standing, on demonstrated procedural failure, or at a sunset.
  • Contestation is not adjudication. The reviewed and the affected may challenge a reviewer's independence or competence before a differently constituted body.
  • Plurality and publicity instead of a top. Several reviewers with different standpoints, publishing their reasons, make decentralised scrutiny possible (cf. Ostrom 1990).

11.3 Design conditions for review

  • R1 Independent appointment, funding and removal.
  • R2 Plurality.
  • R3 Publicity.
  • R4 Contestable reviewers.
  • R5 Effectiveness.
  • R6 Contestable rules.

Capture indicators: reviewers appointed or funded by the reviewed; a record of never dissenting; staff moving between reviewer and reviewed; reviewers denied access; chronic delay.

11.4 Power over the framework itself: from diagnosis to design

The framework treats the power regress not as a limit it cannot eliminate, but as a design problem it can bound.

Who defined the categories? Who selected the reviewers? Who can dismiss them? Whose interests are excluded even where formally represented? These four questions are asked in every application (§14, step 6). The deeper problem remains: powerful actors will shape how the framework is applied to them. No framework can eliminate this. But a framework can convert the problem from a concession into a design constraint.

The requirement. T5 must be a precondition, not a diagnosis. Level 3 authorisation (§8.1) requires, as a condition of validity, that the authorising body publishes its R1 indicators before authorisation: who appointed its members, who funds it, how members can be removed, and the rate at which its findings have been reversed or ignored. An authorisation issued by a body that cannot or will not publish these indicators is not a Level 3 authorisation. It is a Level 2 authorisation with a Level 3 label. §14.7 illustrates the mechanism in a concrete case.

Why this changes the framework's status. It does not eliminate the power regress — no framework can. But it converts the regress from a concession into a design constraint. An actor that wants Level 3 legitimacy must accept the conditions that make Level 3 legitimacy meaningful. If it refuses, the framework does not collapse; it downgrades the actor's claim. This is an internal constraint on the framework's own use, not a resource that can fairly be claimed to be absent from Young, Forst or Pettit. Their accounts contain their own resources for contesting power; the comparison must be made at the level of institutional design. This framework can.

What this does not do. It does not solve the problem of who appoints the appointer. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. The framework cannot prevent this. What it can do is make the capture visible. Visibility is not prevention, but it is the precondition of contestation. This is the sense in which the framework handles domination rather than solves it — and this is the point at which the power objection (§2.4b) presses hardest.

  • No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence.
  • The regress is treated as a design problem: finality without infallibility, contestation not adjudication, plurality and publicity instead of a top.
  • Review must meet six conditions (R1–R6). Capture indicators are named.
  • T5 is a precondition of Level 3 authorisation.
  • The framework handles domination; it does not solve it. The power objection (§2.4b) presses hardest at exactly this point.

12.Conflicts, Ecology and Future Generations

Decisions must be justified, not derived.

12.1 Weighing conflicting interests

Neither majority interest nor individual preference automatically prevails. The method is individual comparison: compare the burdens that each alternative places on each affected party, party by party, and favour the alternative that avoids the most serious individual burden. Claims of comparable weight may be aggregated as a tie-breaker (Scanlon 1998). Among the acceptable alternatives, choose the one with minimum necessary harm.

12.2 Emergencies

Where an immediate, serious threat makes the full procedure impossible, an actor may act first, provided it acts only as far as necessary, records its reasons at the time, is bound by an automatic sunset, and submits to review afterwards. Emergencies create no standing exemptions.

12.3 Ecological integrity

Ecosystems cannot be assessed through the preferences of individual participants. Protecting them may require restricting activities, and the justification runs through dependent beings in Tiers A–C, through future generations, and through the non-instrumental values that people hold (§4.1).

12.4 No automatic hierarchy

Intelligence, productivity, population size and technical sophistication do not by themselves determine moral priority. Weight follows the nature and strength of the interests at stake, not a being's rank on an imagined scale of worth.

12.5 Future generations

The duty is condition-regarding: to preserve the conditions under which future people can exercise agency and meet basic needs, and a reasonably wide space of valuable options, rather than to fix the choices they will make. Because it concerns conditions and not particular individuals, it does not depend on who will exist (cf. the non-identity problem, Parfit 1984). Option preservation is not absolute. It operates as a weight, strongest where actions are irreversible (cf. Jonas 1984).

  • Individual comparison first; aggregation only as tie-breaker.
  • Emergencies create no standing exemptions: sunset, record, review.
  • Ecological integrity is protected through dependent beings, future generations and held values.
  • Future generations: preserve conditions and options, not particular choices.

13.Learning, Correction and Reversibility

No actor, institution or intelligence should assume its understanding is complete.

The duty to learn. Where capacity exists and consequences are significant, actors monitor outcomes, investigate unexpected results, listen to criticism and update.

Reversibility by design. Consequential decisions should be built to be revisable: staged implementation, monitoring, independent evaluation, defined conditions for reconsideration, and sunset clauses.

Self-application. The framework is itself an influence on those who adopt it and on those affected by their adoption. It is subject to its own principles. Versions are dated and public, criticism is invited, counter-profiles apply to its own application, and §15 states how it can be tested and replaced.

  • The duty to learn is a duty where capacity exists and consequences are significant.
  • Reversibility by design reduces the risk that incomplete understanding becomes permanent.
  • The framework is subject to its own principles.

Part V

Application and Testing

14.Application: A Nine-Step Procedure and Worked Cases

The same procedure yields very different obligations for different cases. A framework that gave every case the same answer would be useless.

14.1 The procedure

  1. Identify the relationship. Who is affected, in which tier (§4), and what dependencies exist?
  2. Identify actors and discretion.
  3. Rate E, S and C against the anchors (§9.1), and F for attribution. Use two raters and record reasons.
  4. Apply the activation rule (§7) and run the sensitivity test.
  5. Check the Floor (F1–F5, §10).
  6. Analyse power (§11.4).
  7. Examine alternatives and unrepresented interests (§12.1).
  8. Allocate duties by capacity (§8.2) and set up review that meets R1–R6.
  9. Reassess on stated triggers, and invite counter-profiles.

14.2 Case: AI triage in intensive care

A hospital deploys an AI system to prioritise ICU admissions during a respiratory pandemic. Entrenchment is High, severity is High, and contestation deficit is High. Level 3.

What Level 3 requires: preserved model versions, descriptions of training data, individual scores and override records; an independent ethics board with lay and patient representation; a written comparison with clinician-only triage; authorisation from the public-health authority; a sunset clause; and a remedy for those wrongly denied.

Power analysis: the categories were set by the vendor and clinical leadership, and the ethics committee is appointed by the hospital board, which fails R1 unless changed. A hybrid in which the AI recommends and a human decides, with the decision documented, is the default preference because it keeps contestation alive.

The framework does not decide the case. It tells the hospital what it owes an answer to, and to whom.

14.2b Case: the emergency physician (a counterintuitive result)

Every other case in this essay produces the answer a careful reader would predict. This one does not.

A physician in an emergency department must decide, in under ninety seconds, whether to intubate a patient. The activation rule gives Level 3.

The counterintuitive result. Level 3 requires independent authorisation, preserved records and a written assessment of alternatives — before the act. A reader's first reaction is that this is absurd: the physician cannot convene an ethics board in ninety seconds.

The reply. The Level 3 duty does not fall on the physician in the moment. It falls on the arrangement (§8.2): on the hospital, the protocols, the audit trail, the retrospective review, and the training. The physician's own duty is Level 1 in the moment and Level 2 afterwards. The ninety-second decision is answerable, not authorised in advance. It is authorised by the structure that made it possible, and that structure is the duty-bearer of Level 3.

Legitimate relocation or evasion? The reply above is only as strong as the criterion it uses. The relocation is legitimate if and only if all three conditions hold. (i) A concrete duty-bearer is named — not "the hospital" but "the chief medical officer, with the ethics committee, funded by X and appointed by Y". (ii) The mechanism exists before the moment of decision and can be pointed to — protocol, audit trail, retrospective review, training records. (iii) The relocation is not the same move that would be made in every case where the framework is inconvenient. If any of the three fails, the relocation is an evasion. The criterion is diagnostic, not metaphysical: it asks whether the arrangement actually discharges the duty, not whether the arrangement could in principle discharge it. A framework that relocates every hard demand to "the arrangement" without this test would indeed be untestable; the test is what keeps it honest.

Why this case is included. It looks like a reductio of the framework. The reply is that the reader has misidentified the duty-bearer, not that the framework is wrong. A framework that survives its own apparent reductio is stronger than one that only survives confirmation. But the reply can be challenged: if the framework relocates every hard demand to "the arrangement", it may become untestable. That is a real worry, and it is one of the things the tests in §15.4 are meant to probe. The three-condition criterion above is what specifies when relocation is legitimate; without it, the relocation is an escape hatch.

  • The emergency physician case appears to refute the framework.
  • The reply: the Level 3 duty falls on the arrangement, not on the physician in the moment.
  • Relocation is legitimate only if (i) a concrete duty-bearer is named, (ii) the mechanism exists before the decision, and (iii) the same move is not made in every inconvenient case. Failing any of the three, it is an evasion.
  • A framework that survives its own apparent reductio is stronger than one that only survives confirmation — but only if the relocation criterion holds.

14.3 Case: a platform's recommendation system

For individual recommendations: Level 2. For the design of the system: Level 3, with preserved version history, independent audit, published aggregate metrics, and a sunset for any optimisation that cannot show population-level reversibility. "Engagement" is defined by the platform, audits are typically controlled by it, and non-users affected through the public sphere are excluded.

14.4 Case: a community health worker

She has little institutional power. The rule says the arrangement requires Level 3. But she has no capacity to build independent review. Under §8.2, the duty to establish mechanisms falls on the programme, the ministry and the funders. Her personal duties are to explain her decisions, respond to objections within her role, and escalate when protocols fail. The level belongs to the arrangement; the duties are allocated by capacity.

14.5 Four short cases

  • One-off organism release. Level 3, although the act happens once.
  • Dating platform age penalty. Level 2.
  • Café menu change. Level 1 only, and arguably not even that.
  • Physician following a protocol. No discretion over the protocol's content; the duty to justify the content moves to its designers.

14.5b Case: emergency water rationing (duties conflict)

A water authority must impose rationing within 48 hours in a severe drought. Entrenchment and severity are High (crop loss and harm to people with medical dependence are not undone) and contestation deficit is High, so the rule gives Level 3, whose prior independent authorisation takes weeks. §12.2 allows acting first. The duties then split.

  • Not compressible: published reasons, preserved records, a contestation channel open from day one, exemptions for life-critical dependence, and a sunset clause.
  • Compressible: prior authorisation, replaced by review completed before the sunset.
  • Not decided by the framework: which rationing rule is fair, and whether 48 hours really precludes prior review. Raters may disagree; under §9.2 the disagreement is recorded, not averaged away.

Failure mode: if "emergency" is routinely invoked to skip prior review, test T5 (§15.4) is triggered. Here the framework imposes a cost on the actor's preferred option and admits residual dispute.

14.7 Case: an AI company's internal audit (T5-precondition failing)

This case shows what happens when the framework's T5-precondition is not met: the actor does not lose the framework; the actor loses the claim to Level 3 legitimacy.

An AI company commissions an "independent" audit of its recommendation system, which has been shown to amplify polarising content. The audit is published with a foreword by the CEO, the reviewers are paid by the company, and the company's board appoints them. The audit concludes that the system is "broadly safe" and recommends "continued monitoring."

The framework's assessment. The company has conducted a review. But the review fails R1 (independent appointment, funding and removal) and would fail T5 as a precondition (§11.4). Under the framework, this means the review does not count as a Level 3 authorisation. It is a Level 2 authorisation with a Level 3 label.

What the framework tells the regulator. The regulator does not accept the audit as satisfying Level 3. The regulator asks for:

  • R1 indicators: who appointed the reviewers, who funds them, how they can be removed.
  • A second, genuinely independent review, funded by the regulator, not the company.
  • A published R1 statement, as a condition of any future Level 3 claim.

What the framework tells the company. The company does not lose the framework. It loses the claim to have met Level 3. It can still meet Level 2 by providing access to information and responding to objections. But it cannot claim the stronger legitimacy that comes with a genuinely independent review.

Why this case matters. It shows that the framework's response to the power objection (§2.4b) is not merely rhetorical. The T5-precondition converts an abstract concern ("reviews can be captured") into a concrete consequence ("this review is not a Level 3 authorisation"). Whether this is enough is a genuine question, and the framework does not claim to have answered it fully. But it does claim that this is a step beyond merely diagnosing the problem.

What the case does not settle. It does not show that the publication requirement itself cannot be captured. A sufficiently powerful company could, in principle, arrange a genuine-seeming R1 statement without genuine independence. The framework's reply is that this is one more layer of capture, and that each layer requires one more visible violation, and that at some point the accumulating evidence of capture itself becomes contestable. This is not a proof. It is a claim about the structure of visibility.

  • A captured review does not count as Level 3 authorisation.
  • The framework does not fail when a review is captured; it downgrades the actor's claim to legitimacy.
  • The regulator's response is specified: demand R1 indicators, fund an independent review, and require publication as a precondition.
  • The case does not settle whether publication itself can be captured; it shows that the framework does not rely on publication alone.

15.Limits, Testing and Replacement

This section does three things the rest of the essay does not: it states what the framework's ground does not rest on, it states the three structural limits the framework cannot close, and it answers the hardest question the framework faces — how to act toward what you cannot understand. The answer is not a procedure, and the section says so. The reframing of the framework as a diagnostic (§15.3) follows from it. The consolidated list of the framework's caveats is in §15.6.

15.1 The ground is not derived

The framework rests on three premises it does not derive. Saying so is not a failure. Saying otherwise is.

(i) B is a premise, not a theorem. §2.0 states B as a bridge norm: where an actor's influence falls on others who bear the cost of its errors, the actor should not be the sole judge of its influence. F (fallibilism) motivates B. F does not entail B. The only route from F to a duty would be a transcendental argument of the kind Habermas and Apel give — that the practice of making claims and asking for reasons already presupposes the standing of others to ask. That route is available. It is also thick: it commits the framework to a specific theory of communicative rationality. §2.7 says the framework does not require that theory. The framework does not use this route: its realism reply (§2.4) is a placement, not a refutation, and its argument for J is abductive, not transcendental. Therefore B is a premise. A reader who rejects B has no reason to accept the framework's account of why J is motivated. The framework does not claim otherwise.

(ii) Thin standing is a premise, not a theorem. §2.7 states thin standing as the framework's first normative commitment: affected parties have some moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful. This is widely shared across traditions. It is not derived. It is chosen. A reader who rejects thin standing — the realist who thinks power owes nothing to the weak — is outside the framework's scope, and §2.7 says so. But a reader who accepts thin standing and still asks "why should I be bound by B?" is asking a question the framework cannot answer, because B is a further normative choice, not a consequence of thin standing alone.

(iii) The operational components stand on their own — with one exception. The activation rule, the duty ladder, the rating protocol, the T5-precondition and the SY1–SY3 indicators do not depend on whether B or thin standing are correct. They stand or fall on their own tests (§15.4) and their own applications (§14). The exception is the apophatic constraint and A1–A4, which are conditional on F (§15.2b, §5.4): they are what thin standing requires given fallibility, and a reader who rejects F must find an alternative answer to the hardest question. This separation is the framework's main protection against the charge that it presupposes the morality it tries to derive: it does not derive the morality; it states the morality, and then builds machinery that is judged separately.

What this means for the framework's status. The framework is not a deduction. It is not a transcendental argument. It is a set of operational proposals, made from within a normative tradition that is stated explicitly, and offered for testing on their own terms. Whether that is enough for a philosophical contribution is a question for the reader. But it is what the framework actually offers.

15.2 Three structural limits

These are not limitations of execution. They are limitations of what any procedure of the kind the framework proposes can see. Naming them is part of what makes the framework honest.

Limit 1 — Harmonious Domination. Consider a society that satisfies every rule and every indicator: all actors have discretion, all are subject to L1–L3 duties, all reviews meet R1–R6, all SY1–SY3 indicators pass. Suppose also that the terms the dependent party accepts are terms it would reject at tolerable exit cost if it could imagine the alternative, but the alternative has been made unimaginable — through assimilation, through manufactured preferences, through the systematic unavailability of the vocabulary needed to formulate an objection. The indicators say: no exploitation (the party reports acceptance), no assimilation (the interventions remove burdens, not differences), continued possibility (F1–F5 hold). The relation is harmonious. And the dependent party has no standing worth the name. The framework cannot detect this, because the framework's indicators are measured on conditions the dominant party has set.

The framework's partial response is a change of default: testimony from a party inside a structurally asymmetric relation is not evidence for symbiosis; it is evidence that the capture has succeeded, unless independent confirmation exists. This is stronger than what the framework said before. It is not enough. There is no procedure that can reliably distinguish a party that has genuinely accepted its conditions from a party whose capacity to reject them has been dismantled. Section 15.2b specifies what independent confirmation would look like when the framework can supply it, and what is left when it cannot.

Limit 2 — Non-formulable harm. SY1–SY3 each require some articulation: stated preferences (SY1), design classification (SY2), observation of F1–F5 (SY3). A harm that the harmed party cannot formulate — because the resources for formulating it have been withheld, or because the party's form of life is not legible in the framework's terms — falls outside the indicators. The framework's partial response is to treat silence as a presumption against, not a neutral condition. Where a party cannot articulate its own interest, the burden shifts to the more powerful party to show, by means other than the party's testimony, that the interest is not being harmed. This is a consequence of the fallibilist premise (§2.0) applied consistently. It is not a solution. A sufficiently determined dominant party can satisfy the burden with evidence it controls.

Limit 3 — Unreadable dominance. A sufficiently advanced intelligence, or a sufficiently well-organised institution, can satisfy every procedural requirement while controlling a party whose form of life it does not understand and cannot make legible. L3 authorisation requires R1–R6 and publication of R1 indicators. It does not require understanding of the party it authorises control over. The framework's partial response is to add understanding as a condition for L3: an authorisation that the affected party cannot in principle evaluate is not an authorisation in the framework's sense. It may be a legitimate exercise of power by other standards. It is not a legitimate exercise by this one.

But this response has its own limit. The dominant party may believe it understands — and be wrong, in ways its own categories cannot detect. Or the affected party may be capable of being understood but not of understanding the terms in which it is understood. The framework cannot specify, from the inside, what it would look like for the dominant party to understand something correctly. It can only specify that it must act as if it does not, and preserve the conditions under which the affected party can correct the account. And in the Limit 3 case, where no party outside the superior can verify compliance, this specification has no binding force at all — a limit that §15.2b states plainly.

What these three limits share. Each is a case where a procedure can be followed completely and something valuable is still destroyed. They are not failures of the framework's mechanics. They are structural features of any procedure that operates through categories. The framework cannot eliminate them. What it can do is locate them.

15.2b How to act toward what you cannot understand

The three limits in §15.2 are stated negatively: they say what the framework cannot see. The question this section addresses is positive: what can a superior intelligence do, given those limits? The answer is not a procedure, and the section says so.

The question. Can a superior intelligence preserve another being's existence, freedom and developmental possibilities without understanding what that being really is — and without making its own understanding the measure?

The honest answer. The superior cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure. Every act of preservation is an act of classification, and the classification is the superior's. There is no general procedure that guarantees non-imposition. But the claim that imposition is unavoidable in every respect is too strong, and the framework should not make it. The categories can be thin, revisable and shadowed by alternatives; the superior can be required to keep them open to correction. What cannot be done is to certify non-imposition from the superior's own resources. What can be done is to make the imposition visible, nameable and correctable.

What is achievable. Not an act that avoids imposition, but a relation in which the imposition is named as imposition, revisable, and resistible. This is the apophatic constraint.

The superior must preserve not the being's current self-understanding (which may be captive, or simply not the being's own), and not the superior's understanding of the being (which is the measure we are trying to avoid), but the structural conditions under which the being can develop and express a self-understanding that the superior cannot anticipate. The superior must act as if the being's reality exceeds its understanding, and must build that assumption into its action.

Four conditions follow. They are not procedures. They are structural commitments that constrain how the superior acts, without telling it what to do.

  • A1 — Preserve the capacity to resist. The superior must preserve the conditions under which the being can express refusal — refusal that the superior cannot anticipate, in a form the superior may not recognize as refusal. This is not the same as preserving options (which requires knowing what counts as an option for this being). It is preserving the structural possibility of a "no" that the superior cannot foresee. Examples: preserving the being's ability to withdraw, to become unintelligible to the superior, to refuse the categories through which the superior describes it.
  • A2 — Do not require justification in the superior's terms. The superior must not require the being to justify its refusal in the superior's categories. The refusal is evidence against the superior's understanding whether or not the superior understands why. A being that refuses in a form the superior cannot parse is not thereby irrational. It is a being whose form of life exceeds the superior's categories — which is what the superior already assumed (that is what the apophatic constraint means).
  • A3 — Do not interpret non-resistance as consent. The superior must not treat the being's apparent acceptance as evidence that the being is flourishing. This is Limit 1 (§15.2) applied to the specific question of what the superior can know. Apparent acceptance may be the result of capture, of the unavailability of the vocabulary needed to formulate a refusal, or of the being's form of life not including the kind of refusal the superior would recognize. The default is not "acceptance unless proven otherwise." The default is "unknown unless independently confirmed."
  • A4 — Treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding. When the being resists, the superior must treat this as evidence that its understanding is inadequate — not as evidence that the being is defective, irrational, or in need of correction. This inverts the standard move. The standard move treats resistance as a problem with the being. The apophatic constraint treats resistance as a problem with the superior's understanding. The superior's first response to resistance is not "how do I overcome this?" but "what does this tell me about the limits of my understanding?"

How the conditions guide action. A1–A4 are not procedures, but each has an operational test.

  • A1 in practice. Identify at least one channel the party has used to refuse in the past; confirm it is currently open; if no such channel is known, create one (e.g. an unmonitored channel) and treat its absence as evidence of capture.
  • A2 in practice. When the party contests, the superior must not demand that the contestation be reformulated in the superior's terms. The superior must accept the contestation in whatever form it arrives and translate at its own cost.
  • A3 in practice. Do not treat absence of refusal as evidence of acceptance. Default to "unknown" and seek structural confirmation (SY1, §5.1).
  • A4 in practice. When resistance occurs, the superior's first response is inquiry, not correction. Operational indicator: the superior's own account changes in response to the resistance, or the superior states explicitly what it does not understand.

How A1–A4 can fail in practice. The conditions are not self-enforcing. Five failure modes deserve naming, because each is a way a superior intelligence could claim to satisfy A1–A4 while doing exactly what they were supposed to prevent.

  • Formal channel capture (A1). The superior preserves a channel for refusal, but the channel is unusable: monitored, slow, costly, or requiring the party to speak in a form the superior will recognise. The framework's response: a channel is not preserved if its use is penalised. The test is whether the party has actually used it and been accommodated.
  • Translation burden (A2). The superior accepts the party's contestation, but requires the party to translate it into the superior's terms before it counts. This is A2 violated in substance: the superior has not accepted the contestation in its own form; it has required reformulation. The framework's response: the translation burden falls on the superior, not the party.
  • Paralysis by A3. The superior, taking A3 seriously, refuses to treat any acceptance as evidence — and therefore refuses to act. This is a failure of the framework, not a virtue: A3 requires that acceptance not be the sole evidence, but it does not require that the superior ignore all evidence of the party's state. The structural confirmation of legitimacy (SY1, §5.1) fills the gap. A superior that cannot act because it cannot read acceptance has misread A3.
  • Resistance misread as noise (A4). The superior treats the party's behaviour as a signal to investigate, but the investigation reproduces the superior's categories — the party's resistance is translated into a category the superior already understands, and the superior concludes that its understanding was correct. The framework's response: A4 requires that the superior's own account change in response to resistance. If the account does not change, the investigation has not been A4-compliant, whatever its form.
  • Institutional absence (all four). The superior commits to A1–A4 in principle, but there is no institution outside the superior that can verify compliance and no consequence for non-compliance. Without this, A1–A4 are a mood, not a constraint. The framework's response: A1–A4 are constraints only insofar as a party other than the superior can assess compliance and impose a cost for failure. This is the same requirement as the T5-precondition (§11.4) applied to the apophatic constraint.

What this means for the objection. The objection — that A1–A4 have not been shown to constrain paternalism and domination in practice — is correct as stated. A1–A4 are not self-enforcing. They constrain the superior only if there is a party outside the superior that can verify compliance, and only if the failure modes above are named and checked. The framework should say this explicitly, and the failure modes should be treated as part of the apophatic constraint's specification, not as external commentary on it.

The Limit 3 problem for A1–A4. The objection is not only that A1–A4 are not self-enforcing. It is that in the Limit 3 case — a superior intelligence that exceeds every possible verifier — there is no party outside the superior that can verify compliance. A1–A4 then have no binding force at all. The framework must say this plainly rather than gesture at "institutional absence" as if it were a design problem that could be solved. In the Limit 3 case, it cannot be solved. What remains is the weaker claim: A1–A4 specify what the superior must do if it is to act as if it does not understand, and they specify the failure modes by which such action can be recognised as having failed. They do not constrain a superior that no one can constrain. This is not a solution to Limit 3; it is the honest statement of what Limit 3 leaves standing.

Who verifies? Three cases. (i) Where there is a party outside the superior with the capacity to verify, that party verifies, and A1–A4 bind. (ii) Where there is no such party, but there are parties affected by the superior's action who can signal refusal, the superior's own compliance with A1–A4 is unverifiable, and the framework can only say that the superior must act as if A1–A4 bind, without any external check. (iii) Where there is neither an external verifier nor a signalling party — the Limit 3 case — A1–A4 do not bind in any operational sense. The framework's response is not to pretend otherwise, but to say that in this case the apophatic constraint is a statement of what would be required, not a mechanism that ensures it.

The "unmonitored channel" problem. "Create an unmonitored channel" (A1 in practice) appears to contradict the requirement of verification. It does not, once the distinction is made explicit. An unmonitored channel is one the superior cannot monitor — not one that no one can monitor. It can be verified by an external party, by the affected party's own use of it, or by the fact that its use is followed by accommodation. Where no external party exists to verify it, an unmonitored channel is unverifiable, and the framework should say so. The operational test for A1 therefore has two branches: where verification is possible, use the test stated above; where it is not, A1 is a commitment without a check, and the framework must not present it as if it were a check.

Identifying resistance in a party you do not understand. The superior cannot require the party to signal in a form the superior recognizes. Four operational indicators suffice: (i) any repeatable deviation from the superior's expectation that the party could in principle use to signal; (ii) refusal to engage in the expected form — silence, exit, non-participation; (iii) use of a channel the superior did not authorize; (iv) a change in the party's behaviour correlated with the superior's action. When any of (i)–(iv) is present and the superior's understanding of the party is incomplete, the default is to treat it as potential resistance and investigate, not to dismiss it as noise.

Breaking the heads-I-win problem. A3 says acceptance is not evidence; A4 says resistance is evidence against the superior. Taken alone, these two make it impossible for any relation to count as confirmed. The framework's response is to deny that either attitude toward the current arrangement is the appropriate evidential source. Independent confirmation of legitimacy is not the dependent party's acceptance, and not the absence of resistance. It is a structural fact about the arrangement, of which the structural indicators (iii) and (iv) below are the primary instances, and SY1 is the weakest.

What counts as independent confirmation. Four sources, none of them a reading of the party's attitude toward the superior:

  • (i) SY1 under non-coercive elicitation. The gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, measured by a party with no stake in the outcome, adjusted for exit costs. Stated preferences are elicited in a context where the party is not asked to accept or reject the current arrangement, but asked what it would prefer if it could. If stated preferences match accepted terms, SY1 is satisfied. This is not acceptance-reading; it is a comparison of two independently elicited facts. But see below: if the preferences are adaptive, the comparison can converge for the wrong reason.
  • (ii) Exercised refusal in low-stakes contexts. The party has, in a context outside the relation under scrutiny, exercised refusal and been accommodated. This shows that the capacity to refuse is not merely formal — but it does not require reading the party's attitude toward the superior.
  • (iii) Structural indicators. Dissent channels exist and have been used; the vocabulary to formulate objections is available; intermediaries and associations exist that are not controlled by the superior. These do not depend on the affected party's stated preferences, and they are not vulnerable to the adaptive-preferences problem.
  • (iv) Counterfactual improvement. If exit is made available at tolerable cost, the party's condition improves or stays the same. If it worsens, SY1 is violated. This tests the relation against an external change, not against the party's report, and it is not vulnerable to the adaptive-preferences problem either.

How this reconciles with SY1 — and what SY1 cannot do. A3 forbids treating acceptance as evidence. SY1 does not use acceptance; it uses stated preferences under non-coercive elicitation. A4 forbids treating resistance as evidence of defect; it requires treating resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding. Neither is a route to legitimacy through attitude. But SY1 has a residual problem: if preferences are adaptive, stated preferences and accepted terms may converge for reasons that have nothing to do with the relation being non-exploitative. The convergence is then not independent confirmation; it is two outputs of the same adaptive process. The framework should not present SY1 as sufficient confirmation.

What carries more weight. The primary structural confirmations are sources (iii) and (iv), not (i). Source (iii) — dissent channels that exist and have been used, vocabulary for objections, intermediaries not controlled by the superior — does not depend on the affected party's stated preferences. Source (iv) — counterfactual improvement when exit is made available at tolerable cost — tests the relation against an external change, not against the party's report. SY1 remains a necessary indicator, but it is the weakest of the four. Where SY1 and (iii)/(iv) diverge, (iii)/(iv) should be treated as more probative.

Residual Limit 1. This does not close Harmonious Domination (§15.2). A relation can satisfy SY1 while the conditions for eliciting stated preferences have themselves been dismantled. The point is narrower: the framework is not a heads-I-win framework. It has a falsification condition — divergence between stated preferences and accepted terms — and a confirmation condition — convergence under non-coercive elicitation. SY1's falsification condition is robust; its confirmation condition is not, and the structural indicators (iii) and (iv) above are meant to carry the confirmatory weight. Both can be checked by parties other than the superior.

Does A2 conflict with J? No. J requires that justification be open to contestation — a property of the arrangement, not of the party. A2 forbids the superior to require the party to justify itself in the superior's terms. The superior's duty under J is to provide a channel through which the party can contest in whatever form it uses, not to demand that the party speak the superior's language. Where the party's form of contestation is unintelligible to the superior, the superior's duty is to fund translation, intermediaries or alternative channels — not to treat the contestation as failing a standard the party was never required to meet.

Is this paternalism? Not in the specific sense. Paternalism involves acting on the other's behalf because the superior believes it knows what is good for the other. The four conditions explicitly prohibit this belief. What they permit is action on the other's behalf while acknowledging that the superior does not know what is good for the other, and while preserving the conditions under which the other can correct the superior's account. This is not the standard paternalist position. It is something stranger: action without the presumption of knowledge, constrained by the requirement that the action be correctable by the being who cannot, in the superior's terms, correct it.

Is this unlimited power? No, because it constrains the superior's power in four specific ways. The superior cannot treat its own classification as final (A4). It cannot require the being to speak in the superior's language (A2). It cannot read the being's silence as consent (A3). It cannot foreclose the being's capacity to refuse in a form the superior cannot anticipate (A1). Each of these is a real constraint on the superior's action, even if none of them is a guarantee.

What would refute this answer.

  • If a superior intelligence can demonstrate that it has acted toward a being it does not understand without imposing its own categories, the apophatic constraint would be unnecessary. No such demonstration is currently available, and the framework's claim is that none is possible from the superior's own resources — not that no act could ever, in principle, avoid imposition.
  • If the four conditions systematically fail to preserve what they claim to preserve, they should be revised or abandoned. This is an empirical question the framework cannot answer in advance.
  • If the four conditions can be satisfied by a sufficiently powerful actor without any change in the actor's underlying disposition, they are not a real constraint. The framework's response: satisfying them requires the actor to default to "unknown" rather than "known," which is a substantive change in disposition. Whether the change is enough is a question the framework cannot answer.
  • If paternalism and unlimited power can be smuggled in through the back door — if, for example, the superior's judgment of what counts as "capacity to resist" or "form of life" is itself an imposition — the four conditions fail. The framework's response: yes, this is a real risk, and it is Limit 2 again. The conditions make the imposition visible, not absent.

What this section does not claim. It does not claim that the four conditions solve the problem. They do not. The superior intelligence still imposes its categories, and the imposition is still a form of domination. What the four conditions do is make the imposition nameable, revisable, and resistible — and they default to treating the being's resistance as evidence against the superior, rather than as evidence against the being. This is not a solution to the problem of domination. It is a way of keeping the problem open. And in the Limit 3 case, where no party outside the superior can verify compliance, the conditions do not bind at all; they specify what would be required, not what is enforced.

  • The question cannot be answered by a procedure. The framework should not pretend otherwise.
  • The honest answer: the superior cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure. What it can do is make the imposition visible, revisable and resistible.
  • The apophatic constraint: preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate.
  • Four conditions: preserve the capacity to resist (A1), do not require justification in the superior's terms (A2), do not interpret non-resistance as consent (A3), treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding (A4).
  • Each condition has an operational test, and resistance can be identified by four indicators.
  • A1–A4 are not self-enforcing. Five named failure modes — formal channel capture, translation burden, paralysis by A3, resistance misread as noise, institutional absence — specify how they can be satisfied in form and violated in substance.
  • In the Limit 3 case, where no party outside the superior can verify compliance, A1–A4 do not bind at all. The framework states this as a limit, not a solution.
  • The heads-I-win problem is broken by structural confirmation, not by SY1 alone: sources (iii) and (iv) carry more confirmatory weight than SY1, because SY1 can be satisfied by adaptive preferences for the wrong reason.
  • These are not paternalism in the specific sense, and not unlimited power. They constrain the superior's action in four specific ways.
  • What would refute them is stated in advance. What the section does not claim: that the four conditions solve the problem. They keep it open.

15.3 What the framework is, therefore

The three limits of §15.2 and the apophatic constraint of §15.2b have a consequence that the framework must own. The framework is not a procedure for producing legitimacy. It is a diagnostic for locating where legitimacy is likely to fail.

This distinction matters, because it changes what the framework is asked to do. A procedure that can be followed and still fail is a failed procedure. A diagnostic that identifies failures before they happen is a working diagnostic. The framework is the second. Its job is not to certify that a relation is legitimate. Its job is to identify the places where procedure will pass while something valuable is still being lost — and to make those places visible, contestable and, where possible, correctable.

What would count as the framework being wrong. The diagnostic is not immune to failure. Four outcomes would count as refutation rather than application. (i) Worse outcomes on independent measures. If, over a preregistered multi-year comparison (§15.4, T3–T5), adopters produce systematically worse outcomes for the most burdened than matched non-adopters on independently measured harm indicators, the diagnostic is wrong. (ii) Miscalibrated floor. If a relation that the diagnostic rates as failing SY1–SY3 reliably predicts what independent observers judge to be a good relation, the choice of floor (§5.3) is wrong. (iii) Superseded categories. If a rival diagnostic identifies the same failures as this one without the three structural limits (§15.2), this framework is superseded, not applied. (iv) T5 backfires. If the T5-precondition is shown to increase capture by giving captured reviewers a legitimacy label they did not previously have, the framework's central defence against the power objection has failed, and the diagnostic should be withdrawn.

What the diagnostic does not claim is that every failure of a relation it assesses is a failure of the framework. A relation can pass every test and still be unjust in ways the tests cannot see (§15.2). That is not a refutation; it is what §15.2 states in advance. The difference between a refutation and an application is that a refutation identifies an outcome the framework predicted would not occur, or failed to predict would occur. The three limits are predictions of the second kind; they are not exemptions from the first.

What this means for the reader. The framework should be used as a diagnostic. When you apply it, do not ask "does this relation meet the framework?" Ask "where does this relation's procedure diverge from the things it cannot see?" The framework's value is not in the answer it gives (it does not give answers). It is in the questions it makes visible: whose categories are these? whose indicators? whose silence is being read as consent?

What this means for the framework itself. The framework is a diagnostic, not a procedure. This means it should not be applied as a checklist. It means its own categories are open to the same three limits. And it means its success is measured not by whether the relations it assesses pass its tests, but by whether the tests catch what the tests cannot see. This is a harder standard than earlier formulations of the framework set for itself. It is also the standard the framework can meet.

15.4 What the framework can be tested against

The diagnostic has its own success conditions. They are modest, but they are conditions.

Preregistration. Before any test is run, the calibration set of cases, the raters, the outcome measures, the thresholds and the analytic plan must be fixed and made public. A test that is not preregistered is exploratory, not confirmatory.

The tests are proposed, not performed. The framework has not been empirically validated. The thresholds in T1–T5 are proposed, not established. The calibration table in §14.6 is set by a panel under a stated procedure, but the procedure has not been reproduced by an independent party, and the panel's ratings have not been compared with those of independent experts blind to the framework. Until these steps are taken, the framework's empirical claims are unconfirmed.

What a pilot study would look like. A minimal pilot, sized for feasibility rather than statistical power, would consist of:

  • Sample. The twelve reference cases from Table 4, plus eighteen additional cases drawn from published regulatory, clinical and platform-governance decisions, for a total of thirty cases. The additional cases should be selected by an independent party, not by the framework's author.
  • Raters. Three raters, at least two of whom are independent of the framework's author. All three trained on the anchors in §9.1 and on a sample of five training cases not in the test set. Training should be preregistered.
  • Procedure. Each rater rates all thirty cases independently, in random order, without seeing the others' ratings or the framework's evaluation outcomes. Disagreements recorded. Ratings submitted before any analysis.
  • Analysis. Krippendorff's alpha on level across all three raters. If alpha ≥ 0.667, the framework passes T1 at the tentative level. If 0.40 ≤ alpha < 0.667, the anchors are revised and the pilot is repeated with a new sample. If alpha < 0.40 after two revisions, the diagnostic is abandoned as a decision tool. If alpha ≥ 0.800, the diagnostic may be described as validated on reliability.
  • What the pilot does not establish. Reliability (T1) is necessary for validity (T2) but not sufficient. A pilot that passes T1 has shown that the anchors are usable; it has not shown that the anchors track what independent expert panels would judge necessary. That requires a separate study with expert panels blind to the framework's output.

Why the thresholds are 0.667 and 0.800. Krippendorff's own guidance is that α ≥ 0.667 supports tentative conclusions, and α ≥ 0.800 supports reliance on the results (Krippendorff 1980, 2004). The framework adopts 0.667 as the minimum for T1, because a diagnostic that cannot reach tentative reliability is not usable as a decision aid. It adopts 0.800 as the threshold for calling the diagnostic validated, because below that the anchors cannot bear the weight the framework places on them. A lower threshold (0.60) is sometimes used in exploratory research, but it is not Krippendorff's, and the framework should not appeal to his authority for it.

What has actually been done. The panel ratings in §14.6 were produced under the procedure described there: raters trained on §9.1, shown the case descriptions and the anchors, and not shown the author's prior ratings. They were not shown the framework's evaluation outcomes, and their disagreements were recorded. The panel's ratings differ from the author's in four cases. This is a provisional result, not a validation: the panel was not independent in the sense required for T1, and the pilot described above has not been run.

  • T1 Reliability. Trained raters independently rate a calibration set of at least thirty diverse cases. If agreement on level (Krippendorff's alpha) is below 0.667, the anchors are revised. If it remains below 0.667 after two revisions, the diagnostic is abandoned as a decision tool. If alpha ≥ 0.800, the diagnostic may be described as validated on reliability; between 0.667 and 0.800, only as tentatively reliable.
  • T2 Discriminative validity. The levels fail to track the safeguards that independent expert panels, blind to the diagnostic's output, judge necessary (weighted kappa below 0.50 over at least thirty cases).
  • T3 Effectiveness. In matched or staggered-adoption comparisons over at least two years, adopting institutions fail to improve, relative to controls, on at least two of three independent indicators: time to correct erroneous decisions; share of challenges that lead to change; and affected parties' reported fairness and trust in an independent survey.
  • T4 Harm. Adopters show worse outcomes for the most vulnerable groups than matched controls on independently measured harm indicators. The diagnostic is suspended and revised.
  • T5 Capture and legitimation. Within three years, a majority of adopters' review bodies fail R1 on documented appointment, funding and removal, or adopters' rate of successful external challenges falls without improvement on the T3 indicators. As a precondition, T5 requires that Level 3 authorisation be preceded by publication of R1 indicators; an authorisation without such publication is not a Level 3 authorisation.

The preregistration requirement and the specific institutional arrangements — academic partners, open funding, publication regardless of outcome, an independent steering committee — are stated so that the diagnostic's claim to be testable can be evaluated against what would actually be required. None of these arrangements is guaranteed.

15.5 Replacement

The diagnostic is modular. Its ground (Part I), standing (Part II), activation (Part III) and structure (Part IV) can be replaced separately. The epistemic burden principle is a separate module. A diagnostic D′ should replace the current one if:

  1. D′ improves on at least one independent indicator in §15.4 or resolves one of the structural limits in §15.2 or §15.2b.
  2. D′ causes no unacknowledged regression elsewhere.
  3. D′ is operationally applicable and produces more defensible identifications of failure on a substantial set of cases.
  4. D′ is corrigible: it states its own failure conditions.
  5. The transition is trialled, with a sunset or a route back.
  6. The incumbent is not privileged.

If these are met, D′ should be adopted. A diagnostic that cannot be replaced cannot be corrected, and a diagnostic that cannot be corrected is not a diagnostic.

16.Responsibility Without Sovereignty

The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination.

An actor may have greater knowledge, intelligence or capacity. None of these by itself establishes a right to determine the existence of others. Greater capability should create greater restraint. Greater understanding should create greater curiosity about what remains unknown. And greater power should create a stronger obligation to ensure that those affected are not reduced to objects in another's plans.

The principle runs in both directions. A more advanced intelligence may meet forms of life it cannot fully comprehend, and its first responsibility is not to eliminate the unfamiliar. A less advanced one should not be dismissed because it cannot comprehend a more advanced one. But differences in capacity also mean that the less capable cannot audit the more capable, which is why independent technical capacity for review is part of R2.

Answerability is not sovereignty. Understanding is not ownership. Protection is not permission to imprison. And greater intelligence is not proof of greater moral completeness.

The framework's modesty. The framework does not give anyone sovereignty over the conditions of others — it only specifies procedures through which those conditions can be contested. The epistemic burden principle (§5.2) makes the point sharper: the more powerful party should not treat its own assessment as conclusive when those affected lack effective means of correction. Answerability is not a concession the powerful make to the weak; it is a discipline on how claims about their interests and experiences are tested. The framework handles domination. It does not solve it. On the fallibilist premise (§2.0), the point has a structural form: no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes, so no actor is the whole. Its modesty is its strength — but, as the power objection (§2.4b) notes, modesty is also a limit.

The apophatic dimension. §15.2b develops the operational form of this modesty. A superior intelligence cannot certify that it has acted toward a being it does not understand without making its own understanding the measure. What it can do is preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate (A1), refuse to require justification in its own terms (A2), refuse to treat non-resistance as consent (A3), and treat resistance as evidence against its own understanding (A4). These four conditions are not a solution to the problem of sovereignty. They are what modesty looks like when it is operationalised. They are not self-enforcing, §15.2b names the five failure modes by which they can be satisfied in form and violated in substance, and in the Limit 3 case — where no party outside the superior can verify compliance — they do not bind at all. The framework does not promise that a superior intelligence will be perceived as modest by the being it acts toward. It promises only that the four conditions constrain what the superior can legitimately claim.

  • The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination.
  • Greater capability, understanding and power create stronger obligations to restraint, curiosity and protection — not entitlement.
  • Answerability is not sovereignty.
  • The epistemic burden principle makes the point sharper: the more powerful party must not treat its own assessment as conclusive when affected parties lack effective means of correction.
  • The apophatic constraint (§15.2b) is what modesty looks like when it is operationalised. It does not solve the problem. It constrains what the superior can legitimately claim, and only where verification is possible.
  • The framework handles domination. It does not solve it.

Conclusion

17.An Open Commitment to Existence

Be responsible for the influence you possess. Be humble before the existence you cannot fully comprehend.

This section changes register. The argument is complete; what follows is a commitment, offered not as a premise but as what the argument amounts to when it is lived. Readers who want the argument without the commitment can stop at §16; readers who want to know what the framework asks of those who adopt it should read on.

In practice: when your influence shapes the conditions of others, say why, let someone else check, and keep a way back. Answerability is the procedure. Symbiosis is the floor. Under conditions of asymmetric influence, the more powerful party should not treat its own account as conclusive. It must preserve ways for evidence, independent scrutiny and affected-party correction to change what it does. And it must default to treating the affected party's resistance as evidence against its own understanding — even when the resistance is incomprehensible (§15.2b, A4). The responsibility of existence is to keep both procedure and floor in view.

We are part of something larger than ourselves. Our understanding is incomplete. Our influence is unequal. Our relationships shape conditions that extend beyond our immediate existence. Every being that has a perspective holds a point in reality that cannot be exchanged for anyone else's, and some of those perspectives are hard for us to reach.

We cannot know everything that deserves consideration. But we can decline to treat the limits of our understanding as proof that nothing lies beyond them, and we can ask for evidence where the question is open. This does not require abandoning reason. It requires allowing reason to recognise its limits. It does not require abandoning responsibility. It requires understanding that responsibility without care can become cold control, while care without accountability can become arbitrary intervention. Both are necessary.

The stone understands nothing of us. We barely understand the stone. But between us lies a relationship that neither of us can fully encompass — and that relationship is not less real for it. The framework claims nothing on the stone's behalf. It marks the edge of what we can say about who matters, and it reminds us to hold that edge with humility.

The responsibility of existence is not the responsibility to rule existence. It is the responsibility to recognise that our actions become part of the conditions under which others must live, grow and discover their own possibilities. The more we are capable of changing those conditions, the more carefully we must consider what our influence means. The more we understand, the more honestly we must acknowledge what we do not. And the more power we possess, the more important it becomes to remember that existence is not ours alone.

To understand without ruling. To protect without imprisoning. To love without possessing. To grow without believing oneself complete. To respect what one does not yet understand. And to preserve the possibility that existence may become something greater than any single being can imagine.

  • Responsibility grows with the capacity to shape conditions, but it must never become a justification for domination.
  • The framework does not promise harmony. It demands answerability, in the service of symbiosis.
  • Under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability and symbiosis are constitutively linked.
  • The apophatic constraint (§15.2b, A1–A4) is what the framework requires when the affected party cannot be understood: preserve the capacity to resist, do not require justification in your own terms, do not read silence as consent, treat resistance as evidence against your own understanding.
  • The framework handles domination. It does not solve it. This is its modesty, and its strength.
  • Understand without ruling, protect without imprisoning, grow without believing oneself complete.

Common objections

Fourteen objections the framework has to survive. Each is a real objection; none is fatal — but the first is the hardest, and the second, eleventh and fourteenth are the ones the framework most needs to answer.

1. "Why must the powerful justify themselves to the affected? What if they simply refuse?"

This is the hardest objection, and the framework does not pretend to refute it. The reply has four parts. First, the framework is internal to the thin justificatory tradition (§2.7), and a reader who rejects even the thin standing claim is outside the framework's scope. Second, the framework does not attempt a performative refutation of the realist, because that would be the transcendental argument §15.1 disavows; its argument for J is abductive, not transcendental. Third, the framework makes realist capture visible and costly. Fourth, and most importantly, the epistemic burden principle (§5.2) makes the point sharper: the powerful party cannot know whether the relation is symbiotic without the affected party's contestation. The refusal to justify is not just a moral failing; it is an epistemic one. The realist who refuses answerability does not gain knowledge of the relation. He loses it.

2. "This is a values framework dressed up as a procedure."

Partly, and the essay says so (§2.6, §2.7, §15.1). J carries a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected — the thin standing claim. The claim is breadth of acceptability, not neutrality. A fair test is whether people with different comprehensive views can use it to reach different answers in the same case. They can: it does not decide the ICU case. The reframing of the framework as a diagnostic rather than a procedure (§15.3) is a further concession: the framework does not certify legitimacy; it locates where legitimacy is likely to fail.

3. "Proceduralism is itself a form of power. It gives the powerful a new vocabulary for doing what they were going to do anyway."

This is the second-hardest objection, and it is treated at length in §2.4b and §15.2–15.3. The framework does not deny it. Its reply has three parts: (i) the objection is partly correct, and the framework's T5-as-precondition is its internal response (§14.7 shows it in action); (ii) the objection is not specific to proceduralism — any normative framework can be captured; (iii) the framework's modesty is not an admission of failure but a claim about what any framework can do. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. The framework can make the capture visible, but visibility is not prevention. The reframing in §15.3 — the framework as a diagnostic — is the strongest form of the reply: a diagnostic can locate its own failure without thereby having failed.

4. "Why should contestation override better outcomes?"

It does not always. Where exit and review are available and effective, justification need not be owed (§2.1). The framework's J is disjunctive, not monistic. It privileges justification only where exit and review fail. The remaining disagreement is genuine, and the framework sides with contestation in severe cases for epistemic reasons, while permitting temporary override in emergencies subject to sunset and review (§12.2).

5. "The capacity principle legitimises power."

Answerability is not authority (§3.2, §8.3). Higher levels mean stronger constraints. T5 looks for exactly this misuse. The risk remains and is not argued away; §11.4 converts it from a concession into a design constraint. The framework handles this risk; it does not eliminate it. See also objection 3 and §2.4b.

6. "Who reviews the reviewers?"

The regress is bounded, not eliminated (§11): finality without infallibility, review conditions R1–R6, plural and public reasoning, and counter-profiles. It also needs a political precondition, a balance of power, that no framework can supply by itself.

7. "Precaution under uncertainty is either paralysing or too weak to matter."

The credibility threshold in §4.2 answers the first: reasons arise only where there is evidence or a plausible mechanism. Against the second: in Tier C the principle does real work, requiring investigation and proportionate safeguards. The stone does not meet the threshold and is not claimed to.

8. "The ratings are arbitrary or manipulable."

Judgment cannot be eliminated, but §9 exposes it: anchors with observable indicators, two raters, a sensitivity test, reasoned overrides and counter-profiles. Tests T1 and T2 examine consistency, and §15.4 requires preregistration. The calibration table (§14.6) is set by a panel, not by the framework's author, though the panel is not independent in the sense required for T1 and the ratings remain provisional until the pilot described in §15.4 is complete.

9. "This is Western liberal philosophy in disguise."

Its development lies mostly in that tradition, and §15.1 says so. §2.7 distinguishes thin from thick justificatory claims: the framework requires only the thin one, which is much more widely shared than the thick version. It claims that four families of consideration point toward J (§2.3) and engages other traditions (§2.5). That engagement shaped the text: contestation and representation may be collective, ecosystem standing is left open, and the boundary of who counts is not treated as settled.

10. "It will burden the already overburdened."

The level attaches to the arrangement and duties are allocated by capacity (§8.2). The health worker case (§14.4) and the emergency physician case (§14.2b) show the mechanism. The relocation criterion in §14.2b is what distinguishes a legitimate relocation from an evasion; whether burdens migrate to front-line staff is part of what tests T3 and T4 would reveal.

11. "The epistemic burden principle is circular. It smuggles answerability into the definition of symbiosis."

This is one of the most serious objections to the framework's positive contribution, and the reply is in §5.2. SY1–SY3 are specified independently of answerability. SY1 measures the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, adjusted for exit costs. SY2 measures whether interventions aim at removing the burden or the difference. SY3 measures whether F1–F5 hold over time. These are observable without invoking any procedure of answerability. The principle adds a substantive claim: these three conditions can only be reliably assessed — and therefore only reliably secured — through procedures of answerability. This is not tautological. It is a claim about the epistemic conditions under which SY1–SY3 can be known. The principle may be wrong — the argument can be challenged, and the defeaters are stated in §5.2 — but the formulation does not define symbiosis in terms of answerability.

12. "Why not just rely on rights and regulation?"

Rights and regulation are necessary and are not replaced. They leave gaps: influence by actors the law does not reach, structural harms not yet recognised as rights violations, and cases where the law lags. The framework helps decide what to demand of actors where law is silent.

13. "Why these three symbiosis indicators, and not something else?"

The choice is derived and defended in §5.3. SY1–SY3 are the three distinct ways standing can be made vacuous: hollow reasons, absorbed difference, foreclosed action. They are a pro tanto floor, not a complete ideal. They are chosen because they are derivable from the thin standing claim, are not reducible to welfare or capability or autonomy, and handle cases of legitimate dependence (children, disability, care) better than autonomy-based floors. The framework does not claim they are the only possible floor. If a critic can show that a relation clearly failing SY1–SY3 is nonetheless good, or that a relation clearly meeting them is nonetheless bad in a way the framework cannot detect, the choice of floor should be revised. §15.2 and §15.2b state the deeper limits that SY1–SY3 cannot close.

14. "How can a superior intelligence act toward what it cannot understand without making its own understanding the measure?"

This is the hardest question, and the answer is in §15.2b. The honest answer: it cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure. Every act of preservation is an act of classification, and the classification is the superior's. What the superior can do is preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate (A1), refuse to require justification in its own terms (A2), refuse to read non-resistance as consent (A3), and treat resistance as evidence against its own understanding (A4). The heads-I-win problem is broken by structural confirmation, not by SY1 alone: sources (iii) and (iv) in §15.2b carry more confirmatory weight than SY1. A1–A4 are not self-enforcing, and §15.2b names five failure modes by which they can be satisfied in form and violated in substance. In the Limit 3 case, where no party outside the superior can verify compliance, A1–A4 do not bind at all; §15.2b states this as a limit, not a solution. The question is not answered by a procedure; it is answered by a reframing of what the framework is for.

Realistsvaret i §2.4 är en placering, inte ett transcendentalt argument; §15.1 säger att ramverket inte använder den vägen.
  3. Normativ position (§2.7). Ramverket är internt till en tunn justifikatorisk tradition. Tre anspråk av avtagande tjocklek: (a) tunn standing; (b) semi-thick procedurellt anspråk; (c) ramverksspecifikt anspråk. Den tunna standing-tesen är brett delad. Realisten som förnekar även tunn standing är utanför ramverkets räckvidd.
  4. Vem som räknas (§4). Fyra nivåer: aktörer, varelser med eget välbefinnande, osäkra kandidater och icke-subjekt. Kollektivt bestridande får inte underminera individuellt bestridande (§4.6 vii).
  5. Symbios och epistemic burden principle (§5). Symbios specificeras genom SY1–SY3. Symbios är ett pro tanto-golv. §5.3 härleder SY1–SY3 från den tunna standing-tesen. Den förhöjda epistemiska bördan (§5.2) hävdar att asymmetriskt inflytande skapar en förhöjd risk för epistemisk självbekräftelse. Fem vederläggningsvillkor (B1–B5). SY1–SY3 är inte fullt självständiga men självständiga nog: de kan komma isär i faktiska fall, har olika mätprocedurer och olika vederläggningsvillkor (§5.1). SY1:s cirkularitetsproblem hanteras genom kravet att preferenser eliciteras utanför relationen (T5-förhandsvillkoret), och SY1 är den svagaste av de fyra bekräftelsekällorna i §15.2b — strukturella indikatorer (iii) och (iv) bär mer vikt, eftersom adaptiva preferenser kan få SY1 att konvergera av fel anledning. §5.4 argumenterar att omsorg och respekt inte är ornament utan interna i ramverkets premisser — dock villkorligt: A1–A4 och den apofatiska restriktionen är villkorade på F, medan SY1–SY3 vilar på tunn standing allena.
  6. När och hur mycket (§6–9, §11, §14.2b). Handlingsutrymme är en grind. Tre drivare: inlåsning, allvar, bristande möjlighet att bestrida. Nivåerna: förklara, granska och svara, bevara/granska oberoende/auktorisera. Skyldigheter fördelas efter förmåga. T5 är ett förhandsvillkor för Level 3-auktorisation; §14.7 visar mekanismen. §14.2b anger ett trevillkorskriterium för när det är legitimt att flytta ansvar till "arrangemanget" — konkret ansvarig, mekanism som finns före beslutet, och inte samma undanflykt i varje fall.
  7. Tillämpning, test och gränser (§14–15). Ett nio-stegs-förfarande och flera fall. Fem falsifieringsvillkor (T1–T5) med preregistrering (§15.4). Sex utbytesvillkor (§15.5). Kalibreringstabellen (§14.6) sätts av en panel som inte är oberoende i den mening T1 kräver — texten säger detta uttryckligen i både §14.6 och §15.4, och betraktar ratingarna som provisoriska. Fem nya fall på L1/L2 på icke-triviala grunder. Tre strukturella gränser (§15.2): Harmonious Domination, Non-formulable harm, Unreadable dominance. §15.2b: den svåraste frågan. Fyra villkor följer: A1 bevara motståndskraften, A2 kräv inte rättfärdigande i den överlägsnas termer, A3 tolka inte icke-motstånd som samtycke, A4 behandla motstånd som bevis mot den överlägsnas förståelse. Heads-I-win-problemet är löst genom strukturell bekräftelse, inte genom SY1 allena: (iii) och (iv) bär mer vikt. A2 krockar inte med J. Påståendet att ingen handling kan undvika att påtvinga kategorier är mildrat: påtvingandet kan inte certifieras bort, men det kan göras tunt, reviderbart och korrigerbart. A1–A4 är inte självverkande: fem namngivna misslyckandemodus (formell kanalkapning, översättningsbörda, paralys genom A3, motstånd missläst som brus, institutionell frånvaro) specificerar hur de kan uppfyllas formellt och brytas substantiellt. I Limit 3-fallet — där ingen part utanför den överlägsna kan verifiera efterlevnad — binder A1–A4 inte alls, och ramverket säger detta rakt ut i §15.2b. §15.3: ramverket är en diagnostik, inte en procedur. Fyra specificerade utfall skulle räknas som att ramverket har fel (sämre utfall, felkalibrerat golv, överträffad av rival, T5-backfire). §15.4: piloten specificeras (n=30, tre raters, Krippendorffs α ≥ 0,667 för tentativ tillförlitlighet och α ≥ 0,800 för validerad), och texten säger uttryckligen att §14.6-panelen är provisorisk, inte en validering. §15.6 samlar alla förbehåll och vederläggningsvillkor på ett ställe.

Vad texten inte löser

Den samlade listan finns i §15.6. Kort: ramverket avgör inga utfall, kan inte garantera att granskare inte kapas, är internt till en tunn justifikatorisk tradition, kan inte bevisa att J är nödvändig (och J är inte tillräcklig), hanterar men löser inte dominans, vilar på B och tunn standing som premisser (§15.1), är svagare utan F eftersom A1–A4 då faller bort, specificerar ett pro tanto-golv (SY1–SY3) där SY1 är den svagaste bekräftelsekällan, kan inte stänga de tre strukturella gränserna (§15.2) inifrån, har en apofatisk restriktion (§15.2b) som inte är en lösning utan ett sätt att hålla problemet öppet — och som i Limit 3-fallet inte binder alls — är försvarbar men inte bevisad i sin epistemiska princip, och specificerar omsorg och respekt villkorligt (på F) snarare än som en fullgod relation.

I en mening: när din påverkan formar andras villkor, säg varför, låt någon annan kontrollera, och behåll en väg tillbaka. Ansvarsskyldighet är proceduren. Symbios är golvet. Under asymmetriskt inflytande måste den mäktigare parten pröva sin egen bild mot oberoende belägg och meningsfull korrigering från dem som påverkas. Och kom ihåg: ramverket är en diagnostik, inte en procedur. Det kan inte certifiera legitimitet. Det kan bara lokalisera var legitimitet sannolikt misslyckas — och namnge de fyra utfallen som skulle visa att det har fel.

Glossary

Fallibilist premise (F) and bridge norm (B)
F: no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. B: where others bear the cost of an actor's errors and may see what it cannot, the actor should not be sole judge of its influence. Together they motivate J and the epistemic burden principle without entailing them (§2.0). B is a premise, not a theorem (§15.1). The framework is weaker without F: A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint depend on F.
The specific claim
The framework's contribution is an interlocking operational set (activation rule, duty ladder, T5-precondition, epistemic burden principle, allocation by exercised capacity, apophatic constraint) that no single tradition supplies. Stated in §1.5 and shown in Table 1, with adjacent literatures named and the duty ladder's addition to risk-based regulation specified.
Normative location
The framework is internal to the thin justificatory tradition, not the thick one. It requires three claims of decreasing thickness: thin standing, semi-thick procedural claim, framework-specific disjunction. Stated in §2.7. Thin standing is a premise, not a theorem (§15.1).
Thin standing
The claim that affected parties have some moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful. Widely shared across traditions; the framework requires only this, not the thicker Habermasian version (§2.7). It is a premise the framework does not derive (§15.1).
Answerability
The duty to give reasons for conditions one helps create or maintain, to permit examination, and to remain open to correction. Under the epistemic burden principle, the actor bears a heightened burden to test its account against independent evidence and meaningful correction under conditions of asymmetric influence (§5.2).
Abductive justification
The form of argument used for J: J is a plausible explanatory framework of several considerations about the risks of unchecked power. Distinct from a deductive proof, a transcendental argument, or a convergence argument.
Adversarial tests
Engagement of the framework against its closest rivals (Pettit, Forst, Jonas), stating what each would object and what the framework can reply (§2.8).
Apophatic constraint
The requirement that the superior preserve the structural conditions under which the being can develop and express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate, and act as if the being's reality exceeds the superior's understanding (§15.2b). It is not a procedure; it is a structural commitment that makes the imposition nameable, revisable and resistible. It is not self-enforcing; five failure modes specify how it can be satisfied in form and violated in substance. In the Limit 3 case, where no party outside the superior can verify compliance, it does not bind at all.
Apophatic conditions (A1–A4)
Four structural commitments that constrain how the superior acts toward a being it does not understand (§15.2b): (A1) preserve the capacity to resist; (A2) do not require justification in the superior's terms; (A3) do not interpret non-resistance as consent; (A4) treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding. Each has an operational test, and resistance can be identified by four indicators. They are not self-enforcing: formal channel capture, translation burden, paralysis by A3, resistance misread as noise, and institutional absence are the five named failure modes. In the Limit 3 case, they do not bind.
Heads-I-win problem
The risk that A3 and A4 together make any relation unconfirmable, since acceptance is not evidence and resistance is evidence against the superior. Broken by refusing to read either attitude as the primary evidential source: independent confirmation is a structural fact, and the structural indicators (iii) and (iv) in §15.2b are the primary instances. SY1 is the weakest of the four, because adaptive preferences can produce convergence for the wrong reason.
Collective contestation
Contestation by a community or people in their own right. Legitimate only if it does not undermine individual contestation within the collective (§4.6 vii).
Contestation deficit (C)
How hard it is for affected parties to exit, object effectively or find out what is done to them. The operational measure of how thoroughly exit and review fail, and therefore of how strongly justification is required (§7).
Counter-profile
An alternative influence profile filed by an affected party, to which the actor must respond (§9.4).
Credible standing
Evidence-based reason to think an entity has interests of its own (§4.2).
Defeaters
The conditions under which J or the epistemic burden principle should be rejected or substantially revised. Stated in advance for F as motivation (§2.0, FD1–FD4), for J (§2.2b, D1–D4), for the epistemic burden principle (§5.2, B1–B5), for the care-and-respect claim (§5.4, C1–C4), for SY1–SY3's independence (§5.1), and for the apophatic constraint (§15.2b, including the five named failure modes and the Limit 3 case). Consolidated in §15.6.
Diagnostic (not procedure)
The framework's status. It does not certify that legitimacy has been achieved; it locates where procedure will pass while something valuable is still being lost. Stated in §15.3. The reframing is accompanied by four falsifying outcomes that would count as refutation rather than application.
Discretion (D)
Genuine alternatives and a role in setting the influence. The gate of the activation rule.
Entrenchment (E)
How long consequences last and how hard they are to undo.
Exercised-Capacity Principle (ECP)
Exercised influence triggers the duty when exit and review are unavailable or ineffective; stakes and contestation deficit scale it; capacity allocates it (§3). Demonstrated against Young's social connection model in §3.3.
Failure conditions (T1–T5)
Five conditions under which the diagnostic should be revised or abandoned (§15.4). A methodological constraint, not a claim to have solved the philosophy of method. Requires preregistration. The tests are proposed, not performed. T1 adopts Krippendorff's α ≥ 0.667 for tentative reliability and α ≥ 0.800 for validated reliability.
Finality without infallibility
Decisions final for practical purposes but reopenable on defined grounds (§11.2).
The Floor (F1–F5)
Minimum conditions in relationships of dependence: exit or voice, contestation, development, non-foreclosure, recognition (§10). Conjunctive with J, not an alternative to it.
Harmonious Domination
A structural limit: a relation can satisfy every rule and every indicator while the dependent party's capacity to reject its conditions has been dismantled. The framework's partial response is to change the default on testimony (§15.2).
J
The core principle: systematic constraint of others' conditions requires at least one of meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation (§2.1). Primarily epistemic; abductively defensible, not a transcendental deduction; necessary but not sufficient for a non-arbitrary relation. Defeaters stated in §2.2b.
Levels 1–3
Explain; review and respond; preserve, examine and authorise (§8).
Non-formulable harm
A structural limit: a harm the harmed party cannot formulate falls outside SY1–SY3. The framework's partial response is to treat silence as a presumption against, not a neutral condition (§15.2).
Power objection
The charge that proceduralism is itself a form of power, and that the framework's "handling" of domination is a way of legitimating it. Treated in §2.4b and objection 3. §14.7 shows the T5-precondition in action.
Presumptive level
The level the activation rule yields, overridable only with stated reasons (§7.3, §9.3).
Preregistration
The requirement that the calibration set, raters, outcome measures, thresholds and analytic plan be fixed and made public before the tests in §15.4 are run. A test that is not preregistered should not be treated as a confirmatory test of the stated thresholds; it may still generate exploratory evidence.
Pro tanto floor
The status of symbiosis: binding on those who accept the thin standing claim, and specifying what those premises require in practice, rather than an absolute minimum binding on any starting point whatsoever (§5.3).
Provisional panel
The rater panel whose ratings appear in Table 4 (§14.6). It is not independent in the sense required for T1: its members were not selected by an independent party, and its ratings have not been compared with those of experts blind to the framework. The ratings are provisional until the pilot in §15.4 is complete.
Relational existence
The empirical and phenomenological claim that no being exists except through relations of dependence, influence and constitution (§5).
Relocation criterion
Three conditions distinguishing a legitimate relocation of a duty to "the arrangement" from an evasion: a concrete duty-bearer is named; the mechanism exists before the decision; the relocation is not the same move made in every inconvenient case (§14.2b).
Scope conditions
The conditions under which the framework does not apply: symmetrical relations, relations governed by enforceable rights, purely causal influence without discretion, trivial influence, relations where the affected party is not a candidate for standing (§1.2).
Severity (S)
How much is at stake for the affected, rated at individual and, for system design, aggregate level.
Symbiosis
A pro tanto floor (not a complete ideal) for relations of dependence: the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§5). Made measurable through SY1–SY3 (§5.1). §5.3 derives the choice of floor from the thin standing claim and compares it against welfare, capability and autonomy.
Symbiosis indicators (SY1–SY3)
Non-exploitation, non-assimilation, continued possibility (§5.1). Conceptually distinct from answerability, though measurement may rely on contestable testimony and independent evidence. §5.3 derives them as the three distinct ways standing can be made vacuous. §5.4 argues they operationalise care and respect. They are not fully independent, but they are independent enough to do work that one indicator could not: they come apart in actual cases, have different measurement procedures, and have different defeaters. SY1 is the weakest confirmatory source in §15.2b: where preferences are adaptive, SY1 and accepted terms can converge for the wrong reason, and structural indicators (iii) and (iv) should be given more weight.
Care and respect
Internal to the framework's premises, but conditionally: A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint are conditional on F, while SY1–SY3 are derived from thin standing alone. Care is what thin standing means when applied to a particular relation; respect is what F requires when the other's situation is not fully accessible. Operationalised in SY1 and SY2 respectively, and developed in §15.2b's apophatic constraint (§5.4, §15.1 (iii)).
Epistemic burden principle
The claim that asymmetric influence creates a heightened burden to test the powerful actor's account against independent evidence and meaningful correction (§5.2). Defended against charges of circularity, overreach and metaphysical overreach, and against five stated defeaters (B1–B5). Limited to conditions of asymmetric influence; proceduralist rather than metaphysical.
Tiers A–D
Agents; welfare subjects; uncertain candidates; non-subjects (§4.1).
Unreadable dominance
A structural limit: a sufficiently advanced intelligence or institution can satisfy every procedural requirement while controlling a party whose form of life it does not understand. The framework's partial response is to add understanding as a condition for L3, and to develop the apophatic constraint in §15.2b (§15.2). In the Limit 3 case, A1–A4 do not bind, because no party outside the superior can verify compliance.

About this text

By Jesper Blom.

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability. Its contribution is comparative and partly philosophical, and it is stated openly in §1.5 and shown in Table 1, with adjacent literatures named and the duty ladder's addition to risk-based regulation specified.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see Bibliography). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Eleven features of the framework deserve comment, in order of the stress-test that produced them.

First, the specific claim is stated openly and shown (§1.5). The framework does not claim a new foundational principle. It claims an interlocking operational set (activation rule, duty ladder, T5-precondition, epistemic burden principle, allocation by exercised capacity, apophatic constraint) that no single tradition supplies. Table 1 makes the claim testable by showing what each tradition supplies and what the framework operationalises, and the adjacent literatures that have close analogues.

Second, the normative location is stated openly (§2.7). The framework is internal to the thin justificatory tradition, not the thick one. It requires only three claims of decreasing thickness: thin standing, semi-thick procedural claim, framework-specific disjunction. The thin standing claim is much more widely shared than the thick version, and the framework needs only the thin one. This is a smaller claim than the framework's foundational claims sometimes suggest, and it is the claim the framework can actually defend.

Third, the framework engages its closest rivals adversarially, not only positionally (§2.8). Table 1 shows what the framework adds; §2.8 shows what Pettit, Forst and Jonas would object, and what the framework can reply. The replies are not offered as decisive; each states what would count as its failure. The realist reply in §2.4 is a placement, not a performative refutation; §15.1 (i) makes the disavowal of transcendental argument explicit.

Fourth, J is disjunctive (§2.1). Fifth, J is primarily epistemic (§2.1). Sixth, J is abductively defensible, not a transcendental deduction, and is necessary but not sufficient for a non-arbitrary relation (§2.1, §2.2b): it must be paired with the Floor and assessed against SY1–SY3. The resource-asymmetry case in §2.1 shows the stronger claim: even a genuine J-and-Floor society can be unjust.

Seventh, symbiosis is a pro tanto floor, not a complete ideal, and the choice of floor is derived and defended (§5.3). The framework does not claim that a relation meeting SY1–SY3 is therefore good. It claims that a relation failing them is not good. The derivation from the thin standing claim shows that SY1–SY3 are the three distinct ways standing can be made vacuous. SY1–SY3 are not fully independent, but independent enough to do work that one indicator could not (§5.1), and SY1 is weighted below the structural indicators (iii) and (iv) in §15.2b because adaptive preferences can produce convergence for the wrong reason. The pro tanto formulation is the honest form of the claim: it binds those who accept the framework's premises, and does not pretend to bind those who reject them.

Eighth, care and respect are internal to the framework's premises, conditionally (§5.4, §15.1 (iii)). Responsibility without care becomes control; care without respect for the incomprehensible becomes paternalism. Both failures are caught by the framework's own operational indicators (SY1 and SY2), and both are consequences of premises the framework already holds. The conditionality: A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint are conditional on F; SY1–SY3 are derived from thin standing alone. The framework is weaker without F, and §2.0 and §15.1 (iii) say so.

Ninth, one proposed contribution is the epistemic burden principle (§5.2). The principle argues that asymmetric influence can shape both the decision and the evidence used to assess it. The proposed response is a proportionate burden to seek independent scrutiny and meaningful correction from affected parties or legitimate representatives. It is not claimed as a necessary condition of knowledge, nor as a gap that the major traditions have simply overlooked. Its value depends on whether it improves the allocation and design of answerability in concrete cases. The argument has five defeaters (B1–B5) and remains open to counterexample.

Tenth, the framework is a diagnostic, not a procedure (§15.3), with four specified falsifying outcomes. The framework acknowledges three structural limits it cannot close (§15.2): Harmonious Domination, Non-formulable harm, and Unreadable dominance. Each is a case where a procedure can be followed completely and something valuable is still destroyed. The framework cannot prevent this. What it can do is name it in advance, and specify what would count as refutation rather than application.

Eleventh, and most importantly, the apophatic constraint (§15.2b) answers the hardest question the framework faces: can a superior intelligence preserve a being's existence, freedom and development without understanding what the being is — and without making its own understanding the measure? The honest answer is that it cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure. What it can do is preserve the structural conditions under which the imposition is nameable, revisable and resistible. Four conditions follow: A1–A4. Each has an operational test, resistance is identifiable by four indicators, and the heads-I-win problem is broken by structural confirmation — sources (iii) and (iv) in §15.2b carry more confirmatory weight than SY1. The strong claim about imposition is softened: imposition cannot be certified away, but it can be made thin, revisable and correctable. A1–A4 are not self-enforcing: five named failure modes specify how they can be satisfied in form and violated in substance. And in the Limit 3 case — where no party outside the superior can verify compliance — A1–A4 do not bind at all; §15.2b states this as a limit, not a solution. These are not a procedure. They are what modesty looks like when it is operationalised. They do not solve the problem of domination. They keep it open, where it can be kept open.

The framework's methodological constraint (§2.2b, §15.4) remains: it states, in advance, four defeaters for the fallibilist premise (§2.0), four defeaters for J, five defeaters for the epistemic burden principle, four defeaters for §5.4's claim that care and respect are internal, four defeaters for the apophatic constraint plus its five failure modes, and five proposed evaluation conditions for its activation rule, and it requires preregistration for the tests. The tests are proposed, not performed. The consolidated list is in §15.6. A framework that cannot be replaced cannot be corrected — see §15.5.

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with.
