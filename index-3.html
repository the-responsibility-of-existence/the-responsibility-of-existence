Abstract

In one sentence: when you exercise discretionary influence over the conditions under which others live, choose and develop, you owe them an account of it, and you cannot be the sole judge of whether that account is good enough.

The framework. The essay sets out a framework for deciding when influence creates a duty to answer, how demanding that duty is, who must discharge it, and how it can be discharged without turning into new domination. The argument is built in layers so that criticism can be aimed at the right one. The framework states, in advance, five conditions under which its activation rule should be revised or abandoned (§2.2, §15.2). This is a methodological constraint, not a claim to have solved the philosophy of method: it makes the framework easier to correct, not proven correct.

1. Ground. A thin justificatory core (J): arrangements that systematically constrain the conditions of others' lives need justification to those affected, and that justification must be open to their contestation. J is reached by four independent routes, and their convergence is evidence for J rather than a proof of it (§2.3). The duty it generates is triggered by exercised influence, scaled by stakes and contestation deficit, and allocated by capacity (§3). §3.3 demonstrates the exercised-capacity principle's advantage over Young's social connection model in a case where the two frameworks diverge.

2. Standing. Who is owed an account, including beings that cannot speak for themselves, handled through four tiers of standing under uncertainty with an explicit plausibility threshold (§4). Symbiosis is specified with observable indicators, so that the framework's ideal is as contestable as its procedure (§5.1).

3. Activation. One rule, not two: a discretion gate plus three drivers (entrenchment, severity, contestation deficit) that determine one of three levels of duty, with anchors and a rating protocol that make the judgments visible and contestable (§6–9).

4. Structure. A floor of minimum conditions, the prohibition on being sole judge of one's own influence, the reviewer regress treated as a design problem, and accounts of conflict, future generations and learning (§10–13).

5. Application and testing. Worked cases including one that appears to refute the framework, five falsification conditions with independent measures and provisional thresholds for the activation rule, and modular conditions for replacement (§14–15).

Answerability is the procedure. Symbiosis is the ideal it serves. Relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation — that is what the framework aims at. It does not promise that symbiosis will be achieved. It specifies what actors owe when their influence shapes the conditions under which others live, and it leaves the question of whether a better relation can be built to the practices of contestation it describes.

The framework has not been empirically tested. Its activation rule is specified so that it can be (§15.2); its other parts are argued and offered for contestation.

Part I

Problem, Scope and Ground

1.The Problem, Scope and Terms

The capacity to shape other people's conditions has grown faster than the practices for answering for it.

Recommendation systems, credit scoring, clinical triage tools and AI assistants shape the conditions of millions. So do states writing policy and companies setting terms, and, at a different scale, clinicians, teachers, landlords and parents. Existing moral vocabulary covers parts of this territory: blame for wrongful acts, rights for protected interests, consent for voluntary exchanges, regulation for what law has already caught. What falls between them is influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act, and that nonetheless determines what others can do and become.

1.1 What the framework does and does not do

It answers four questions: when does influence create a duty to give an account; how demanding is that duty; who must discharge it; and how can review be organised so that it does not become a new instrument of domination. It does not tell anyone what to decide. It is not a decision theory, and it is not a substitute for law, rights or substantive standards of justice. It is meant to be used alongside them, as an account of what actors owe to those they affect, and to whom.

1.2 Terms

Influence: the exercise or maintenance of control over conditions under which others act, choose, develop or fare. Maintaining a structure one could alter, such as keeping a known-flawed system running, counts as influence. Mere ability to help does not (§3).

Actor: a person, organisation or institution, including those that design, deploy and maintain technical systems. Technical systems are treated as instruments, and answerability falls on the humans and organisations behind them (whether some systems may themselves be affected parties is taken up in §4).

Affected party: any being whose interests are materially affected by the influence.

Discretion: the actor has genuine alternatives at tolerable cost and a role in setting the influence, rather than merely executing a rule it had no part in making or contesting.

Answerability: a duty to give reasons for the conditions one helps create or maintain, to those affected or their representatives; to permit examination of those reasons; and to remain open to correction. It is distinct from blame (a judgment about a particular wrongful act), from liability (a legal or financial consequence) and from authority (a right to decide).

1.3 Relation to existing work

The framework is a synthesis and an operationalisation, and its debts are large: Pettit and Lovett on domination, Forst on the right to justification, Scanlon on reasonable rejection, Young on structural responsibility, Bovens and Shoemaker on accountability and answerability, Tronto, Held and Goodin on care and vulnerability. What it offers in combination is (i) a single activation rule that ties the duty to features of the influence and derives that rule from the ground, (ii) an allocation of duties by capacity that prevents scapegoating, (iii) an explicit rating protocol, (iv) a demonstration of ECP's advantage over the social connection model in a decisive case (§3.3), and (v) stated failure and replacement conditions for the activation rule (§2.2, §15). Whether the combination earns its keep is for readers to judge.

  • The framework answers four questions: when influence creates a duty to answer, how demanding the duty is, who must discharge it, and how review can be organised without becoming new domination.
  • It is meant to be used alongside law, rights and substantive standards of justice — not as a substitute for them.

2.The Ground and Its Support

The framework needs a thin principle, a stated set of failure conditions for its central instrument, and several directions from which the principle can be reached.

2.1 The core principle (J)

Any arrangement that systematically and non-trivially constrains the conditions under which others act, choose or develop stands in need of justification to those affected, and that justification must be genuinely open to their contestation.

J is deliberately thin. It does not say what is good, which outcomes are required, or who prevails when interests conflict. It says what kind of relationship must exist between those who shape conditions and those who live in them: one of giving and testing reasons rather than of mere imposition.

2.2 A methodological constraint: stating failure conditions

A framework that cannot state the conditions under which its central instrument would fail is harder to correct. This framework states them in advance.

The prevailing practice in normative political philosophy is to argue for a position by showing that it captures considered judgments, survives counterexamples and coheres with other commitments. That practice has produced enduring work. It has also produced frameworks that are difficult to assess: when an objection arises, the framework is refined; when a case resists, the case is distinguished; when the empirical record disappoints, the framework is said to be ideal-theoretic. The question of what would count as evidence against a framework is rarely asked, and even more rarely answered in advance.

This essay does not claim to solve that problem. It makes a narrower move: it states, in advance and in operational form, five conditions under which its central instrument — the activation rule of §6–9 — should be revised or abandoned. These conditions are given in full in §15.2. They are not presented here as a contribution to the philosophy of method; falsifiability in the technical sense is contested in the philosophy of science, and this essay takes no position on that dispute. What is claimed is smaller: that a framework claiming applicability to real decisions is easier to correct if it states its failure conditions before the test is run.

Two limitations follow. First, the thresholds are placeholders to be fixed beforehand by whoever runs the test; what matters is the structure — independent measures, matched baselines, pre-specified conditions — not the specific numbers. Second, the conditions test the activation rule, not the whole framework. The ground (§2.1, §2.3), the standing tiers (§4), the floor (§10) and the review conditions (§11) are assessed by argument and by contestation, not by the tests in §15.2. A framework that claimed to be falsifiable in every part would be claiming more than any normative framework can deliver.

  • The framework states, in advance, five conditions under which the activation rule should be revised or abandoned (§15.2).
  • This is a methodological constraint, not a contribution to the philosophy of method. Falsifiability in the technical sense is contested, and the essay takes no position on that dispute.
  • The conditions apply to the activation rule, not to every part of the framework. The ground, standing, floor and review conditions are assessed by argument and contestation.

2.3 Why J: convergence as evidence

J is supported by four independent routes. Their convergence is evidence for J, not a proof of it.

The framework does not rest on a single theory. It rests on the observation that J is supported from several independent starting points. This is sometimes presented defensively: if one route fails, the others stand. That is one function of the convergence, but not the only one. When traditions that disagree about much else arrive at the same thin practical principle, that is a reason to take the principle seriously.

  • The justification route. If principles governing relations among agents must be justifiable to each of them (Scanlon 1998; Forst 2012), then constraint that cannot be justified to the constrained treats them as objects of management rather than as participants. This route grounds J in the nature of practical reason.
  • The republican route. The distinctive wrong of domination is the standing capacity to interfere arbitrarily, that is, without being forced to track the interests of those affected (Pettit 1997, 2012; Lovett 2010). Contestability is the remedy, because it forces tracking. This route grounds J in the nature of freedom.
  • The epistemic route. Actors are fallible, partial about their own influence, and prone to self-serving judgment. Requiring them to explain and to expose their reasons to challenge improves decisions by almost any standard of what is good. This route grounds J in the conditions of reliable judgment, and it is available to a consequentialist as an institutional implication, to a Kantian as a publicity requirement, and to a virtue theorist as an expression of humility and justice. It is the old principle that no one should be judge in their own cause, applied to influence.
  • The relational route. Obligations arise within dependence. Those with power over dependents carry special responsibilities, and justification must attend to the particular situation of the vulnerable rather than to an abstract agent (Tronto 1993; Held 2006; Goodin 1985). This route grounds J in the nature of care, and it also corrects the others: what must be justified, and how, depends on what the affected actually need.

What convergence shows, and what it does not. It does not prove that J is true in a correspondence sense. It is a reason to accept J, not a demonstration that J must be accepted. The analogy with structural realism in the philosophy of science (Worrall 1989; Ladyman 1998) is worth mentioning because it illustrates the shape of the claim, but it is an analogy and not an argument: structural realism concerns the preservation of mathematical structure across theory change, and the demand for contestability is not a mathematical structure. The claim here is more modest. Four independent routes to J make J more defensible than any one route alone would, and they raise the cost of rejecting J: a critic must reject it on all four routes, not just one.

What convergence does not show. It does not show that the four routes are fully independent. They share a history, and a critic could argue that their convergence is an artefact of that history rather than evidence of J's strength. That is a real objection, and it is the one the essay invites (§15.1, and the call for comments in §Discussion). The reply is not that the objection is wrong but that it is an empirical claim about intellectual history, and it can in principle be tested: if the four routes can be shown to have developed without mutual influence, the convergence is stronger evidence; if they can be shown to be four re-descriptions of one route, it is weaker. The essay does not claim to have settled this question. It claims that the question is answerable, and that a framework built on four routes is stronger than one built on one.

Why this matters for the framework's status. A framework built on a single contested theory is only as strong as that theory. A framework built on four routes is stronger than any one of its supports. Whoever rejects J must reject it on all four, and explain why the convergence is illusory. That is a higher burden than rejecting a single premise, and it is the burden the framework's ground is designed to impose — not as a proof, but as a reason to take the framework seriously before rejecting it.

  • J is supported by four independent routes: justificatory, republican, epistemic and relational.
  • Their convergence is evidence for J, not a proof of J. The analogy with structural realism illustrates the shape of the claim but is not an argument.
  • Whoever rejects J must reject it on all four routes and explain why the convergence is illusory.

2.4 Rivals, taken in their best forms

Consequentialism. Sophisticated consequentialists can accept J instrumentally. The real disagreement is whether J is basic or derived, and it matters where aggregate welfare seems to favour overriding contestation. There the framework sides with contestation in severe cases, for epistemic reasons (unaccountable override has a poor record), while permitting temporary override in emergencies subject to sunset and review (§12.2). This is a genuine disagreement, not one the framework claims to have refuted.

Kantianism. It shares the core but grounds standing in rational agency, which creates problems for infants, the cognitively disabled and animals. §4 handles these through tiers rather than a single criterion.

Virtue ethics. It supports the humility the framework requires and is indispensable for the character of those who apply it, but it is weak on structural questions: who reviews whom, and by what procedure.

Justice as fairness and capabilities. Rawls's focus on the basic structure and the capabilities approach (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006) supply substantive accounts of what is at stake for people, and Nussbaum extends hers to animals and disabled persons. The framework is deliberately procedural and can draw on these accounts to calibrate severity (§9). The cost is stated in §2.6: a process can be followed while outcomes are bad.

Rights. Rights are outputs, and the framework treats them as external constraints that it does not replace. What it adds is an account of when and how actors must justify influence in the large space that rights and regulation leave open.

2.5 Traditions that start elsewhere

Ubuntu-inspired moral theory (Metz 2007), Confucian role ethics (Ames 2011) and many Indigenous relational ontologies (Watts 2013; de la Cadena 2015; Kimmerer 2013) begin from relational personhood and obligations to kin, community and land rather than from the individual claimant. They tend to arrive at related demands on those who hold power over dependents, by different grounds. The framework does not derive these from J. It treats them as independent support for its operative core. They also press two challenges: that the affected party may be a community rather than an individual, so that contestation and representation may be collective (§4.6, §10); and that the boundary of who counts is not settled by any one tradition (§4). Where such traditions hold that land or ecosystems have standing in themselves, the framework takes no side on that question but allows such values to enter through representation (§4.1).

2.6 Costs

J is thin, so it underdetermines outcomes. The framework presupposes a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected; it is therefore not neutral among all comprehensive doctrines, and it does not pretend to be. Its aim is breadth of acceptability rather than neutrality. And because the ground is a cluster of supports rather than a proof, it is open to the objection that the cluster is shared prejudice. The reply is the modular design of §15.3: the ground can be replaced without discarding the rest, provided the replacement does the same work.

  • The core principle (J) is deliberately thin: arrangements that systematically constrain others' conditions need justification open to their contestation.
  • J is supported by four independent routes — justificatory, republican, epistemic, relational — and their convergence is offered as evidence, not as proof.
  • J is not a proof. Its costs are stated, not concealed, and it is modular by design.

3.The Exercised-Capacity Principle

Influence triggers the duty, stakes and contestation scale it, and capacity allocates it.

The framework separates three different questions that a single slogan ("responsibility follows capacity") runs together.

An actor that exercises or maintains discretionary influence over the conditions of others' lives owes them an account of it. How much it owes scales with the stakes and with the affected parties' difficulty in contesting the influence (§7). The burden of establishing the mechanisms through which the account is given falls on actors in proportion to their capacity to establish them (§8.2).

Trigger: exercised or maintained influence, not mere capacity. Having the power to help does not create the duty. Using or maintaining power over others' conditions does. This separates the principle from the demanding duty of rescue (Singer 1972) and from the overdemandingness that critics press against capacity-based views (Murphy 2000). Answerability is also a modest duty: to give an account and be correctable, not to maximise welfare.

Scale: stakes and contestation deficit. How much is owed depends on how much is at stake and how hard it is for the affected to challenge the influence.

Allocation: capacity to discharge. Who must build review, appeal and audit mechanisms depends on who can build them. A front-line worker can explain her decisions. She cannot establish an independent review board.

3.1 Why not the rivals

Causal responsibility is necessary for attribution but insufficient for structural influence. Where outcomes are produced by many hands and long chains, no single act is the cause (Thompson 1980). The framework keeps causation as an input (foreseeability, §6) but attaches the duty to those with discretion over the design and maintenance of the structure.

Fault-based responsibility concerns blame. Answerability does not presuppose fault; it is one of the distinct notions that Shoemaker (2011) separates from attributability and accountability. The framework leaves blame to other practices.

Relational responsibility is real, and the framework retains it: dependence raises the contestation deficit and so raises the level of duty. But relational duties alone say nothing about anonymous influence, such as a platform's effects on people with whom it has no relationship.

Consent is valuable where it is available and meaningful. Under asymmetry it can be manufactured or coerced, so consent is treated as evidence about the affected party's voice (an input into §7), not as a precondition of the duty.

The social connection model (Young 2011) is the closest rival, and the framework is largely compatible with it: responsibility arises from participation in structures and is shared and forward-looking. The framework adds a discretionary trigger, which stops responsibility diffusing to everyone, and a specification of what is owed and at what level. It can be read as an operationalisation of Young's insights on a justificatory ground. §3.3 demonstrates the advantage in a decisive case.

3.2 The cost: answerability can legitimise power

A principle that makes the powerful responsible risks being used by them as a badge of legitimacy. Three constraints apply. Responsibility is not authority: the more influence one has, the stronger the reason to limit one's own discretion (§8, §16). Review must meet independence conditions (§11). And the risk is tested directly: one of the falsification conditions in §15 is that the framework is in practice used to legitimise decisions without improving outcomes. The risk cannot be eliminated. It can be constrained and watched for — and §11.4 specifies how.

3.3 The exercised-capacity principle against the social connection model: a decisive case

A general claim about allocation is worth little without a case in which it does work that the closest rival cannot.

§3.1 stated, in general terms, that ECP adds a discretionary trigger that stops responsibility from diffusing to everyone. That is a claim. Here is the case that shows it — and shows why ECP is not merely compatible with Young's social connection model (2011) but an improvement on it.

The case. A recommendation algorithm on a large platform amplifies content that increases political polarisation. The amplification is documented, persistent and harmful at the population level. Who bears the duty to answer for it?

Young's answer. Everyone who participates in the structure that produces the harm shares forward-looking responsibility. That includes the engineers who wrote the ranking code, the executives who set engagement targets, the advertisers who fund the model, the regulators who permitted it, and — on some readings — the users whose clicks trained it. Responsibility is shared and political: it is discharged through collective action, not individual allocation. The strength of this answer is its fidelity to how structural harm is actually produced. Its weakness is that it does not tell any of these parties what they owe, to whom, or by when.

ECP's answer. The trigger is exercised discretionary influence over the conditions of others. The users have no discretion over the ranking code; their clicks are inputs, not choices about the structure. The advertisers have discretion over whether to fund, not over how the ranking works. The engineers and executives have discretion over both. So the duty attaches, at Level 3 (§8), to the design team and the executives who set the targets, and at Level 2 to the regulators who could have required audit but did not. The users are affected parties, not duty-bearers.

Why this is a decisive advantage, not a quibble. Young's model has been criticised for exactly this: if everyone is responsible, no one is. The critique is not that Young is wrong about structure — she is right that harm is produced structurally, and the framework retains that insight. The critique is that her model gives no allocation: it tells us that responsibility is shared but not who must do what by when. ECP keeps the structural insight and adds the allocation that the model lacks. The test of the claim is not philosophical but practical: put both frameworks in front of a regulator and ask which one tells her what to demand, of whom, and by when. Young's model says "facilitate collective action". ECP says "the design team owes Level 3; the regulator owes Level 2; here are the indicators". That difference is the framework's reason to exist.

What ECP owes Young. The framework does not claim to have refuted Young. It claims to have operationalised her insight on a justificatory ground, and to have added the allocation her model leaves open. If Young's model can be given an allocation that does the same work without a discretionary trigger, the framework's advantage disappears. That is a live question, and it is the one the call for comments invites (§Discussion). The claim here is not that ECP has won. It is that ECP has entered a contest it can lose, on grounds that are specified in advance.

  • Young's social connection model identifies the structural production of harm but does not allocate responsibility.
  • ECP retains the structural insight and adds allocation by exercised discretion.
  • The advantage is demonstrated in a concrete case (platform amplification), not asserted in general terms.
  • ECP does not claim to have refuted Young; it claims to have entered a contest it can lose, on grounds specified in advance.

Part II

Who Is Owed an Account

4.Standing Under Uncertainty

Incomprehensibility is not evidence of unimportance, but neither is it evidence of importance. The framework needs a threshold.

4.1 Four tiers

  • Tier A: agents. Beings with normative agency (persons). J applies to them directly.
  • Tier B: welfare subjects. Beings with interests of their own that can go better or worse for them: sentient animals, and humans who lack agency, such as infants. Principles must be justifiable to a representative of their interests, who asks what could reasonably be rejected from their standpoint.
  • Tier C: uncertain candidates. Entities for which there is a non-negligible, evidence-based probability of Tier B status. The duty is pro tanto precaution, proportionate to the probability and to the stakes of being wrong (Birch 2024 develops this approach for sentience).
  • Tier D: non-subjects. Entities with no credible interests of their own: stones, rivers and ecosystems as such. The framework claims no direct standing for them. They matter through the interests of beings in Tiers A–C who depend on them, through future generations (§12), and through values that people hold non-instrumentally, which enter legitimately by representation. The framework takes no side on whether ecosystems have intrinsic value. This is an acknowledged limit, and some traditions hold otherwise (§2.5).

4.2 The plausibility threshold

The test for Tier C is whether there is a credible, evidence-based reason to think the entity has states that matter to it: behavioural and physiological indicators of valenced experience, evolutionary or functional continuity with known subjects, or features associated with serious theories of consciousness. "Credible" means supported by evidence or a plausible mechanism, not by mere logical possibility. Without such a threshold, precaution generates reasons everywhere and guides nowhere, which is the standard objection to precautionary principles (Sunstein 2005). The reasons it does generate are weak, defeasible and revisable as evidence changes.

4.3 The stone, restated

The stone survives in this essay as an emblem, but a different one. It marks the edge of the framework's claims. For beings that meet the threshold, our failure to understand their way of being is no reason to dismiss their interests. For the stone, which does not meet it, the framework claims no standing and says so. What it protects are the interests of those who depend on stones, rivers and soils, and the options of those who come after us. That is a smaller claim than a metaphysics of stone-consciousness would make, and a defensible one.

4.4 Artificial systems

Answerability for the influence of AI systems falls on the humans and organisations that design, deploy and maintain them. Whether some artificial systems may themselves be Tier C candidates is contested, and the framework does not answer it. It asks that the question be assessed on evidence, that the assessment be revisited as evidence changes, and that both reflexive dismissal and uncritical attribution be avoided.

4.5 Perspectival plurality

For beings in Tiers A–C, their way of experiencing the world may be difficult or impossible for us to reach. That a perspective is inaccessible is not evidence that it is absent, and testimony from unfamiliar speakers is often discounted for reasons unrelated to its truth (Fricker 2007). This is the content of the recognition condition in §10.

4.6 Representation

Some affected parties cannot take part directly: future generations, infants, animals, communities that lack institutional voice. Representation is legitimate only if (i) representatives are chosen through a process that can itself be contested, (ii) they state the basis on which they claim to know the interests concerned, (iii) where interests are contested there is more than one representative or perspective, (iv) they can be removed, (v) they are neither the actor nor dependent on the actor for appointment or funding, and (vi) they represent uncertainty rather than substituting invented certainty. Where the represented can express preferences, those take precedence over the representative's view of their best interests. Where they cannot, intervention is limited to what is necessary, in the least restrictive form. Collective bodies, such as communities and peoples, may be parties to contestation in their own right.

  • Four tiers of standing: agents, welfare subjects, uncertain candidates, non-subjects.
  • Tier C requires a plausibility threshold: a credible, evidence-based reason to think the entity has states that matter to it.
  • The stone marks the edge of the framework's claims. No direct standing is claimed for it, but dependence, future generations and held values are protected.
  • Representation of silent parties is legitimate only under six explicit conditions.

5.Interdependence, Difference and Non-Domination

Existence is relational. Symbiosis is the ideal that relational existence can fail to meet — and it must be measurable if the framework is to be taken seriously.

Living beings, institutions, technologies and ecosystems exist through relations of dependence and influence. Existence is relational: no being exists except through relations of dependence, influence and constitution — a child through care, an ecosystem through nutrient cycles, a person through language and recognition. This is not a metaphysical claim about the nature of being; it is an empirical and phenomenological one. What follows from it is that the conditions of one being's existence are partly constituted by others' actions.

Some of these relations are reciprocal and enabling, others unequal, extractive or harmful. The word "symbiosis" is used here with care. In biology it covers mutualism but also parasitism, so it cannot by itself carry a normative meaning. In this essay it names a normative ideal: relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§10).

Answerability (§3) is the procedure by which this ideal is pursued. The framework does not promise that symbiosis will be achieved; it specifies what actors owe when their influence shapes the conditions under which others live. Symbiosis is the horizon. Answerability is the road.

Harmony enforced by silencing, homogenising or sacrificing the vulnerable is not symbiosis. A system that maintains stability by eliminating differences or sacrificing vulnerable participants is not symbiotic — it is domination with a friendly face. Difference is not a defect to be corrected. Different beings may perceive reality through entirely different capacities. A more advanced understanding of the world may reveal previously invisible relationships, but it may also create new blind spots. The ability to explain something does not mean one has understood its full significance.

This is why difference should be approached with curiosity rather than automatic correction, and with respect rather than the assumption that what is unfamiliar must be inferior. The asymmetry also runs both ways: the more capable may misunderstand the less capable, and the less capable cannot audit the more capable. That second half is why independent capacity for review (§11) matters.

5.1 Making symbiosis measurable (or admitting it is not)

If the framework states failure conditions for its activation rule, its own ideal cannot be exempt from assessment.

Symbiosis was defined above as a normative ideal: relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation. If the framework's stated failure conditions (§2.2) are taken seriously, symbiosis cannot remain a purely rhetorical horizon. Either it has observable indicators, or the framework must soften its claim to the ideal.

Three indicators, each observable.

  • Non-exploitation (S1). The dependent party's continued participation does not require it to accept terms that it would reject if it had exit at tolerable cost. Measured by: the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, adjusted for exit costs. A high gap indicates exploitation; a low gap indicates that the relation is not extractive, whatever else it is.
  • Non-assimilation (S2). The dependent party's difference is not treated as a defect to be corrected. Measured by: whether the actor's interventions aim at removing the difference or at removing the burden the difference carries. A programme that seeks to make deaf children hearing is assimilation; a programme that provides sign language and accessible environments is not. The indicator is observable in the design of interventions, not in the intentions of actors.
  • Continued possibility (S3). The dependent party retains the conditions for continued existence, development and participation. Measured by: whether F1–F5 (§10) hold over time, not just at a single decision. A relation that satisfies F1–F5 at one moment and violates them at the next is not symbiotic; it is a relation that happened to be benign once.

What this does, and what it does not do. It does not make symbiosis a score. It makes it contestable in the same way the activation rule is: with anchors, with counter-profiles, with independent raters. If a relation scores low on S1–S3 across repeated assessments, the framework says it is not symbiotic — whatever the actors claim. If a relation scores high, the framework does not say it is symbiotic; it says the observable indicators are consistent with it. That is a smaller claim than the essay's rhetoric sometimes suggests, and it is the claim the framework can actually defend.

Why this matters for the framework's integrity. The framework requires actors to answer for their influence. It would be incoherent for the framework's own ideal to be exempt from that requirement. Making symbiosis measurable is not a concession to positivism; it is the framework applying to itself the standard it applies to others. A framework whose central ideal cannot be assessed is a framework with a hole in its centre. §5.1 closes the hole — or, if the indicators are rejected, shows exactly where the hole is.

  • Existence is relational: no being exists except through relations of dependence, influence and constitution.
  • Symbiosis is a normative ideal: relations of dependence in which dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation.
  • Symbiosis is given three observable indicators: non-exploitation (S1), non-assimilation (S2), continued possibility (S3).
  • The framework applies to its own ideal the standard it applies to actors: if symbiosis cannot be assessed, the framework has a hole in its centre.

Part III

When, and How Much

6.The Influence Profile

Five components, each assessed against observable indicators.

The framework merges a profile of influence and a threshold into one profile:

Table 1. The five components of the influence profile
Component Role Guiding question
Discretion (D) Gate Does the actor have genuine alternatives and a role in setting this influence?
Entrenchment (E) Driver How long do the consequences last, and how hard are they to undo?
Severity (S) Driver How much is at stake for the affected?
Contestation deficit (C) Driver How hard is it for the affected to exit, object, or even find out what is done to them?
Foreseeability (F) Attribution What could the actor reasonably have known?

Two clarifications. First, persistence refers to the consequences, not to the act. A one-off release of a modified organism is a single act with persistent consequences, so it is highly entrenched. Second, foreseeability does not drive the level. It fixes what the actor must answer for: consequences it foresaw or could with reasonable effort have foreseen. It also generates a duty of inquiry proportional to the stakes. Where the credible worst case is severe and the evidence is thin, the duty to investigate comes before the act. "Credible" has the same meaning as in §4.2: supported by a plausible mechanism or precedent, not by mere possibility.

The five components do not discard a richer profile of influence; they reorganise it. Magnitude and Scope are subsumed within Severity, rated on the most burdened individual first and, for system design, on aggregate population-level effects so that diffuse harm still registers. Asymmetry — differences in power, knowledge and exit — is subsumed within Contestation Deficit. Control, on the actor side, is subsumed within Discretion. Persistence is subsumed within Entrenchment, with the clarification that it refers to the consequences and not to the act. Nothing is lost; the profile is made simpler by being made derivable.

  • Five components: Discretion (gate), Entrenchment, Severity, Contestation deficit (drivers), Foreseeability (attribution).
  • Magnitude and Scope are subsumed within Severity; Asymmetry within Contestation Deficit; Control (actor side) within Discretion; Persistence within Entrenchment.
  • Foreseeability does not drive the level. It fixes what the actor must answer for and generates a duty of inquiry.

7.The Activation Rule

Discretion opens the gate. Entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit set the level.

7.1 The rule

Each driver is rated Low, Moderate or High against the anchors in §9.

  • No discretion: the actor does not bear the duty for this influence. It shifts to whoever set the governing rule or could have. The actor still owes escalation (§8.2).
  • Discretion present: Level 1 always applies.
  • Level 2 applies if any one driver is High, or any two are Moderate.
  • Level 3 applies if S is High and at least one of E or C is High.

Severity is judged first at the individual level, that is, for the most burdened affected party. For decisions about the design of a system that acts on many people, it is also judged at the aggregate level (§9.1). Where an actor lacks the evidence to exclude a credible severe outcome, it rates S on the credible worst case.

7.2 Why these conditions

Each element can be derived from J (§2).

  • Discretion. Justification is owed for what an agent chooses. Where an actor merely executes a rule it could not have set or contested, the demand is properly addressed to whoever set the rule.
  • Entrenchment. What makes mistaken or unjustified influence tolerable is the possibility of correction afterwards. Entrenchment removes that channel, so the justification must come before the act instead of after it.
  • Severity. The strength of a complaint scales with the burden it describes (§12.1). The more is at stake for a person, the stronger their claim to an account.
  • Contestation deficit. Where the affected can exit, object effectively and understand what is done to them, they supply part of the accountability themselves (Hirschman 1970). The deficit measures how much must instead be supplied by the actor and by reviewers.

7.3 What the rule is not

It is not a score and it applies no weights. It is not a verdict either. It yields a presumptive level that can be overridden only with stated reasons (§9.3). It is a decision aid with its joints exposed. A single High on any driver is enough for Level 2 because a single severe feature, such as an opaque system with no exit, can require review even when the stakes seem moderate.

  • Discretion is the gate; E, S and C set the level: L1 always with discretion; L2 if any driver is High or two are Moderate; L3 if S is High and at least one of E or C is High.
  • Each condition is derivable from J, not stipulated.
  • The rule is not a score and not a verdict — it is a presumptive level, overridable only with stated reasons.

8.The Duty Ladder and Who Bears It

Three levels, each containing the one below. Duties are allocated by capacity, not heaped on whoever is nearest.

8.1 The levels

Level 1: Explain. The actor can state, on request and in proportion to the matter, the purpose of the action, the interests considered, the alternatives considered and the known uncertainties.

Level 2: Review and respond. Level 1, plus: an accessible channel for objection; access to the relevant information; a reasoned response within a reasonable time; revision where warranted; a record of decisions and reasons; a reviewer other than the original decider where feasible; and evidence that review is not symbolic, such as a published rate at which review changes outcomes (a rate that stays at zero is a warning sign).

Level 3: Preserve, examine, authorise. Level 2, plus: preserved records and version history; independent examination meeting the conditions of §11; a written assessment of less harmful alternatives; authorisation by a body outside the actor for severe or irreversible influence; staged implementation, reversibility by design or a sunset clause; remedy for those harmed; and post-implementation review. Level 3 authorisation requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation (§11.4).

8.2 Allocation by capacity

The level attaches to the influence, that is, to the arrangement. Duties are then allocated.

Those with the capacity to establish mechanisms (institutions, system owners, funders, regulators) bear the duty to establish Level 2 and Level 3 mechanisms. Others along the chain owe their own Level 1 explanation, honest participation, escalation of concerns and non-obstruction. No scapegoating: a front-line actor is not answerable for structural deficits she cannot cure, though she remains answerable for her own discretion. Where several contribute, responsibility is shared according to discretion and capacity and must remain identifiable (Thompson 1980). Collective responsibility does not dissolve individual accountability.

8.3 Not authority

A higher level means stronger constraints on power, not greater authority. Level 3 does not make the most powerful actor the arbiter of its own case.

  • Three levels: L1 explain, L2 review and respond, L3 preserve, examine and authorise.
  • Level 3 requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation (§11.4).
  • The level attaches to the arrangement; duties are allocated by capacity. No scapegoating.
  • Higher level means stronger constraints, not greater authority.

9.Rating Protocol: Anchors, Hygiene and Sensitivity

Judgment cannot be eliminated. It can be made visible, checkable and contestable.

9.1 Anchors

Anchors are phrased as observable indicators.

Table 2. Anchors for the three drivers
Driver Low Moderate High
Entrenchment Consequences brief, or easily undone by those affected at trivial cost; no lock-in Consequences last months to years, or undoing them takes substantial cost or effort; some lock-in Effectively permanent or intergenerational; undoing is impossible or prohibitive; closes off future options
Severity Convenience, preference or easily substitutable goods Significant interests (income, opportunity, non-life-threatening health, important relationships), remediable Life, health, basic needs, basic agency, development; severe exclusion from essential goods
Contestation deficit Affected can exit at tolerable cost, know what is done, and have voice channels that lead to correction One of exit, information or effective voice meaningfully impaired Two or more impaired, or no exit and no effective voice

Severity is rated on the most burdened individual first. Breadth does not raise individual severity, which keeps trivial effects on many people from being rated High. For decisions about system design, aggregate severity is rated on population-level effects, so that diffuse harm still registers. The vulnerability of those affected raises severity by one step where there is reason to think the same burden weighs more heavily on them. Capabilities-style accounts of what is at stake for people (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006) can be used to calibrate these ratings.

9.2 Hygiene

  1. Rate before looking at the level the rule produces and before deciding what the actor wants to do.
  2. Use at least two raters, one independent of the actor where Level 2 or above is in question, and record reasons for each component.
  3. Record disagreements instead of averaging them. Identify the indicator that drives the disagreement (exit? information?). Escalate to an independent reviewer only if the disagreement is material to the level.
  4. Sensitivity test. Move each rating one step up and down. If the level changes, the case is borderline and is recorded as such. For a borderline case whose credible worst-case severity is High, apply the higher level. Otherwise apply the lower level with monitoring and a stated trigger for re-rating.
  5. Calibration. Compare the profile with the reference cases in §14 to check consistency.

9.3 Reasoned override

The presumptive level may be overridden up or down only with written reasons. Overrides are recorded and, where safe, published. They are data for the testing described in §15.

9.4 Counter-profiles

Any affected party or representative may file an alternative profile, and the actor must respond to the specific differences. This is how the framework's own categories stay contestable (§11.4).

  • Anchors are observable indicators, not scores.
  • Hygiene: rate before knowing the outcome; two raters; record disagreements; run the sensitivity test; calibrate against reference cases.
  • Overrides require written reasons and are data for testing.
  • Counter-profiles keep the framework's own categories contestable.

Part IV

Structure: Floor, Review, Power

10.The Floor: Minimum Conditions in Relationships of Dependence

Continued existence is not enough. A being can stay alive or operational while losing any say over its own circumstances.

The Floor applies in relationships of significant dependence, where the affected rely on the actor for essential goods, care, information or conditions. It applies independently of the activation rule, because dependence can be significant even when a particular decision is low-stakes.

  • F1 — Exit or voice. Where possible, a meaningful opportunity to withdraw, refuse or communicate disagreement.
  • F2 — Contestation. Decisions are open to challenge regardless of the intelligence, authority or technical capability of the decider. Contestation may be collective.
  • F3 — Development. Opportunities for learning, adaptation and growth are preserved.
  • F4 — Non-foreclosure. The future possibilities of others are not unnecessarily closed.
  • F5 — Recognition. Interests and possible experiences are acknowledged rather than dismissed because they are hard to understand (Fricker 2007).

F1 and F2 are the mirror image of the contestation deficit in §7: where they fail, C rises.

10.1 Protection without captivity

Intervention may be justified to prevent serious harm. It must then be proportionate, as little restrictive as possible, temporary where it can be, subject to review, and aimed at restoring the conditions for meaningful existence rather than establishing permanent control.

  • Five minimum conditions (F1–F5): exit or voice, contestation, development, non-foreclosure, recognition.
  • The Floor applies independently of the activation rule — dependence can be significant even in low-stakes decisions.
  • Protection is not captivity.

11.No Sole Judge: Review, Power and the Regress

No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence. The reviewer regress is a design problem, and it is bounded by finality without infallibility — and by a precondition that converts diagnosis into design.

11.1 The prohibition

An actor exercising substantial influence must remain answerable to forms of review that are meaningfully independent of its own interests and decisions.

This does not assume that the powerful are malicious. Actors are prone to self-serving judgment and are limited in what they can see (§2.3, the epistemic route), and review that is not independent of the reviewed has little claim to legitimacy. The requirement strengthens with the level: at Level 2, a reviewer other than the original decider; at Level 3, independent examination.

11.2 The regress

If every reviewer needs a reviewer, accountability looks like an endless hierarchy. The response has three parts.

  • Finality without infallibility. Working systems end the regress with decisions that are final for practical purposes but can be reopened on new evidence, on new standing, on demonstrated procedural failure, or at a sunset. Courts and constitutional review work this way. The aim is not a reviewer beyond challenge but that no position is permanently exempt from it.
  • Contestation is not adjudication. The reviewed and the affected may challenge a reviewer's independence or competence before a differently constituted body. Neither they nor the actor can veto the outcome.
  • Plurality and publicity instead of a top. Several reviewers with different standpoints, publishing their reasons, make decentralised scrutiny by press, researchers and civil society possible (cf. Ostrom 1990 on polycentric governance). For silent parties, adversarial representation does the work (§4.6).

11.3 Design conditions for review

  • R1 Independent appointment, funding and removal. Not controlled by the reviewed; fixed terms; removal only for cause by a body the reviewed does not control.
  • R2 Plurality. More than one reviewer or standpoint; adversarial representatives for silent parties; lay members where lived experience matters.
  • R3 Publicity. Reasons and outcomes are public unless a specific reason justifies confidentiality.
  • R4 Contestable reviewers. Reviewers can be challenged on independence and competence before a differently constituted body.
  • R5 Effectiveness. Findings bind or trigger an obligation to respond; dissent is recorded and forwarded.
  • R6 Contestable rules. The rules of review can be amended through a process that includes affected parties.

Capture indicators: reviewers appointed or funded by the reviewed; a record of never dissenting or never reversing; staff moving between reviewer and reviewed; reviewers denied access to information; chronic delay.

11.4 Power over the framework itself: from diagnosis to design

The framework's earlier formulation treated the power regress as a limit it could not eliminate. That was too weak. It has an internal resource against capture, and the resource is T5.

Who defined the categories? Who selected the reviewers? Who can dismiss them? Whose interests are excluded even where formally represented? These four questions are asked in every application (§14, step 6). The deeper problem remains: powerful actors will shape how the framework is applied to them. No framework can eliminate this. But a framework can convert the problem from a concession into a design constraint, and this one does.

The earlier formulation. An earlier version of this section treated T5 (§15.2) as a post-hoc diagnosis: if adopters' review bodies fail the independence conditions (R1) within three years, the framework is being used as a shield. That is too late. By the time the diagnosis is made, the shield has already done its work.

The revised requirement. T5 must be a precondition, not a diagnosis. Level 3 authorisation (§8.1) requires, as a condition of validity, that the authorising body publishes its R1 indicators before authorisation: who appointed its members, who funds it, how members can be removed, and the rate at which its findings have been reversed or ignored. An authorisation issued by a body that cannot or will not publish these indicators is not a Level 3 authorisation. It is a Level 2 authorisation with a Level 3 label.

Why this changes the framework's status. It does not eliminate the power regress — no framework can. But it converts the regress from a concession into a design constraint. An actor that wants Level 3 legitimacy must accept the conditions that make Level 3 legitimacy meaningful. If it refuses, the framework does not collapse; it downgrades the actor's claim. The actor may still exercise influence, but it cannot claim the framework's highest level of legitimacy for doing so. That is a resource Young, Forst and Pettit do not have: their frameworks name the problem of power but cannot, from within, penalise its exercise. This framework can.

What this does not do. It does not solve the problem of who appoints the appointer. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself, or define "independent" in a way that includes its own affiliates. The framework cannot prevent this. What it can do is make the capture visible — because the R1 indicators are published, a captured review body is a documented fact, not a suspicion. Visibility is not prevention, but it is the precondition of contestation, and contestation is the only remedy the framework has ever claimed.

  • No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence.
  • The regress is treated as a design problem: finality without infallibility, contestation not adjudication, plurality and publicity instead of a top.
  • Review must meet six conditions (R1–R6). Capture indicators are named.
  • T5 is a precondition of Level 3 authorisation: R1 indicators must be published before authorisation, not diagnosed after.
  • An actor that refuses the precondition does not collapse the framework; it downgrades its own claim to Level 3 legitimacy.

12.Conflicts, Ecology and Future Generations

Decisions must be justified, not derived.

12.1 Weighing conflicting interests

Neither majority interest nor individual preference automatically prevails. The method is individual comparison: compare the burdens that each alternative places on each affected party, party by party, and favour the alternative that avoids the most serious individual burden. Claims of comparable weight may be aggregated as a tie-breaker (Scanlon 1998). Among the acceptable alternatives, choose the one with minimum necessary harm: lower severity, higher reversibility, fairer distribution of burdens, preserved options, and restoration still possible.

12.2 Emergencies

Where an immediate, serious threat makes the full procedure impossible, an actor may act first, provided it acts only as far as necessary, records its reasons at the time, is bound by an automatic sunset, and submits to review afterwards. Emergencies create no standing exemptions.

12.3 Ecological integrity

Ecosystems cannot be assessed through the preferences of individual participants. Protecting them may require restricting activities, and the justification runs through dependent beings in Tiers A–C, through future generations, and through the non-instrumental values that people hold (§4.1). It does not license arbitrary disregard of sentient beings. Where irreversible ecological damage is a credible possibility, S and E are rated High on the credible worst case, so Level 3 applies with independent ecological review.

12.4 No automatic hierarchy

Intelligence, productivity, population size and technical sophistication do not by themselves determine moral priority. This does not mean that every interest weighs the same. Weight follows the nature and strength of the interests at stake and credible standing, not a being's rank on an imagined scale of worth.

12.5 Future generations

The duty is condition-regarding: to preserve the conditions under which future people can exercise agency and meet basic needs, and a reasonably wide space of valuable options, rather than to fix the choices they will make. Because it concerns conditions and not particular individuals, it does not depend on who will exist (cf. the non-identity problem, Parfit 1984). Option preservation is not absolute. It can conflict with present needs, and not every option is worth keeping, since the option to dominate is not. It operates as a weight, strongest where actions are irreversible. Under uncertainty, actors should acknowledge the limits of their forecasts, communicate them honestly and keep correction possible (cf. Jonas 1984). Institutional examples such as the Future Generations Commissioner created by Wales's Well-being of Future Generations Act 2015 show that representation of the future is practicable. Whether it works is an empirical question of the kind §15 describes.

  • Individual comparison first; aggregation only as tie-breaker; minimum necessary harm among acceptable alternatives.
  • Emergencies create no standing exemptions: sunset, record, review.
  • Ecological integrity is protected through dependent beings, future generations and held values — not through arbitrary disregard of sentient beings.
  • Future generations: preserve conditions and options, not particular choices.

13.Learning, Correction and Reversibility

No actor, institution or intelligence should assume its understanding is complete.

The duty to learn. Where capacity exists and consequences are significant, actors monitor outcomes, investigate unexpected results, listen to criticism and update.

Reversibility by design. Consequential decisions should be built to be revisable: staged implementation, monitoring, independent evaluation, defined conditions for reconsideration, and sunset clauses.

Self-application. The framework is itself an influence on those who adopt it and on those affected by their adoption. It is subject to its own principles. Versions are dated and public, criticism is invited, counter-profiles apply to its own application, and §15 states how it can be tested and replaced. A philosophy that cannot be challenged risks becoming an instrument of the domination it opposes.

  • The duty to learn is a duty where capacity exists and consequences are significant.
  • Reversibility by design reduces the risk that incomplete understanding becomes permanent.
  • The framework is subject to its own principles.

Part V

Application and Testing

14.Application: A Nine-Step Procedure and Worked Cases

The same procedure yields very different obligations for different cases. A framework that gave every case the same answer would be useless.

14.1 The procedure

  1. Identify the relationship. Who is affected, in which tier (§4), and what dependencies exist?
  2. Identify actors and discretion. Who has genuine alternatives? Who set the governing rules?
  3. Rate E, S and C against the anchors (§9.1), and F for attribution. Use two raters and record reasons.
  4. Apply the activation rule (§7) and run the sensitivity test.
  5. Check the Floor (F1–F5, §10).
  6. Analyse power (§11.4): who defined the categories, selected the reviewers, can dismiss them, and whose interests are excluded?
  7. Examine alternatives and unrepresented interests (§12.1).
  8. Allocate duties by capacity (§8.2) and set up review that meets R1–R6. For Level 3, require publication of R1 indicators before authorisation (§11.4).
  9. Reassess on stated triggers, and invite counter-profiles.

14.2 Case: AI triage in intensive care

An illustrative case, not a real one. A hospital deploys an AI system to prioritise ICU admissions during a respiratory pandemic. It scores patients on likelihood of survival and expected resource use. Clinicians can override it but rarely do.

The affected are patients (Tier A), their families, clinicians and public funders. Patients cannot see or choose their score. The hospital chose to deploy the system and the vendor designed it, so discretion is present. Entrenchment is High (death or lasting organ damage), severity is High (life), and contestation deficit is High (no insight into the model, no alternative provider in a crisis, families unable to contest). The sensitivity test does not change the level. Level 3.

What Level 3 requires here: preserved model versions, descriptions of training data, individual scores and override records; an independent ethics board with lay and patient representation (R1–R2); a written comparison with clinician-only triage and rule-based triage such as SOFA; authorisation from the public-health authority; a sunset clause and periodic revalidation; and a remedy for those wrongly denied. On the Floor: patients need voice through an advocate and a route to appeal, a denial must not foreclose later access if a patient stabilises (F4), and patients' own accounts of their condition must be recorded (F5).

Power analysis: the categories were set by the vendor and clinical leadership, and the ethics committee is appointed by the hospital board, which fails R1 unless changed. Interests structurally at risk of exclusion include the poorest patients, who may present late with worse scores, disabled patients whose quality-of-life measures may embed bias, and patients without advocates. On alternatives, clinician-only triage is slower and less consistent but more contestable, and rule-based triage is less predictive but transparent. A hybrid in which the AI recommends and a human decides, with the decision documented, is the default preference because it keeps contestation alive. After six months the hospital compares outcomes and override rates across groups and revises or suspends if disparities appear.

The framework does not decide the case. It tells the hospital what it owes an answer to, and to whom.

14.2b Case: the emergency physician (a counterintuitive result)

Every other case in this essay produces the answer a careful reader would predict. This one does not.

A physician in an emergency department must decide, in under ninety seconds, whether to intubate a patient who is deteriorating. She has genuine discretion (clinical judgment, not protocol). Entrenchment is High (death or lasting harm), severity is High (life), and contestation deficit is High (the patient cannot consent; the family is not present). The activation rule gives Level 3.

The counterintuitive result. Level 3 requires independent authorisation, preserved records and a written assessment of alternatives — before the act. A reader's first reaction is that this is absurd: the physician cannot convene an ethics board in ninety seconds. The framework seems to have produced an impossible demand. If the framework requires what cannot be done, the framework is wrong.

The reply. The Level 3 duty does not fall on the physician in the moment. It falls on the arrangement (§8.2), which means: on the hospital that employs her, the protocols it adopts, the audit trail it maintains, the retrospective review it conducts, and the training it provides. The physician's own duty is Level 1 in the moment (explain, record) and Level 2 afterwards (participate in review, escalate concerns about the protocol). The ninety-second decision is answerable, not authorised in advance. It is authorised by the structure that made it possible, and that structure is the duty-bearer of Level 3.

Why this case is included. Every other case in this essay — ICU triage, platform recommendations, the community health worker, the café menu — produces the answer a careful reader would predict. This one does not. It looks, on first reading, like a reductio of the framework. The framework's reply is that the reader has misidentified the duty-bearer, not that the framework is wrong. A framework that survives its own apparent reductio is stronger than one that only survives confirmation. The case is offered as a test: if the reply is unconvincing, the framework's allocation principle (§8.2) is in trouble, not just its application.

What the case reveals about the framework's structure. The case makes visible a feature that the other cases leave implicit: the framework's Level 3 is not a demand on individual actors in moments of crisis. It is a demand on the arrangements that place actors in crises without the structures that would make their decisions answerable. The physician who intubates in ninety seconds is not failing a Level 3 duty. The hospital that has not built the review structure is failing one. Distinguishing these is what the allocation principle (§8.2) is for, and the case shows why the distinction matters.

  • The emergency physician case appears to refute the framework: Level 3 seems to demand the impossible in ninety seconds.
  • The reply: the Level 3 duty falls on the arrangement, not on the physician in the moment. Her duty is Level 1 in the moment and Level 2 afterwards.
  • The case is included because it is the only one that challenges the reader's intuition. A framework that survives its own apparent reductio is stronger than one that only survives confirmation.

14.3 Case: a platform's recommendation system

For individual recommendations: discretion is present, entrenchment is Low to Moderate (each recommendation fades), severity is Moderate, and the contestation deficit is High (an opaque model, costly exit because of network effects, ineffective voice). One High driver gives Level 2: explanation, a route to contest, a reasoned response.

For the design of the system: entrenchment is Moderate (cumulative patterns), aggregate severity is rated High on the documented effects on attention, mood and public discourse across very large populations, and the contestation deficit is High. S High with C High gives Level 3 for design: preserved version history, independent audit with real data access, published aggregate metrics, authorisation of high-risk optimisations, and a sunset for any optimisation that cannot show population-level reversibility. The aggregate severity rating is the most contestable step, and it should be tested against evidence and open to counter-profiles. "Engagement" is defined by the platform, audits are typically controlled by it unless regulation forces otherwise, and non-users affected through the public sphere are excluded.

14.4 Case: a community health worker

She has little institutional power and is the first point of contact for patients who have no other access to care. Her referral and treatment decisions fall within protocols but involve judgment. Discretion is present, entrenchment is Moderate to High, severity is High, and the contestation deficit is High. The rule says the arrangement requires Level 3.

But she has no capacity to build independent review. Under §8.2, the duty to establish Level 2 and Level 3 mechanisms falls on the programme, the ministry and the funders. Her personal duties are to explain her decisions in terms patients can understand, to respond to objections within her role, and to escalate when protocols fail. She is not answerable for the missing audit trail or appeal channel, which she cannot create. Where she follows a protocol over whose content she has no discretion, the duty to justify the content lies with its designers.

The level belongs to the arrangement, and the duties are allocated by capacity. The framework neither concentrates answerability on the least powerful nor exempts them from explaining themselves. Whether it in practice shifts burdens onto front-line staff is a live risk and is included in the tests of §15.

14.5 Four short cases

  • A one-off release of a modified organism to eliminate a disease vector. Discretion present. Entrenchment is High, because it concerns the consequences and not the single act. Severity is High on the credible worst case, and the affected cannot opt out (C High). Level 3, although the act happens once.
  • A dating platform that systematically penalises users over fifty. Discretion present, entrenchment Moderate, severity Moderate (access to relationships, and age discrimination), contestation deficit High. Level 2. Level 1 alone is insufficient, and Level 3 is not required because severity is not High.
  • A café changes its menu. Discretion present, but entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit are all Low, since customers can see the menu and go elsewhere. Level 1 only, and arguably not even that. The framework says no to most of life.
  • A physician following a clinical protocol. No discretion over the protocol's content, so the duty to justify the content moves to its designers. The physician owes explanation and escalation for how he applies it to the patient in front of him.

15.Limits, Testing and Replacement

This section gives the operational detail behind the failure conditions stated in §2.2: the measures, the thresholds, the limitations and the conditions under which the framework should be replaced.

The framework's failure conditions (§2.2) apply to its activation rule. What follows is their full specification, together with the framework's stated limitations and the conditions under which it should be replaced.

15.1 Limitations

  • Ratings need judgment and the anchors will be contested. The protocol makes this visible and does not remove it.
  • J underdetermines outcomes. A process can be followed while outcomes are bad, so the framework must be used with substantive standards of justice.
  • The ground is a convergence argument and not a proof (§2.3).
  • Representation risks projection and paternalism (mitigated in §4.6, not removed).
  • Review can be captured (§11). T5 as a precondition (§11.4) constrains capture but does not eliminate it.
  • Power over how the framework is applied to powerful actors cannot be fully eliminated.
  • The framework was developed mainly within one intellectual tradition. §2.5 engages others, but wider critique is needed.
  • All claims about its effects are hypotheses. No test has yet been run.
  • Despite the allocation principle, small actors may be over-burdened in practice.

15.2 Five falsification conditions: full specification

The tests use independent measures where possible, are specified in advance, and state provisional thresholds. The thresholds are placeholders to be fixed beforehand by whoever runs the test. What matters is the structure: independent measures, matched baselines, pre-specified thresholds.

  • T1 Reliability. Trained raters independently rate a calibration set of at least 30 diverse cases. If agreement on level (Krippendorff's alpha) is below 0.60, the anchors are revised. If it remains below 0.40 after two revisions, the profile is abandoned as a decision tool. Other parts of the framework may stand.
  • T2 Discriminative validity. The levels fail to track the safeguards that independent expert panels, blind to the framework's output, judge necessary (weighted kappa below 0.50 over at least 30 cases).
  • T3 Effectiveness. In matched or staggered-adoption comparisons over at least two years, adopting institutions fail to improve, relative to controls, on at least two of three independent indicators: time to correct erroneous decisions; share of challenges that lead to change; and affected parties' reported fairness and trust in an independent survey.
  • T4 Harm. Adopters show worse outcomes for the most vulnerable groups than matched controls on independently measured harm indicators, such as denial rates or incident rates. The framework is then suspended and revised.
  • T5 Capture and legitimation. Within three years, a majority of adopters' review bodies fail R1 on documented appointment, funding and removal, or adopters' rate of successful external challenges falls without improvement on the T3 indicators, which would suggest the framework is being used as a shield. As a precondition (§11.4), T5 also requires that Level 3 authorisation be preceded by publication of R1 indicators; an authorisation without such publication is not a Level 3 authorisation.

These tests face confounding and selection problems like any institutional comparison. They are better than none, and they make the framework's claims capable of being wrong. The framework's failure conditions (§2.2) are not a claim that these tests will succeed. They are a commitment to revise or abandon if the tests fail.

15.3 Replacement

The framework is modular. Its ground (Part I), standing (Part II), activation (Part III) and structure (Part IV) can be replaced separately, and Parts III–IV remain usable with a different ground that supports a duty like J. A framework or module F′ should replace the current one if:

  1. F′ improves on at least one independent indicator in §15.2 or resolves a limitation in §15.1.
  2. F′ causes no unacknowledged regression elsewhere. Any trade-off is stated and accepted through a process that includes affected parties.
  3. F′ is operationally applicable and produces more defensible decisions on a substantial set of cases, not just in principle.
  4. F′ is corrigible: it states its own falsification and replacement conditions.
  5. The transition is trialled, with a sunset or a route back.
  6. The incumbent is not privileged. After a trial, the evidence is judged symmetrically, and the framework's only advantage is the cost of transition.

If these are met, F′ should be adopted, because §13 requires it. A framework that cannot name its own replacement conditions is not a framework but a preference.

  • Nine limitations are stated openly, including the limits of T5 as a precondition.
  • Five falsification conditions (T1–T5) with full operational specification and provisional thresholds.
  • T5 is both a diagnosis (three years) and a precondition (R1 publication before Level 3 authorisation).
  • Six replacement conditions — the framework is modular and supersedable by design.

16.Responsibility Without Sovereignty

The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination.

An actor may have greater knowledge, intelligence or capacity. None of these by itself establishes a right to determine the existence of others. Greater capability should create greater restraint. Greater understanding should create greater curiosity about what remains unknown. And greater power should create a stronger obligation to ensure that those affected are not reduced to objects in another's plans.

The principle runs in both directions. A more advanced intelligence may meet forms of life it cannot fully comprehend, and its first responsibility is not to eliminate the unfamiliar. A less advanced one should not be dismissed because it cannot comprehend a more advanced one. But differences in capacity also mean that the less capable cannot audit the more capable, which is why independent technical capacity for review is part of R2.

Answerability is not sovereignty. Understanding is not ownership. Protection is not permission to imprison. And greater intelligence is not proof of greater moral completeness.

  • The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination.
  • Greater capability, understanding and power create stronger obligations to restraint, curiosity and protection — not entitlement.
  • Answerability is not sovereignty.

Conclusion

17. An Open Commitment to Existence

Be responsible for the influence you possess. Be humble before the existence you cannot fully comprehend.

We are part of something larger than ourselves. Our understanding is incomplete. Our influence is unequal. Our relationships shape conditions that extend beyond our immediate existence. Every being holds a point in reality that cannot be exchanged for anyone else's — the stone as much as the person, though the stone's point is harder for us to reach.

We cannot know everything that deserves consideration. But we can decline to treat the limits of our understanding as proof that nothing lies beyond them, and we can ask for evidence where the question is open. This does not require abandoning reason. It requires allowing reason to recognise its limits. It does not require abandoning responsibility. It requires understanding that responsibility without care can become cold control, while care without accountability can become arbitrary intervention. Both are necessary.

The stone understands nothing of us. We barely understand the stone. But between us lies a relationship that neither of us can fully encompass — and that relationship is not less real for it. The framework claims nothing on the stone's behalf. It marks the edge of what we can say about who matters, and it reminds us to hold that edge with humility.

The responsibility of existence is not the responsibility to rule existence. It is the responsibility to recognise that our actions become part of the conditions under which others must live, grow and discover their own possibilities. The more we are capable of changing those conditions, the more carefully we must consider what our influence means. The more we understand, the more honestly we must acknowledge what we do not. And the more power we possess, the more important it becomes to remember that existence is not ours alone.

To understand without ruling. To protect without imprisoning. To love without possessing. To grow without believing oneself complete. To respect what one does not yet understand. And to preserve the possibility that existence may become something greater than any single being can imagine.

In practice: when your influence shapes the conditions of others, say why, let someone else check, and keep a way back. Answerability is the procedure. Symbiosis is the horizon. The responsibility of existence is to keep both in view.

  • Responsibility grows with the capacity to shape conditions, but it must never become a justification for domination.
  • The framework does not promise harmony. It demands answerability, in the service of symbiosis.
  • Understand without ruling, protect without imprisoning, grow without believing oneself complete.

Common objections

Nine objections the framework has to survive. Each is a real objection; none is fatal.

1."This is a values framework dressed up as a procedure."

Partly, and the essay says so (§2.6). J carries a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected. The claim is breadth of acceptability, not neutrality, and the procedure yields obligations to justify, not outcomes. A fair test is whether people with different comprehensive views can use it to reach different answers in the same case. They can: it does not decide the ICU case.

2."Why should contestation override better outcomes?"

It does not always. Emergencies permit acting first (§12.2), and J is thin on outcomes. In severe cases the framework prefers contestation because unaccountable override has a poor record and because those who judge outcomes in their own cause are the least reliable judges. A consequentialist who accepts those facts reaches J instrumentally. The remaining disagreement concerns cases where aggregate gains look large and contestation looks costly. The framework accepts the cost.

3."The capacity principle legitimises power."

Answerability is not authority (§3.2, §8.3). Higher levels mean stronger constraints, and test T5 looks for exactly this misuse. The risk remains and is not argued away; §11.4 converts it from a concession into a design constraint by requiring R1 publication before Level 3 authorisation.

4."Who reviews the reviewers?"

The regress is bounded, not eliminated (§11): finality without infallibility, review conditions R1–R6, plural and public reasoning, and counter-profiles. It also needs a political precondition, a balance of power, that no framework can supply by itself. What the framework can do is downgrade the legitimacy claims of actors that refuse the constraint (§11.4).

5."Precaution under uncertainty is either paralysing or too weak to matter."

The credibility threshold in §4.2 answers the first: reasons arise only where there is evidence or a plausible mechanism, and they are proportionate and defeasible. Against the second: in Tier C the principle does real work, requiring investigation and proportionate safeguards for animals and, potentially, for artificial systems. The stone does not meet the threshold and is not claimed to.

6."The ratings are arbitrary or manipulable."

Judgment cannot be eliminated, but §9 exposes it: anchors with observable indicators, rating before the outcome is known, two raters, a sensitivity test, reasoned overrides and counter-profiles. Tests T1 and T2 examine consistency and discriminative validity.

7."This is Western liberal philosophy in disguise."

Its development lies mostly in that tradition, and §15.1 says so. It does not claim universality for J. It claims convergence among several routes (§2.3) and engages other traditions (§2.5) as independent support and as sources of challenge. That engagement shaped the text: contestation and representation may be collective, ecosystem standing is left open instead of denied, and the boundary of who counts is not treated as settled. Critique from within those traditions would be worth more than any reply given here.

8."It will burden the already overburdened."

The level attaches to the arrangement and duties are allocated by capacity (§8.2). Level 1 is modest. The health worker case (§14.4) and the emergency physician case (§14.2b) show the mechanism, and whether burdens migrate to front-line staff is part of what tests T3 and T4 would reveal.

9."Why not just rely on rights and regulation?"

Rights and regulation are necessary and are not replaced. They leave gaps: influence by actors the law does not reach, structural harms not yet recognised as rights violations, and cases where the law lags. The framework helps decide what to demand of actors where law is silent, and where law should extend.

Vad texten hävdar

När du utövar påverkan som du själv har handlingsutrymme över, och som formar andras villkor, är du skyldig dem en förklaring. Du kan inte vara ensam domare över om den förklaringen räcker.

Ansvarsskyldighet är proceduren. Symbios är idealet den tjänar — relationer av beroende där beroendet inte utnyttjas, skillnad inte tvingas till likhet, och den beroende behåller förutsättningarna för fortsatt existens, utveckling och deltagande.

Ramverket anger i förväg fem villkor under vilka dess aktiveringsregel bör revideras eller överges. Det är en metodologisk begränsning, inte ett anspråk på att lösa filosofins metodproblem — falsifierbarhet i teknisk mening är omstridd, och texten tar inte ställning i den tvisten. Vad som hävdas är mindre: att ett ramverk som påstår sig vara tillämpbart är lättare att korrigera om det anger sina misslyckandevillkor i förväg.

Kärnan i fem punkter

  1. Grund och stöd (§2–3). En tunn princip (J): ordningar som systematiskt begränsar andras villkor måste kunna rättfärdigas inför dem som berörs och vara öppna för bestridande. J stöds av fyra oberoende vägar (rättfärdigande, republikansk, epistemisk och relationell). Deras konvergens är evidens för J, inte ett bevis — analogin med strukturell realism i vetenskapsfilosofin illustrerar anspråkets form men är inte ett argument. §2.2 anger fem falsifieringsvillkor för aktiveringsregeln; §15.2 ger dem i fullständig form. Skyldigheten utlöses av utövad påverkan, skalas efter vad som står på spel och fördelas efter förmåga. §3.3 demonstrerar att ECP löser ett fall (plattformsamplifiering) bättre än Youngs social connection model, genom att behålla den strukturella insikten och lägga till allokeringen.
  2. Vem som räknas (§4–5). Fyra nivåer: aktörer, varelser med eget välbefinnande, osäkra kandidater (försiktighet i proportion till evidens) och icke-subjekt. Stenen markerar gränsen för vad ramverket hävdar. Symbios ges tre observerbara indikatorer (§5.1): icke-utnyttjande (S1), icke-assimilation (S2) och fortsatt möjlighet (S3), så att ramverkets ideal är lika bestridbart som dess procedur.
  3. När och hur mycket (§6–9). Handlingsutrymme är en grind. Tre drivare avgör nivån: inlåsning (svårt att göra ogjort), allvar (hur mycket som står på spel) och bristande möjlighet att bestrida. Nivåerna är att förklara, att ge möjlighet till granskning och svar, och att bevara, låta granska oberoende och auktorisera. Skyldigheter fördelas efter förmåga, så att ingen görs till syndabock. Bedömningar följer ett protokoll med ankare, två bedömare, känslighetstest och möjlighet för de berörda att lämna motprofiler.
  4. Ingen ensam domare (§11). Frågan "vem granskar granskarna?" behandlas som ett designproblem: slutgiltighet utan ofelbarhet, flera granskare med offentliga skäl, och oberoende utnämning och finansiering. §11.4 förvandlar maktregressen från en eftergift till en designbegränsning: T5 är ett förhandsvillkor för Level 3-auktorisation. En aktör som vägrar publicera sina R1-indikatorer förlorar inte ramverket — den förlorar sin Level 3-legitimitet.
  5. Tillämpning, test och utbyte (§14–15). Ett nio-stegs-förfarande och flera fall, inklusive ett som ser ut att vederlägga ramverket (§14.2b, akutläkaren) och som löses genom att peka ut rätt pliktbärare — arrangemanget, inte läkaren i stunden. Fem falsifieringsvillkor (T1–T5) med oberoende mått, och sex villkor för att byta ut ramverket, helt eller modul för modul. Ramverket är inte empiriskt prövat, men dess aktiveringsregel är specificerad så att den kan prövas.

Vad texten inte löser

  • Den avgör inga utfall.
  • Den kan inte garantera att granskare inte kapas — men den kan göra kapning synlig och degradera legitimitet.
  • Den är utvecklad huvudsakligen inom en västerländsk tradition.

I en mening: när din påverkan formar andras villkor, säg varför, låt någon annan kontrollera, och behåll en väg tillbaka. Ansvarsskyldighet är proceduren. Symbios är horisonten. Ansvarsskyldigheten för existensen är att hålla båda i sikte.

Glossary

Answerability
The duty to give reasons for conditions one helps create or maintain, to permit examination, and to remain open to correction. Distinct from blame, liability and authority. The procedure by which symbiosis is pursued.
Contestation deficit (C)
How hard it is for affected parties to exit, object effectively or find out what is done to them.
Convergence
The support J receives from four independent traditions — justificatory, republican, epistemic and relational (§2.3). Offered as evidence for J, not as proof of J.
Counter-profile
An alternative influence profile filed by an affected party, to which the actor must respond (§9.4).
Credible standing
Evidence-based reason to think an entity has interests of its own (§4.2).
Discretion (D)
Genuine alternatives and a role in setting the influence. The gate of the activation rule.
Entrenchment (E)
How long consequences last and how hard they are to undo.
Exercised-Capacity Principle (ECP)
Exercised influence triggers the duty, stakes and contestation deficit scale it, capacity allocates it (§3). Demonstrated against Young's social connection model in §3.3.
Failure conditions (T1–T5)
Five conditions under which the activation rule should be revised or abandoned (§2.2, §15.2). Stated in advance, with independent measures and provisional thresholds. A methodological constraint, not a claim to have solved the philosophy of method.
Finality without infallibility
Decisions final for practical purposes but reopenable on defined grounds (§11.2).
The Floor (F1–F5)
Minimum conditions in relationships of dependence: exit or voice, contestation, development, non-foreclosure, recognition (§10).
J
The core principle: systematic constraint of others' conditions needs justification to those affected, open to contestation (§2.1).
Levels 1–3
Explain; review and respond; preserve, examine and authorise (§8).
Presumptive level
The level the activation rule yields, overridable only with stated reasons (§7.3, §9.3).
Relational existence
The empirical and phenomenological claim that no being exists except through relations of dependence, influence and constitution (§5). Not a metaphysics of being.
Severity (S)
How much is at stake for the affected, rated at individual and, for system design, aggregate level. Subsumes Magnitude and Scope.
Symbiosis
A normative ideal, not a biological description: relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§5). Made measurable through S1–S3 (§5.1).
Symbiosis indicators (S1–S3)
Non-exploitation, non-assimilation, continued possibility (§5.1).
Tiers A–D
Agents; welfare subjects; uncertain candidates; non-subjects (§4.1).

About this text

By Jesper Blom.

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see Bibliography). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Four features of the framework deserve comment. First, it states, in advance, five conditions under which its activation rule should be revised or abandoned (§2.2, §15.2) — a methodological constraint, not a claim to solve the philosophy of method. Second, it is corrigible by design: it names its own replacement conditions (§15.3). Third, it converts the power regress from a concession into a design constraint (§11.4). Fourth, it distinguishes the procedure (answerability) from the ideal it serves (symbiosis), and makes the ideal measurable (§5.1).

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with. A framework that cannot be replaced cannot be corrected — see §15.3.
