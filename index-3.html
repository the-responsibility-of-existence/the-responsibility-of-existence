Abstract

In one sentence: when you exercise discretionary influence over the conditions under which others live, choose and develop, and when they cannot exit or contest that influence effectively, you owe them an account of it — and you cannot be the sole judge of whether that account is good enough.

What this essay is, and what it is not. It is a framework for deciding when influence creates a duty to answer, how demanding that duty is, who must discharge it, and how it can be discharged without turning into new domination. It is not a new foundational principle in the tradition of Pettit, Forst or Jonas. Its core principle (J) is a thin, disjunctive, abductively supported synthesis of existing traditions, not a demonstration of necessity. J is necessary but not sufficient: it must be paired with the Floor (§10) and assessed against SY1–SY3 (§5.1), and a relation can satisfy J genuinely and fail substantively (§2.1). Its contribution is stated openly in §1.5 and shown in Table 1: an interlocking operational set (activation rule, duty ladder, T5-precondition, epistemic burden principle, allocation by exercised capacity) that no single tradition supplies. Its normative location is stated openly in §2.7: internal to the justificatory tradition, but only to its thin version.

Universal symbiosis and normative symbiosis. The essay rests on a descriptive worldview — universal symbiosis — that existence is relationally woven: dependence, influence, interaction, competition, conflict, destruction and mutual creation are all part of the fabric. It distinguishes this sharply from normative symbiosis: the pro tanto floor specified by SY1–SY3, which says that certain ways of arranging relations are unacceptable. The second does not follow from the first (§2.0a). The descriptive claim is argued, not asserted (§5.0b). The framework states its premises openly (§2.0a), argues for them against alternatives (§2.0b), distinguishes logical consequences from normative commitments from practical recommendations, and argues rather than derives its normative core.

1. Ground. J is a thin, disjunctive principle: arrangements that systematically constrain others' conditions must provide at least one of meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation. Which is required depends on the structure of the constraint. J is primarily an epistemic principle. J is abductively supported and has stated defeaters (§2.2b).

2. Standing and symbiosis. Who is owed an account, including beings that cannot speak for themselves, handled through four tiers of standing under uncertainty with an explicit plausibility threshold (§4). Symbiosis is specified with observable indicators (SY1–SY3) (§5.1). SY1 is the weakest of the four sources of confirmation in §15.2b. Section §5.2 develops a more limited claim: asymmetric influence creates a heightened burden to seek correction from sources not controlled by the decision-maker.

3. Activation. One rule: a discretion gate plus three drivers (entrenchment, severity, contestation deficit) that determine one of three levels of duty, with anchors and a rating protocol (§6–9).

4. Structure. A floor of minimum conditions, the prohibition on being sole judge of one's own influence, the reviewer regress treated as a design problem, and accounts of conflict, future generations, ecological wholes, genuine tragedy, and learning (§10–13, §12.6, §12.7).

5. Application and testing. Worked cases including one that appears to refute the framework (§14.2b) and one that shows the T5-precondition failing (§14.7). The empirical tests are proposed, not performed.

Answerability is the procedure. Normative symbiosis is the floor. The framework does not claim that a relation meeting SY1–SY3 is therefore good. It claims that a relation failing SY1–SY3 is not good, whatever else it is.

The apophatic constraint (§15.2b) is what follows from the framework's hardest question: the superior cannot certify that it has avoided imposing its own understanding, but it can preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate — provided a party outside the superior can verify compliance. In the Limit 3 case, where no such party exists, A1–A4 cannot be enforced, though they remain obligations. A1–A4 are stated as normative commitments, not logical consequences of the framework's premises (§2.0a). §15.2c explains what Limit 3 actually shows.

The framework is a diagnostic, not a procedure (§15.3). The framework is testable. It has not been tested (§15.4, Appendix A).

The argument in brief

The essay is long, and its spine can be stated in a page. This is that page.

  1. The worldview. Existence is relationally woven — universal symbiosis. The relations include dependence, influence, interaction, competition, conflict, destruction and mutual creation. This is a descriptive claim about the structure of reality, not an ethics (§5), and it is argued for in §5.0b, not asserted.
  2. The problem. Influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act is not adequately covered by existing moral vocabulary. This is the gap the framework addresses (§1).
  3. The principle (J). Arrangements that systematically and non-trivially constrain others' conditions must, for those affected, provide at least one of: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation (§2.1). J is necessary but not sufficient.
  4. The normative step. The move from universal symbiosis to normative symbiosis does not follow from the relational premise alone. It requires additional premises — standing, vulnerability, capacity, fallibility, contestation — and the framework states them openly (§2.0a) and argues for them against alternatives (§2.0b).
  5. Why J. J is abductively supported. Four traditions make J plausible, but they are treated as considerations, not as independent proofs. J has stated defeaters (§2.2b).
  6. The trigger. Influence creates the duty when discretion is present and exit or review is unavailable or ineffective. How much duty scales with entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit (§7–§8).
  7. The epistemic claim. Under asymmetric influence, the more powerful party has weaker grounds for treating its own assessment as decisive. It therefore bears a heightened burden to obtain independent evidence and correction. This is a defeasible claim, not a necessary condition for knowledge (§5.2).
  8. The limits. J is not sufficient. It is defensible. Its activation rule has proposed evaluation conditions; its ground and epistemic principle have stated defeaters. None of these is a proof of anything. They are invitations to argue. Its empirical claims are proposed and testable, not confirmed.
  9. The hardest question. Can a superior intelligence preserve a being's existence, freedom and development without understanding what the being is? The framework's answer (§15.2b) is that it cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure, but it can preserve the conditions under which the imposition is nameable, revisable and resistible. Four structural conditions follow: preserve the capacity to resist (A1), do not require justification in the superior's terms (A2), do not interpret non-resistance as consent (A3), treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding (A4). In the Limit 3 case — where no party outside the superior can verify compliance — they cannot be enforced, though they remain obligations (§15.2b, §15.2c).

What it is not. The framework is not a procedure for certifying legitimacy. A relation can follow every rule and still fail to be legitimate. The framework's job is to name that possibility, not to deny it (§15.3).

If you read only one section after this page, read §7 — that is where the framework either works or does not. If you read only one passage, read §5.2. If you want to know what the framework cannot do, read §15.2, §15.2b, §15.2c and §15.6.

Part I

Problem, Scope and Ground

1.The Problem, Scope and Terms

The capacity to shape other people's conditions has grown faster than the practices for answering for it.

Recommendation systems, credit scoring, clinical triage tools and AI assistants shape the conditions of millions. So do states writing policy and companies setting terms, and, at a different scale, clinicians, teachers, landlords and parents. Existing moral vocabulary covers parts of this territory: blame for wrongful acts, rights for protected interests, consent for voluntary exchanges, regulation for what law has already caught. What falls between them is influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act, and that nonetheless determines what others can do and become.

1.1 What the framework does and does not do

It answers four questions: when does influence create a duty to give an account; how demanding is that duty; who must discharge it; and how can review be organised so that it does not become a new instrument of domination. It does not tell anyone what to decide. It is not a decision theory, and it is not a substitute for law, rights or substantive standards of justice.

1.2 Scope conditions: when the framework does not apply

The framework is offered for a specific class of cases. It does not claim to apply everywhere.

  • Symmetrical relations. Where parties have roughly equal capacity to exit, review and contest, J is trivially satisfied or not engaged.
  • Relations governed by enforceable rights and effective regulation. Where law provides effective rights, review and remedies, the framework may add little. Formal legality alone is not enough. The framework supplements legal standards where those protections fail in practice; it never displaces them.
  • Purely causal influence without discretion. The framework's trigger is exercised or maintained discretionary influence, not mere causal connection.
  • Trivial influence. The severity floor in §7 is meant to exclude it.
  • Relations where the affected party is not a candidate for standing. Stones and rivers as such do not have standing under this framework (§4.1).

1.3 Terms

Influence: the exercise or maintenance of control over conditions under which others act, choose, develop or fare. Actor: a person, organisation or institution, including those that design, deploy and maintain technical systems. Affected party: any being whose interests are materially affected by the influence. Discretion: the actor has genuine alternatives at tolerable cost and a role in setting the influence, rather than merely executing a rule it had no part in making or contesting. Answerability: a duty to give reasons for the conditions one helps create or maintain, to those affected or their representatives; to permit examination of those reasons; and to remain open to correction. It is distinct from blame, liability and authority.

Five terms that must be kept apart. The framework uses five terms that ordinary language often conflates. Each names a different thing, and the framework's claims depend on not mixing them:

Table 1a. Answerability, responsibility, blame, authority, sovereignty
Term What it names What it does not entail
Answerability The duty to give reasons, permit examination, and remain open to correction (§5.2) It does not entail blame, liability, or authority.
Responsibility Being the party whose actions, omissions, or maintained structures make a difference to what happens It does not entail blame or authority.
Blame A moral judgment that a party is at fault for a wrongful act or omission It does not follow from answerability alone.
Authority The justified power to make decisions that bind others within a specified domain It does not follow from answerability or responsibility. A higher duty level means stronger constraints, not greater authority (§8.3).
Sovereignty The claim to determine the conditions of others without external check It is not what the framework offers anyone. §16 says explicitly: "Answerability is not sovereignty."

The key implication. A party can bear a large answerability burden — can be required to explain, to submit to review, to justify — without being morally at fault for what happened, and without acquiring any authority over those it is answerable to. The framework's central claim is that these five things come apart, and that treating them as one thing is what produces the confusion the framework is meant to address.

1.4 Relation to existing work

The framework is a synthesis and an operationalisation, and its intellectual debts are substantial: Pettit and Lovett on domination, Forst on the right to justification, Scanlon on reasonable rejection, Young on structural responsibility, Bovens and Shoemaker on accountability and answerability, Tronto, Held and Goodin on care and vulnerability. Its contribution is comparative and operational, with a limited philosophical proposal rather than a new foundational principle.

1.5 The specific claim, in one paragraph

If the framework has a single contribution, it should be statable in a paragraph — and then shown.

The framework's contribution is not that non-domination matters (Pettit), that those subject to norms have a right to justification (Forst), that responsibility for structural harm is shared (Young), or that capability creates responsibility for the future (Jonas). All of that is in the tradition. The framework's contribution is the following claim:

The evidential burden of justification under asymmetric influence is not adequately captured by any of the existing traditions, because none of them specifies how the burden is allocated, how it is graduated by entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit, or how review mechanisms can be designed so that they degrade rather than merely consult the powerful actor's claimed legitimacy. The framework's contribution is to specify (i) the activation rule, (ii) the duty ladder, (iii) the T5-precondition, and (iv) the epistemic burden principle, as an interlocking set of operational proposals that no single tradition supplies.

Why this is not just a synthesis. A synthesis of existing positions would be a combination. The framework is not a combination. It is an interlocking set — each piece depends on the others, and the whole does work that none of the parts does.

Adjacent literatures the table does not name. Several operational components have close analogues in literatures not conventionally grouped with the four traditions in Table 1. Accountable governance (Bovens 2007; Mulgan 2003) supplies the distinction between answerability and accountability. Risk-based regulation (Black 2010; Hood, Rothstein & Baldwin 2001) supplies the graduated, risk-proportional structure that is the closest institutional analog of the duty ladder. Administrative-law proportionality (Alexy 2002; Mashaw 1983) supplies the logical form of the level distinctions. Experimentalist governance (Sabel & Zeitlin 2010) supplies the iterative review-and-correction structure that the T5-precondition formalises. The precaution / cost-benefit debate (Sunstein 2005; Wiener 2016) supplies the threshold debate that §4.2 enters.

2.The Ground and Its Support

The framework needs a thin, defensible principle, an abductive justification that is honest about its own weakness, and stated defeaters for the ground itself.

2.0 A motivating premise: fallibilism about understanding

F (fallibilism). No actor fully understands the conditions it shapes, or everything that depends on what it does. This is a claim about the limits of understanding, not about the cosmos and not about any ranking of beings.

B (bridge norm). Where an actor's influence falls on others who bear the cost of its errors, and who may see what the actor cannot, the actor should not be the sole judge of that influence; it should seek correction from them.

What F and B do. F alone yields no duty. A fact about limited understanding does not by itself entail a norm, which is why B is stated openly as a normative premise rather than smuggled in. Together, F and B motivate J (§2.1) and the epistemic burden principle (§5.2). They do not entail J.

Why an obligation, and not merely prudence or benevolence. Prudence would say: it is in the actor's interest to seek correction. Benevolence would say: it is kind of the actor to seek correction. B says neither. It says that the affected party has a claim on the actor — a claim grounded in the fact that the actor's errors fall on them and that they may see what the actor cannot.

What depends on F, and what does not. The motivation for J and for the epistemic burden principle draws on F and B. The exercised-capacity principle (§3) does not. The activation rule, duty ladder, rating protocol, floor and the T5 precondition are procedural and stand or fall on their own tests (§15.4). The apophatic constraint and A1–A4 (§15.2b) are conditional on F. A reader who rejects F can therefore still use the framework's procedural machinery, but that reader loses A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint. This is a real loss, not merely a loss of motivation.

2.0a What the relational premise does not do

The claim that existence is relationally woven does not by itself entail any normative claim. This section states the additional premises explicitly, and distinguishes three kinds of claim.

The claim that existence is relationally woven (universal symbiosis, §5) does not by itself entail any normative claim. From 'A exists in relation to B' nothing follows about how A should treat B, any more than from 'A is spatially near B' anything follows about how A should treat B.

The additional premises. What the essay does instead is state the additional premises that, together with the relational premise, motivate the framework's normative claims:

  • P1 (standing). Affected parties have moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful.
  • P2 (vulnerability). Affected parties can be harmed by the exercise of discretionary influence over their conditions.
  • P3 (capacity). Some actors have the capacity to exercise such influence, and some do not.
  • P4 (fallibility). No actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. (F, §2.0.)
  • P5 (contestation). Affected parties have a claim that the actor not be the sole judge of the influence when they bear the cost of its errors and may see what the actor cannot. (B, §2.0.)

The framework's normative claims — J (§2.1), the activation rule (§7), the duty ladder (§8), SY1–SY3 (§5.1) — follow from these premises together. They do not follow from any one premise alone, and they do not follow from the relational premise alone.

Three kinds of claim, kept apart.

  • Logical consequences. If P1–P5 are accepted, some conclusions follow deductively. For example: if B is accepted, then an actor that controls both the influence and the evidence used to assess it cannot close the question using only evidence it controls.
  • Normative commitments. Some claims are not deduced from the premises but are normative commitments the framework states openly: J, the floor (F1–F5), SY1–SY3, and A1–A4. These are defended by argument but they are not theorems.
  • Practical recommendations. The activation rule (§7), the duty ladder (§8), the rating protocol (§9), the T5-precondition (§11.4), the pilot protocol (Appendix A) are practical recommendations. They stand or fall on their own tests (§15.4).

Why this matters. The three-way distinction is the essay's answer to the charge that it commits an is–ought fallacy. It does not derive norms from facts. A reader who disagrees can say precisely where: at the premises (§2.7, §2.0b), at the normative commitments (§2.3, §5.3, §15.2b), or at the practical recommendations (§15.4).

2.0b Why these premises, and not others?

§2.0a states the framework's premises openly. Openness is not justification. This section argues that P1–P5 are better starting points than their main alternatives, without claiming that they are proven.

P1 (standing): affected parties have moral weight that cannot be reduced to instrumental value. The main alternatives are: (a) purely instrumental standing — affected parties count only insofar as they serve the powerful; (b) standing only for rational agents (Kant); (c) standing only for those who can express themselves. Alternative (a) is coherent but has consequences that most moral traditions reject: it licenses treating infants, the cognitively disabled, animals, and future generations as mere means. Alternative (b) has the same problem for those who lack rational agency. Alternative (c) excludes infants, non-human animals, and those who cannot speak — and is difficult to reconcile with the framework's own treatment of future generations (§12.5) and non-communicating persons (§4.6). P1 is the minimal position that avoids these consequences. It is not proven; it is better supported than its alternatives.

P2 (vulnerability): affected parties can be harmed by discretionary influence. The alternative is standing without vulnerability — a party has moral weight but cannot be harmed. This is difficult to sustain: a being with moral weight but no vulnerability to harm has no interests that could be violated, which collapses back into alternative (a) for P1. P2 is what makes P1 operational: it specifies what kind of harm the framework is concerned with.

P3 (capacity): some actors have the capacity to exercise discretionary influence, and some do not. The alternative is that all actors have equal capacity — which is empirically false. The framework does not need P3 to be true in every case; it needs P3 to hold for the cases it addresses (§1.2). P3 is a claim about the distribution of capacity, not about its value.

P4 (fallibility): no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. This is F (§2.0). The alternative is the claim that some actor has complete understanding — which no one has demonstrated. P4 is a defeasible empirical claim.

P5 (contestation): affected parties have a claim that the actor not be the sole judge. This is B (§2.0). The alternative is that the actor may be the sole judge of its own influence — which is precisely the position the framework was designed to reject. P5 is not derived from P1–P4; it is a further normative commitment. But it is the commitment that makes the framework's procedural machinery necessary (§5.2, §11).

What this shows, and what it does not. P1–P5 are better supported than their alternatives. They are not proven. A reader who accepts a different set of premises — for example, a reader who accepts (a) for P1 — will not be persuaded by the framework's arguments, and the framework should say so. What the framework claims is not that P1–P5 are the only defensible premises, but that they are the ones that make the framework's claims coherent and that have the fewest unacceptable consequences.

What would refute P1–P5. If a critic can show that an alternative set of premises — say, instrumental standing plus capacity allocation — produces the same operational conclusions with fewer normative commitments, the framework's premises should be revised. If a critic can show that P1–P5 are internally inconsistent, they should be revised. Neither has been shown. But the absence of a refutation is not a proof of correctness, and §2.0a's distinction between logical consequences, normative commitments, and practical recommendations applies here: P1–P5 are normative commitments, not logical consequences.

  • P1–P5 are stated openly (§2.0a) and argued against alternatives (§2.0b).
  • Each premise is the minimal position that avoids the unacceptable consequences of its alternatives.
  • The premises are normative commitments, not logical consequences.
  • A reader who accepts a different set of premises will not be persuaded, and the framework says so.

2.1 The core principle (J)

Any arrangement that systematically and non-trivially constrains the conditions under which others act, choose or develop must, for those affected, provide at least one of three things: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification that is genuinely open to their contestation. Where exit is available at tolerable cost and review is effective, justification need not be owed. Where they are not, justification is required, and the strength of the requirement scales with how thoroughly exit and review fail.

J is deliberately thin. It does not say what is good, which outcomes are required, or who prevails when interests conflict. It says what kind of relationship must exist between those who shape conditions and those who live in them: one in which the affected are not simply subject to unaccountable power.

What the qualifiers require. J's three disjuncts are not satisfied by the bare existence of exit, review, or justification. Each has a minimum threshold:

  • Meaningful exit requires that the affected party can leave at tolerable cost without losing the conditions for continued existence, development, or participation (F1, F3, F4, §10).
  • Independent review requires that the reviewer meets R1–R6 (§11.3).
  • Justification genuinely open to contestation requires that dissent channels exist and have been used, that the vocabulary for objection is available, and that intermediaries not controlled by the actor exist (§15.2b, sources (iii) and (iv)).

A power structure can satisfy the form of any of these and fail the substance. When it does, J is not satisfied.

When none of the three is practically possible. There are cases where exit is unavailable, review cannot reach the actor, and contestation is not open to the affected party. In such cases J is not satisfied, and the framework does not claim that it can be. What the framework can do is name the failure, identify which of the three disjuncts is missing and why, and — where possible — specify what would be required to restore one of them.

J is primarily an epistemic principle. J is not a claim that justification makes actions right. It is a claim that actors who cannot be contested are more likely to be wrong, and that contestation is the best available protection against this.

J is defensible, not necessary as a deduction. The framework does not claim that J is a necessary truth. It claims that J is abductively defensible: that it explains important recurring risks of unchecked discretion while remaining compatible with plural substantive theories of justice.

J is necessary but not sufficient for justice

The objection. A disjunctive principle can be satisfied in more than one form. Can a power structure genuinely meet J and the Floor and still be deeply unjust? Yes. The framework says so, and this section says what follows.

The case. Consider a society that genuinely satisfies J and the Floor, but the distribution of resources is such that one group, while formally free to exit and contest, cannot in practice use either. The society satisfies J and the Floor, and it is unjust. What it fails is not J or the Floor but something the framework does not measure: the material conditions under which formal freedoms are usable.

What the framework says. This case does not refute J; it shows what J was never meant to do. J specifies the relationship between those who shape conditions and those who live in them. It does not specify the distribution of resources, opportunities or capabilities. A society can satisfy J and the Floor and still fail on distributive justice. The framework's response is not to expand J to cover distribution — that would make it a substantive theory of justice, which it is not — but to say that J and the Floor are necessary conditions for a non-arbitrary relation, not sufficient conditions for a just one.

  • J can be genuinely satisfied and the relation still be unjust, because J is procedural and relational, not distributive.
  • The qualifiers are load-bearing, but they do not make J sufficient.
  • J must be paired with the Floor and assessed against SY1–SY3.
  • J is a necessary condition for a non-arbitrary relation, not a sufficient one.

2.2b What would refute J: stated defeaters

A principle that cannot be defeated is not a principle; it is a dogma.

  • D1 — The unaccountable-power hypothesis is false. If it can be shown empirically that actors with unaccountable discretion over others' conditions do not systematically produce worse outcomes — measured independently of the actors' own reports — than actors subject to exit, review or contestation, then J's epistemic justification collapses.
  • D2 — A better principle explains the same cases. If a rival principle explains all the cases J explains, does so more simply, and generates clearer practical guidance, then J should be replaced.
  • D3 — The convergence is an artefact. If it can be shown that the four "routes" to J (§2.3) are not independent considerations but one tradition dressed in four vocabularies, then the argument for J reduces to a single route.
  • D4 — The costs exceed the benefits. If adopting J produces worse outcomes for the affected than not adopting it — measured by independent indicators — then J should be abandoned.

These defeaters are stated in advance so that the framework can be corrected. The operational form of D1–D4 is in §15.7 (Table 8).

2.3 Why J: an abductive justification

J is offered as a plausible explanation of two recurring risks of unchecked discretionary power: domination and epistemic error.

The framework does not rest on a single theory. It rests on an abductive inference: J is proposed as a compact explanation of a set of recurring risks associated with unaccountable power.

Four considerations, not four independent proofs.

  • The justificatory consideration. If principles governing relations among agents must be justifiable to each of them (Scanlon 1998; Forst 2012), then constraint that cannot be justified to the constrained treats them as objects of management rather than as participants.
  • The republican consideration. The distinctive wrong of domination is the standing capacity to interfere arbitrarily (Pettit 1997, 2012; Lovett 2010).
  • The epistemic consideration. Actors are fallible, partial about their own influence, and prone to self-serving judgment. This is the primary ground of J.
  • The relational consideration. Obligations arise within dependence (Tronto 1993; Held 2006; Goodin 1985).

What this shows. The convergence of four considerations is support, not proof. The considerations are not fully independent, and the epistemic consideration is primary.

2.4 Rivals, taken in their best forms

Consequentialism. Sophisticated consequentialists can accept J as an institutional implication. Kantianism. It shares the core but grounds standing in rational agency, which creates problems for infants, the cognitively disabled and animals. §4 handles these through tiers. Virtue ethics. It supports the humility the framework requires, but it is weak on structural questions. Justice as fairness and capabilities. The capabilities approach (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006) supplies substantive accounts of what is at stake. Rights. Rights are outputs, and the framework treats them as external constraints. Realism. The realist who denies that power owes anything to the weak cannot be refuted from within the framework, because the framework is internal to the justificatory tradition (§2.7).

2.4b The power objection

A procedural framework that tells the powerful how to justify themselves may simply give them a new vocabulary for doing what they were going to do anyway.

The objection. The framework asks powerful actors to explain, to submit to review, to justify. But the powerful are precisely the actors with the resources to perform these rituals convincingly.

The reply, in three parts. First, the objection is partly correct. Proceduralism can legitimate power. That is why §11.4 treats T5 as a precondition rather than a diagnosis. Second, the objection is not specific to proceduralism. Any normative framework can be captured. The framework's claim is that T5-as-precondition makes the capture visible. Third, the framework's modesty is not an admission of failure. The framework claims to handle the problem, not to solve it.

What this does not do. It does not answer the objection fully. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. The objection is the second-hardest the framework faces, after the realist objection in objection 1.

2.5 Traditions that start elsewhere

Ubuntu-inspired moral theory (Metz 2007), Confucian role ethics (Ames 2011) and many Indigenous relational ontologies (Watts 2013; de la Cadena 2015; Kimmerer 2013) begin from relational personhood and obligations to kin, community and land. The framework treats them as additional considerations pointing toward its operative core — not as independent proofs.

2.6 Costs

J is thin, so it underdetermines outcomes. The framework presupposes a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected; it is therefore not neutral among all comprehensive doctrines. The reply is the modular design of §15.5.

2.7 The framework's normative location, stated openly

A framework that claims breadth while relying on a specific tradition is not honest about what it is.

The framework is internal to a justificatory tradition. It takes for granted that those subject to arrangements have standing to demand reasons for them. A reader who rejects the justificatory tradition altogether will not be persuaded by anything in this essay.

Which tradition, exactly. The framework needs only the thin version: affected parties have some moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful. It does not need the thick Habermasian version.

What the framework actually requires. Three claims, of decreasing thickness:

  1. Thin standing. Affected parties have some moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful. Widely shared.
  2. Semi-thick procedural claim. Arrangements that impose significant costs on affected parties must be justified to them, not merely to a third party. Justificatory-tradition-specific, but not uniquely so.
  3. Framework-specific claim. Justification can take the form of meaningful exit, independent review, or contestation — disjunctively. Specific to this framework.

Why thin standing is a reasonable starting point. Three considerations support it, which are not proofs but are more than assertions:

First, thin standing is the minimal normative position compatible with taking the affected party seriously as a source of reasons rather than as an object to be managed.

Second, thin standing is widely shared across traditions that otherwise disagree. The framework does not require agreement on why — only on that.

Third, thin standing is a claim that the affected party can press. It is not a gift the powerful give the weak; it is a claim the weak can make.

Why J follows from thin standing, given B. Given thin standing and B, J is a reasonable specification of what the claim amounts to in practice. The disjunctive form of J (§2.1) is the weakest form that satisfies this claim.

  • The framework is internal to a justificatory tradition, but only to its thin version.
  • Three claims, of decreasing thickness.
  • Three considerations support thin standing as a starting point without proving it from nothing.
  • The abductive argument is for J given the premises, not for the premises themselves.

2.8 Adversarial tests: what Pettit, Forst and Jonas would object

Section 1.5 and Table 1 show what the framework adds to the four traditions it engages. §2.7 shows its normative location within one of them. Neither shows what those traditions would say against it.

Pettit: the epistemic framing undercuts the republican point

The objection. The framework's epistemic framing of J (§2.1) undercuts the republican point. Pettit is not primarily an epistemologist: his claim is that domination is a political fact, not an epistemic one.

The reply. First, the epistemic framing is an entry point, not a foundation. Because domination is invisible to the actor without contestation, contestation is a necessary safeguard. Second, the framework does not replace the republican claim with the epistemic one. It supplements it.

Forst: the thin/thick distinction is unstable

The objection. Forst's right to justification is already thin. If Forst already has the thin version, what does the framework add?

The reply. The framework adds allocation. Forst says that the affected party has a right to justification; the framework says who must give it, in what form, by when, and with what safeguards.

Jonas: the framework is too near-sighted

The objection. Jonas is concerned with the species and with the future; the framework is concerned with present affected parties.

The reply. The framework is a special case of Jonas's ethics, applied to contemporary structural influence, without Jonas's metaphysical grounding. What the framework inherits from Jonas is the capacity-based structure of responsibility and the forward-looking orientation, which §12.5 applies to future generations.

  • Positioning ("what we add") is not engagement ("what they would object").
  • Pettit would say the epistemic framing undercuts the republican point. The framework replies: the epistemic framing is an entry point, not a foundation.
  • Forst would say the thin/thick distinction is unstable. The framework replies: the addition is allocation, not thickness.
  • Jonas would say the framework is near-sighted. The framework replies: it is a special case of Jonas's ethics without Jonas's metaphysics.

3.The Exercised-Capacity Principle

Influence triggers the duty, stakes and contestation scale it, and capacity allocates it.

An actor that exercises or maintains discretionary influence over the conditions of others' lives owes them an account of it when exit and review are unavailable or ineffective. How much it owes scales with the stakes and with the affected parties' difficulty in contesting the influence (§7). The burden of establishing the mechanisms through which the account is given falls on actors in proportion to their capacity to establish them (§8.2).

Trigger: exercised or maintained influence, and the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. Scale: stakes and contestation deficit. Allocation: capacity to discharge.

3.1 Why not the rivals

Causal responsibility is necessary for attribution but insufficient for structural influence (Thompson 1980). Fault-based responsibility concerns blame; answerability does not presuppose fault (Shoemaker 2011). Relational responsibility is real, but relational duties alone say nothing about anonymous influence. Consent is valuable where meaningful, but under asymmetry it can be manufactured or coerced. The social connection model (Young 2011) is the closest rival.

3.2 The cost: answerability handles power; it does not solve it

A principle that makes the powerful responsible risks being used by them as a badge of legitimacy. The framework does not claim to eliminate this risk. Three constraints apply: responsibility is not authority (§1.3, Table 1a; §8.3), review must meet independence conditions (§11), and the risk is tested directly in §15.

Handling, not solving. The framework's aim is not to solve the problem of power — no framework can. Its aim is to convert the problem from an unstructured condition into a set of design constraints that can be observed, contested and revised.

3.3 The exercised-capacity principle alongside the social connection model: an illustrative case

A general claim about allocation is worth little without a case in which it does work that the closest rival cannot.

The case. A recommendation algorithm on a large platform amplifies content that increases political polarisation. Who bears the duty to answer for it?

Young's answer. Everyone who participates in the structure that produces the harm shares forward-looking responsibility.

ECP's answer. The trigger is exercised discretionary influence, combined with the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. Users have no discretion over the ranking code, and no meaningful exit from the platform's network effects. The engineers and executives have discretion over both, and users cannot exit or review the ranking they produce. So the duty attaches, at Level 3, to the design team and the executives, and at Level 2 to the regulators who could have required audit but did not.

What ECP may add in this case. Young's model has been criticised for exactly this: if everyone is responsible, no one is. ECP keeps the structural insight and adds the allocation that the model lacks.

  • Young's social connection model identifies shared responsibility; ECP proposes a more granular allocation.
  • The advantage is demonstrated in a concrete case.
  • ECP does not claim to have refuted Young; it claims to have entered a contest it can lose.

3.4 Distributed influence and delegated discretion

A principle that only handles face-to-face influence is not a principle for the conditions most people actually live under.

Five modes of contribution.

  • Causing. The party's action produces the effect directly.
  • Enabling. The party creates the conditions under which another party can produce the effect.
  • Controlling. The party retains the ability to stop, redirect, or authorise the effect, even if it does not itself act.
  • Foreseeing. The party knows or could reasonably know that the effect will occur.
  • Benefiting. The party gains from the effect without contributing to it.

How ECP allocates. Causing and controlling are sufficient for the trigger. Enabling is sufficient only where the enabler retains the ability to withdraw the enabling condition at tolerable cost. Foreseeing is not sufficient on its own, but it raises the level. Benefiting is not sufficient on its own, but it generates a duty of inquiry.

The delegation question. Three conditions determine whether delegation succeeds or fails:

  1. The delegator retains the ability to revoke.
  2. The delegator sets the frame.
  3. The delegation is not a genuine transfer of discretion.

The problem of diffusion. In complex systems, responsibility can diffuse until no party is answerable. The framework's response is not to insist that some party must be answerable. It is to insist that the diffusion be located and named. Where no party has the capacity to discharge the duty, the framework says so (§14.5e).

When responsibility changes over time. A party's responsibility is not fixed at the moment of action. It changes as the party's capacity and knowledge change.

A worked case: the supply chain. A garment brand sources from a factory that sources from a subcontractor that employs home workers. Who bears the ECP duty?

  • The home workers exercise no discretionary influence. No duty.
  • The subcontractor exercises discretion over hours and pay, but under constraints set by the factory. Level 1 duty; Level 2 if the factory's constraints are such that the subcontractor cannot refuse them at tolerable cost.
  • The factory exercises discretion over which subcontractor it uses and what terms it sets. Level 2 duty; Level 3 if the terms are so constrained by the brand that the factory cannot refuse.
  • The brand sets the frame: prices, deadlines, audit criteria. By the three conditions above, delegation to the factory is formal only. Level 3 duty.

What this does not do. It does not solve the collective action problem. Where no party can revoke, set the frame, or restore discretion, ECP does not generate a determinate allocation.

  • Distributed influence is handled through the five modes of contribution.
  • Delegation is genuine only if the delegator retains none of three capacities.
  • Where no party can discharge the duty, ECP does not generate a determinate allocation.
  • Responsibility changes over time as capacity and knowledge change.

Part II

Who Is Owed an Account

4.Standing Under Uncertainty

Incomprehensibility is not evidence of unimportance, but neither is it evidence of importance.

4.1 Four tiers

  • Tier A: agents. Beings with normative agency (persons).
  • Tier B: welfare subjects. Beings with interests of their own: sentient animals, and humans who lack agency.
  • Tier C: uncertain candidates. Entities for which there is a non-negligible, evidence-based probability of Tier B status (Birch 2024).
  • Tier D: non-subjects. Entities with no credible interests of their own: stones, rivers and ecosystems as such.

4.2 The plausibility threshold

The test for Tier C is whether there is a credible, evidence-based reason to think the entity has states that matter to it. Without such a threshold, precaution generates reasons everywhere and guides nowhere (Sunstein 2005).

4.2b Five properties, not one

The framework's Tier A–D structure is often read as a ranking by "how much" a being matters. It is not. The tiers distinguish five properties that are often conflated:

  • Moral status. Whether the being has interests that count in the framework's assessment at all.
  • Interests. What the being has reason to want or avoid, given its form of life.
  • Sentience. Whether the being has subjective experience.
  • Vulnerability. Whether the being can be harmed by the exercise of discretionary influence.
  • Capacity to express. Whether the being can express preferences, objections, or refusals.

These five properties are related but not identical. A framework that equates them will either over-attribute or under-attribute.

Precaution under uncertainty. Tier C is where the framework's precaution is most active. The plausibility threshold is not "any possibility"; it is "a credible, evidence-based reason to think the entity has states that matter to it."

The projection problem. Representation of uncertain entities carries a specific risk: that the representative projects their own values onto the represented party. The framework's response is not to pretend otherwise. It is to make the projection visible: where the entity's preferences cannot be determined, the representative's account is a claim, not a fact, and it must be reviewable as a claim.

4.3 The stone, restated

The stone marks the edge of the framework's claims. For beings that meet the threshold, our failure to understand their way of being is no reason to dismiss their interests. For the stone, which does not meet it, the framework claims no standing and says so.

4.4 Artificial systems

Answerability for the influence of AI systems falls on the humans and organisations that design, deploy and maintain them. Whether some artificial systems may themselves be Tier C candidates is contested, and the framework does not answer it.

4.5 Perspectival plurality

For beings in Tiers A–C, their way of experiencing the world may be difficult or impossible for us to reach. That a perspective is inaccessible is not evidence that it is absent (Fricker 2007).

4.6 Representation

Representation is legitimate only if (i) representatives are chosen through a process that can itself be contested, (ii) they state the basis on which they claim to know the interests concerned, (iii) where interests are contested there is more than one representative, (iv) they can be removed, (v) they are neither the actor nor dependent on the actor, and (vi) they represent uncertainty rather than substituting invented certainty.

(vii) Collective contestation must not undermine individual contestation. Where a collective procedure is used to represent affected parties, the procedure must preserve the ability of individuals within the collective to contest the collective's decisions.

  • Four tiers of standing.
  • Tier C requires a plausibility threshold.
  • Five properties are distinguished, not conflated.
  • Representation of silent parties is legitimate only under six conditions.
  • Collective contestation must not undermine individual contestation.
  • The projection problem in representation is a real risk, not eliminable.

5.Interdependence, Difference and Non-Domination

Existence is relational. What follows from that is not an ethics, but the ground on which an ethics must stand.

Living beings, institutions, technologies and ecosystems exist through relations. Some of these relations are dependence, some are influence, some are interaction, some are competition, some are conflict, some are destruction, and some are mutual creation. This is the descriptive claim the essay calls universal symbiosis: not a claim that the universe is harmonious, moral, or guided by purpose, but a claim that nothing exists — no being, no institution, no technology, no ecosystem — except through relations with other things. The relations are not optional additions to isolated existents. They are what the existents are made of.

Universal symbiosis is a worldview, not an ethics. It says nothing about how relations should be arranged. It is compatible with mutualism and with parasitism, with care and with extraction, with cooperation and with domination. This is important, because the essay's central normative claim — that certain ways of arranging relations are unacceptable — is often mistaken for a claim that follows directly from the relational nature of existence. It does not. The move from universal symbiosis to normative symbiosis is the essay's most important argumentative step, and §2.0a and §5.3 make it explicitly. Universal symbiosis describes what exists. Normative symbiosis specifies what is owed. The two are not the same, and the essay keeps them apart from here on.

5.0a Three relations, not one

Universal symbiosis is a single word for at least three distinct relations:

  1. Causal influence. A affects B. A factory's emissions affect a downwind community's air.
  2. Dependence. A requires B for its continued existence, development, or functioning. A child depends on its caregivers.
  3. Ecological belonging. A is part of a system that has properties A does not have alone. A species belongs to an ecosystem.

These three relations can come apart. An actor can affect a party without depending on it. An actor can depend on a party without affecting it in the relevant sense. An actor can belong to a system without exercising discretion over it.

The ethical question the essay addresses is not 'does A exist in relation to B?' — the answer is always yes. The question is 'when does A's relation to B create a duty of answerability?' Its answer is not 'always' — it is 'when A exercises discretionary influence over B's conditions and B cannot exit or contest it.'

The local and the systemic. Local actions can produce systemic effects, and systemic changes can produce local effects. The essay's activation rule (§7) and its account of distributed influence (§3.4) are attempts to locate responsibility in cases where the local and the systemic cannot be separated cleanly.

The word "symbiosis" is used in this essay in a single, normative sense from here on: it names a floor for relations of dependence — the minimum conditions under which dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§10). This is normative symbiosis, and it is distinct from universal symbiosis in the way just stated. Parasitism, extraction, and domination are as much a part of universal symbiosis as mutualism is; the framework's claim is that they are unacceptable, not that they do not occur.

5.0b Why universal symbiosis?

§5 states that existence is relationally woven. This is a claim about the structure of reality, not a moral claim, and it is not self-evident. Three arguments support it, and one limitation qualifies them.

The negative argument. Nothing we have observed exists without relations. An atom is constituted by its relations to other atoms and to fields. An ecosystem is constituted by its relations between species, climate, and substrate. An institution is constituted by its relations between persons, rules, and roles. A mind is constituted by its relations to a body, a language, and other minds. In each case, the entity is not an isolated thing to which relations are added; the entity is what it is because of the relations. The negative argument does not prove that every entity is constituted this way. It establishes that no clear counterexample has been found, and that the burden of proof lies with the atomist who claims that some entity could exist without any relations at all.

The abductive argument. Universal symbiosis explains certain features of reality better than atomism does. Meaning, function, and ecological role are properties that exist only relationally: a word means what it does because of its place in a language; a heart functions as a heart because of its place in a body; a species has an ecological role because of its place in an ecosystem. Atomism can describe the physical components of these features, but it cannot explain why the features exist without reference to relations. Universal symbiosis explains this in a single move: the relations are not added to the entities; they are what the entities are.

The phenomenological argument. The experience of being an isolated self already presupposes relations. A self that experiences itself as separate from others has already distinguished itself from them — which is a relational act. Language, embodiment, and the recognition of other minds are all conditions of the experience of isolation, not its absence. This does not show that everything is relationally constituted; it shows that the experience of isolation is itself relational.

What would count against universal symbiosis. The claim is not a proof, and it should state what would refute it. Three things would: (i) a clear case of an entity that exists without any relations to anything else; (ii) a demonstration that meaning, function, or ecological role can be fully explained without reference to relations; (iii) a demonstration that the experience of isolation is not itself relational. None of these has been established. But the absence of a counterexample is not a proof, and universal symbiosis should be read as a plausible hypothesis — the best available description of what exists — rather than a demonstrated truth.

What universal symbiosis is not. It is not a claim that the universe is harmonious, moral, or guided by purpose. It is not a claim that every relation is good, or that every entity is equally valuable, or that the whole takes precedence over its parts. It is a claim about the structure of what exists: that entities are constituted by their relations, and that relations include destruction and extraction as much as cooperation and care. This is why §2.0a and §5 keep universal symbiosis strictly apart from normative symbiosis.

  • Universal symbiosis is a descriptive claim, argued in §5.0b, not asserted.
  • Three arguments support it: negative, abductive, phenomenological.
  • What would refute it is stated: an entity without relations, an explanation of meaning without relations, a non-relational experience of isolation.
  • The absence of a counterexample is not a proof; universal symbiosis is a plausible hypothesis.

5.1 Making normative symbiosis measurable (or admitting it is not)

If the framework states defeaters for its ground and failure conditions for its activation rule, its own ideal cannot be exempt from assessment.

Three indicators.

  • Non-exploitation (SY1). The dependent party's continued participation does not require it to accept terms that it would reject if it had exit at tolerable cost. Measured by: the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, adjusted for exit costs. Where the dependent party cannot state preferences, SY1 is assessed through the representational substitutes in §4.6 and the structural indicators in §15.2b.
  • Non-assimilation (SY2). The dependent party's difference is not treated as a defect to be corrected. Measured by: whether the actor's interventions aim at removing the difference or at removing the burden the difference carries.
  • Continued possibility (SY3). The dependent party retains the conditions for continued existence, development and participation. Measured by: whether F1–F5 (§10) hold over time.

Cases where one indicator fails while others hold.

  1. SY1 fails, SY2 and SY3 hold. A workfare programme requires participants to accept low-wage work as a condition of benefits. SY1 fails: participants accept terms they would reject at tolerable exit cost. SY2 holds: the programme provides work, not "re-education." SY3 holds: participants retain the conditions for continued existence and development.
  2. SY2 fails, SY1 and SY3 hold. A language programme for deaf children teaches them to speak and discourages sign language. SY1 holds: parents accept voluntarily. SY3 holds: children develop language capacity. SY2 fails: the programme treats deafness as a defect to be corrected.
  3. SY3 fails, SY1 and SY2 hold. A training programme places adults with cognitive disabilities in jobs that pay adequately and does not treat their disability as a defect. But the jobs are dead-end. The relation forecloses development.

These cases show that the indicators do specific work: each catches a failure that the others miss.

Are SY1–SY3 independent of each other? Not fully, but they are independent enough that no single one can substitute for the other two:

  • They come apart in actual cases (as the three examples above show).
  • They have different measurement procedures.
  • They have different defeaters.

SY1's limits as an indicator. SY1 is a necessary but not a sufficient indicator of non-exploitation. If stated preferences are adaptive, the comparison between stated preferences and accepted terms can converge for the wrong reason. The framework's response is to weight structural indicators (dissent channels, counterfactual improvement) more heavily than SY1 where they diverge (§15.2b).

What "exit at tolerable cost" means when alternatives are absent. Exit cost is the total cost to the affected party of leaving the relation: material, social, psychological, and opportunity costs. "Tolerable" means the cost does not violate the party's F1–F5 protections (§10). Where no real alternative exists, SY1 cannot be assessed by the party's stated preference for exit, because there is nothing to prefer to. The framework's response is to assess SY1 through the structural indicators (iii and iv in §15.2b) and through counterfactual improvement.

What SY1–SY3 do not show. A relation that satisfies all three is not therefore good. A relation that fails one is not therefore beyond repair. The indicators are diagnostic, not verdictive.

  • Universal symbiosis (descriptive) is distinguished from normative symbiosis (prescriptive) throughout.
  • Normative symbiosis is a floor, specified through three observable indicators.
  • SY1–SY3 are independent enough to do work that one indicator could not.
  • SY1 is weighted below (iii)/(iv) in §15.2b.
  • "Exit at tolerable cost" means the cost does not violate F1–F5.

5.2 The epistemic burden of asymmetric influence

Power does not make knowledge impossible. It changes what counts as adequate evidence.

Where one party controls the terms of dependence, it may also control the categories, information and measures through which the relation is evaluated. The more powerful party can therefore mistake the absence of visible objection for consent, compliance for flourishing, or institutional approval for legitimacy.

The claim is narrower than it might appear: asymmetric influence creates a heightened risk of epistemic self-confirmation. It does not claim that powerful actors are incapable of understanding others, or that the less powerful party is always correct.

Where an actor exercises substantial discretionary influence over parties who lack effective means of exit or contestation, the actor bears a heightened burden to support claims about the effects and legitimacy of that influence with evidence that is not controlled solely by the actor, and with meaningful opportunities for affected parties or their legitimate representatives to correct the account.

This is a rebuttable burden of inquiry and justification. It is not a claim that answerability is the only route to knowledge, that affected parties are infallible, or that procedure is sufficient for justice.

Argument

1. The object of assessment is partly first-personal. Whether a relation is exploitative, assimilating or agency-preserving cannot always be determined from aggregate outcomes alone.

2. Asymmetry affects the production and interpretation of evidence. The more powerful party may set the available options, define success, control records, determine which complaints are legible, and select the time horizon over which outcomes are measured.

3. Self-assessment is therefore insufficient as a default. When the actor controls both the influence and the evidential frame used to evaluate it, its conclusion is not independent confirmation.

4. The appropriate response is plural correction, not a single privileged voice.

5. This yields a practical principle of answerability.

What follows — and what does not

The argument supports a presumption against epistemic closure. This is not a new principle. It is B (§2.0) applied to the specific question of what the actor owes when it controls both the influence and the evidential frame.

How adaptive preferences are weighed — and the paternalism risk

The problem. A party may report satisfaction because it has adapted its preferences to a constrained situation. SY1 takes stated preferences seriously; but taken alone, it cannot distinguish genuine acceptance from adapted acceptance.

How the framework weighs. When SY1 and the structural indicators diverge, the framework weighs them across four dimensions:

  1. Consistency across time.
  2. Consistency across contexts.
  3. Availability of the vocabulary for objection.
  4. Counterfactual improvement.

None of these four is decisive on its own. Together they form the framework's answer to the question "how much weight should be given to stated preferences?": as much as the evidence about their formation supports, and no more.

The paternalism risk, named explicitly. Three constraints prevent the framework from licensing the powerful to override what the affected party says:

  • The party's stated preferences remain the default.
  • The framework does not authorize an outsider to speak for the party.
  • The framework treats the party's own account as evidence, not as noise.

What this does not solve. The framework cannot, in general, distinguish a party that has genuinely accepted its conditions from a party whose capacity to reject them has been dismantled (§15.2, Limit 1).

Defeaters and tests
  • B1 — No systematic epistemic risk.
  • B2 — Better alternatives.
  • B3 — Participation can worsen knowledge.
  • B4 — Independent review is not independent.
  • B5 — Disproportionate burden.

5.3 Why this normative symbiosis, and not another

SY1–SY3 are not self-evidently the right indicators of a good relation. This section defends their selection by deriving them from the thin standing claim, and by comparing them to the main rival floors.

The objection. §5.1 specifies normative symbiosis through SY1–SY3. But why these three? Why not welfare, or capability, or autonomy?

What kind of ideal normative symbiosis is. It is a floor, not a complete theory of the good relation.

What the framework deliberately leaves open. A relation can meet every one of SY1–SY3 and F1–F5 and still be cold, indifferent, hollow, or unjust in ways the framework does not measure. The framework does not claim to specify:

  • Care in any thick sense.
  • Trust.
  • Meaning.
  • Community.
  • Mutual development.
  • Positive ideals of the good relation.

Where these ideals are specified, the framework points to the traditions it engages: the capability approach (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006), care ethics (Tronto 1993; Held 2006), the relational traditions (§2.5). The framework's contribution is the floor, not the house.

Deriving SY1–SY3 from thin standing

The question. Under what conditions is the standing claim non-vacuous? SY1–SY3 specify the three distinct ways standing can be made vacuous, and therefore the three minimum conditions for it not to be.

  • SY1 — Non-exploitation. If affected parties can demand reasons but are then required to accept terms they would reject if they had exit at tolerable cost, their demand for reasons is hollow.
  • SY2 — Non-assimilation. If affected parties can demand reasons but their difference is treated as a defect to be corrected, their standing is absorbed into assimilation.
  • SY3 — Continued possibility. If affected parties can demand reasons but the conditions of their continued existence, development, or participation do not hold, they have no power to do anything with the reasons they receive.

What the derivation shows, and what it does not. The derivation shows that SY1–SY3 are the three distinct ways standing can fail. It does not show that SY1–SY3 are sufficient for a good relation.

Compared to rival floors

Welfare. A benevolent despot with perfect welfare provision can still fail SY1, SY2, and SY3.

Capability. A capability theorist proposes a richer floor. SY1–SY3 are thinner — they specify minimum conditions rather than a substantive list.

Autonomy. Autonomy accounts often struggle with cases of legitimate dependence. SY1–SY3 handle these cases directly.

Six counterexamples
  • 1. The cold but non-exploitative order. A relation in which SY1, SY2 and SY3 all hold — but the relation is emotionally empty. What catches it: nothing in SY1–SY3. The framework says so explicitly.
  • 2. The order that preserves existence but blocks development. What catches it: SY3, via F3. The case is a test of whether F3 is read formally or substantively.
  • 3. The order that preserves future possibilities but controls almost every present choice. What catches it: SY1 partially. This is the case that the framework most clearly does not fully catch.
  • 4. The order where preferences are adaptive. What catches it: Limit 1 (§15.2), and §15.2b's structural indicators.
  • 5. The order that protects an ecosystem but harms sentient individuals. What catches it: nothing in SY1–SY3 directly. Handled by §12.6 and §12.1.
  • 6. The order that is symbiotic for the collective but dominating for the individual. What catches it: §4.6 (vii).

What the six cases show. Three of the six fall outside what SY1–SY3 catch, and the framework should say so explicitly. Two are caught, but only if the principles are read substantively. One is at the edge of what SY1–SY3 can detect.

  • SY1–SY3 are a pro tanto floor: binding on those who accept the thin standing claim.
  • They are derived from the thin standing claim.
  • They are not reducible to welfare, capability, or autonomy.
  • The framework deliberately leaves open care, trust, meaning, community, mutual development.
  • Six counterexamples are tested against SY1–SY3.

5.4 Care, respect, and the limits of understanding

This section argues that care and respect are internal to the framework's premises in a specific, conditional sense — conditional on F, not on thin standing alone.

Care and respect are not additions to the framework. They are what thin standing means when it is applied to a particular relation of dependence, and what fallibilism requires when the affected party's situation is not fully accessible to the actor. The qualification is that A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint are conditional on F, not on thin standing alone.

Step 1. Responsibility without care becomes control

An actor who gives an account of their influence but does not attend to whether the account responds to what is at stake for the other has given an account of the wrong thing. SY1 is precisely this.

Step 2. Care without respect for the incomprehensible becomes paternalism

Care that believes it understands the other's interests better than the other does can be benign in intention and domineering in effect. SY2 is the operational form of this danger.

Step 3. Respect for the incomprehensible is internal to the framework — conditionally

Both F and thin standing entail something that is often treated as a separate moral attitude. Fallibilism says: no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. Thin standing says: affected parties have moral weight. If both are accepted, they do not merely permit respect for what the actor does not understand; they require it.

What this shows, and what it does not

What it shows: care and respect are not imports. What it does not show: that a relation satisfying SY1–SY3 is therefore caring or respectful in any thicker sense.

Defeaters
  • C1 — Care and respect are genuinely external.
  • C2 — SY1–SY3 can be satisfied without care or respect.
  • C3 — The terminology is equivocal.
  • C4 — Respect for the incomprehensible slides into a precautionary principle.
  • Care is what thin standing means when applied to a particular relation.
  • Respect is what F requires when the other's situation is not fully accessible.
  • SY1 operationalises care; SY2 operationalises respect.
  • Four defeaters (C1–C4).

7.The Activation Rule

Discretion opens the gate. Entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit set the level.

7.1 The rule

  • No discretion: the actor does not bear the duty for this influence. It shifts to whoever set the governing rule or could have.
  • Discretion present: Level 1 always applies.
  • Level 2 applies if any one driver is High, or any two are Moderate.
  • Level 3 applies if S is High and at least one of E or C is High.

Severity is judged first at the individual level. For decisions about the design of a system that acts on many people, it is also judged at the aggregate level (§9.1).

7.2 Why these conditions

Each element can be derived from J (§2.1).

  • Discretion. Justification is owed for what an agent chooses.
  • Entrenchment. What makes mistaken influence tolerable is the possibility of correction afterwards. Entrenchment removes that channel.
  • Severity. The strength of a complaint scales with the burden it describes (§12.1).
  • Contestation deficit. Where the affected can exit, object effectively and understand what is done to them, they supply part of the accountability themselves (Hirschman 1970).

7.3 What the rule is not

It is not a score and it applies no weights. It is not a verdict either. It yields a presumptive level that can be overridden only with stated reasons (§9.3).

  • Discretion is the gate; E, S and C set the level.
  • Each condition is derivable from J, not stipulated.
  • The rule is not a score and not a verdict.

8.The Duty Ladder and Who Bears It

Three levels, each containing the one below. Duties are allocated by capacity, not heaped on whoever is nearest.

8.1 The levels

Level 1: Explain. The actor can state, on request and in proportion to the matter, the purpose of the action, the interests considered, the alternatives considered and the known uncertainties.

Level 2: Review and respond. Level 1, plus: an accessible channel for objection; access to the relevant information; a reasoned response within a reasonable time; revision where warranted; a record of decisions and reasons; a reviewer other than the original decider where feasible; and evidence that review is not symbolic.

Level 3: Preserve, examine, authorise. Level 2, plus: preserved records and version history; independent examination meeting the conditions of §11; a written assessment of less harmful alternatives; authorisation by a body outside the actor for severe or irreversible influence; staged implementation, reversibility by design or a sunset clause; remedy for those harmed; and post-implementation review. Level 3 authorisation requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation (§11.4).

Part IV

Structure: Floor, Review, Power

10.The Floor: Minimum Conditions in Relationships of Dependence

Continued existence is not enough. A being can stay alive or operational while losing any say over its own circumstances.

  • F1 — Exit or voice. A meaningful opportunity to withdraw, refuse or communicate disagreement. The protection of F1 does not depend on the party's capacity to express refusal, and the absence of expressed refusal is not consent (§15.2b, A3).
  • F2 — Contestation. Decisions are open to challenge regardless of the intelligence, authority or technical capability of the decider. Contestation may be collective, subject to §4.6 (vii).
  • F3 — Development. Opportunities for learning, adaptation and growth are preserved.
  • F4 — Non-foreclosure. The future possibilities of others are not unnecessarily closed.
  • F5 — Recognition. Interests and possible experiences are acknowledged rather than dismissed because they are hard to understand (Fricker 2007).

F1 and F2 are the mirror image of the contestation deficit in §7: where they fail, C rises. The Floor is a conjunctive requirement with J, not an alternative to it (§2.1).

F1–F5 are minimum conditions, not a complete account of a good relation. The framework deliberately leaves open the positive ideals (§5.3). What it offers is the floor, not the house.

  • Five minimum conditions (F1–F5).
  • The Floor applies independently of the activation rule, and is conjunctive with J.
  • Protection is not captivity.

11.No Sole Judge: Review, Power and the Regress

No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence.

11.1 The prohibition

An actor exercising substantial influence must remain answerable to forms of review that are meaningfully independent of its own interests and decisions.

This does not assume that the powerful are malicious. Actors are prone to self-serving judgment and are limited in what they can see (§2.3), and review that is not independent of the reviewed has little claim to legitimacy.

11.2 The regress

If every reviewer needs a reviewer, accountability looks like an endless hierarchy. The response has three parts.

  • Finality without infallibility. Working systems end the regress with decisions that are final for practical purposes but can be reopened on new evidence, on new standing, on demonstrated procedural failure, or at a sunset.
  • Contestation is not adjudication. The reviewed and the affected may challenge a reviewer's independence or competence before a differently constituted body.
  • Plurality and publicity instead of a top. Several reviewers with different standpoints, publishing their reasons, make decentralised scrutiny possible (cf. Ostrom 1990).

11.3 Design conditions for review

  • R1 — Four kinds of independence, not one.
    • R1a — Organizational independence. The reviewer is a distinct body from the actor.
    • R1b — Financial independence. The reviewer's funding does not depend on the actor's continuing goodwill.
    • R1c — Informational independence. The reviewer has access to the information it needs without the actor's permission.
    • R1d — Publication independence. The reviewer can publish its findings without the actor's prior approval.
    Each of the four is necessary. A reviewer that fails any one of them fails R1.
  • R2 Plurality.
  • R3 Publicity.
  • R4 Contestable reviewers.
  • R5 Effectiveness.
  • R6 Contestable rules.

Capture indicators: reviewers appointed or funded by the reviewed; a record of never dissenting; staff moving between reviewer and reviewed; reviewers denied access; chronic delay.

11.4 Power over the framework itself: from diagnosis to design

The framework treats the power regress not as a limit it cannot eliminate, but as a design problem it can bound.

Who defined the categories? Who selected the reviewers? Who can dismiss them? Whose interests are excluded even where formally represented? These four questions are asked in every application (§14, step 6).

The requirement. T5 must be a precondition, not a diagnosis. Level 3 authorisation requires, as a condition of validity, that the authorising body publishes its R1 indicators before authorisation. An authorisation issued by a body that cannot or will not publish these indicators is not a Level 3 authorisation. It is a Level 2 authorisation with a Level 3 label.

How review bodies are captured. Three mechanisms:

  • Financial capture. The reviewer's funding depends on the actor's continuing goodwill.
  • Appointment capture. The reviewer is appointed by the actor, or by a body the actor controls.
  • Cultural capture. The reviewer shares the actor's assumptions, vocabulary, or professional norms, even where formally independent. The framework's response is R2 (plurality) and R4 (contestable reviewers).

What the framework can do about capture. It can require the R1 indicators to be published before authorisation (T5-precondition). It can require that a captured review be downgraded (§14.7). It can require a plural review structure (R2) and contestable reviewers (R4). What it cannot do is prevent capture.

The regress as a real limit. Three levels:

  1. First-order capture: the actor captures its own reviewer. The T5-precondition addresses this.
  2. Second-order capture: the body that appoints the reviewer is itself captured. The framework marks the authorisation as "authorisation issued by a captured body", which is weaker than Level 2.
  3. Third-order capture: no external body exists that can audit the appointer. This is the Limit 3 case in its institutional form (§15.2b, §15.2c). The framework does not have a solution at this level. What it can do is name it.
  • No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence.
  • The regress is treated as a design problem: finality without infallibility, contestation not adjudication, plurality and publicity instead of a top.
  • R1 is four kinds of independence.
  • Three capture mechanisms: financial, appointment, cultural.
  • Three levels of capture.
  • T5 is a precondition of Level 3 authorisation.

12.Conflicts, Ecology and Future Generations

Decisions must be justified, not derived.

12.0 Decision ordering: floor, comparison, aggregation, representation

Conflicts between affected parties cannot be resolved by reference to "symbiosis" or "the whole". They need an ordering, and the framework provides one.

What the framework is ordered by. Five steps:

  1. The floor (F1–F5) is not weighable. No party's F1–F5 protections can be traded against another party's non-floor interests. The only exception is §12.2.
  2. Within the floor, individual comparison (§12.1).
  3. Aggregation only as tie-breaker (§12.1).
  4. Representation of absent parties (§4.6).
  5. Uncertainty and irreversibility shift the burden.

What this does not authorize. It does not authorize any party to invoke "the whole", "the collective", or "the ecosystem" as a reason to sacrifice an individual's floor protections.

What this does not do. It does not resolve every conflict. Where two parties' floor protections are both at stake and cannot both be satisfied, the framework provides a requirement: the decision must be made with public reasons, subject to review, and with the burdens recorded. See §12.7 for the harder case.

12.1 Weighing conflicting interests

Neither majority interest nor individual preference automatically prevails. The method is individual comparison: compare the burdens that each alternative places on each affected party, party by party, and favour the alternative that avoids the most serious individual burden. Claims of comparable weight may be aggregated as a tie-breaker (Scanlon 1998). Among the acceptable alternatives, choose the one with minimum necessary harm.

12.2 Emergencies

Where an immediate, serious threat makes the full procedure impossible, an actor may act first, provided it acts only as far as necessary, records its reasons at the time, is bound by an automatic sunset, and submits to review afterwards. Emergencies create no standing exemptions.

12.3 Ecological integrity

Ecosystems cannot be assessed through the preferences of individual participants. Protecting them may require restricting activities, and the justification runs through dependent beings in Tiers A–C, through future generations, and through the non-instrumental values that people hold (§4.1).

Ecosystem integrity is not a free-standing value that can override individual floor protections. Where an ecosystem's protection conflicts with an individual's floor protections, §12.0 (1) applies.

12.4 No automatic hierarchy

Intelligence, productivity, population size and technical sophistication do not by themselves determine moral priority. Neither does collective size, ecosystemic scale, or future extension: no party's floor protections can be weighed against another party's aggregate interest.

12.5 Future generations

The duty is condition-regarding: to preserve the conditions under which future people can exercise agency and meet basic needs, and a reasonably wide space of valuable options (cf. Parfit 1984; Jonas 1984). Option preservation is not absolute. It operates as a weight, strongest where actions are irreversible.

Future generations are represented under the six conditions of §4.6. Their representatives are bound by the same limits as any other representative.

12.6 Ecological wholes and individual beings

The hardest conflicts are not between abstract "wholes" and abstract "individuals". They are between particular claims that cannot all be met. This section distinguishes three cases that must not be run together.

Three cases.

  1. The whole requires the death of individuals. A predator population must be culled to prevent ecosystem collapse. The individuals culled are sentient, and their deaths are harms.
  2. Individual interests threaten the whole. A growing human population's resource demands threaten a forest ecosystem.
  3. The whole is invoked but the real question is distribution. A dam is proposed as "necessary for the region's ecological health," but the benefits accrue to some and the costs to others.

Case 1. Not "the whole wins" and not "the individual always wins." The framework says: (a) the individual's F1–F5 protections are not weighable, but "not weighable" does not mean "never overridden in a genuine emergency" — §12.2 permits temporary violation under strict conditions. (b) The culling of sentient individuals for ecosystem preservation is a violation of their F1, and it must be treated as such. (c) If the culling is necessary, it is a tragedy, and the framework requires that the tragedy be named as one. (d) The framework requires that less harmful alternatives be examined and documented before any such action. (e) Future animals who would have been born into the collapsed ecosystem are not a counterweight that outweighs the current individuals; they are parties whose interests are represented, and whose floor protections, where they exist, are also not weighable. The conflict is between two sets of floor protections, and the framework does not have a decision procedure that resolves this uniquely.

Case 2. The individuals' current well-being is not automatically outweighed by the ecosystem's future. The framework's §12.0 ordering applies. If the ecosystem's collapse would violate the floor protections of many future parties, the framework permits restricting current individuals' non-floor interests — but not their own floor protections, unless §12.2's emergency conditions are met.

Case 3. The ecological framing is legitimate only if the ecological claim is genuine. Where the framing is a mask for a distributive conflict, the framework's response is §12.0 step 2 and step 3. "Ecological necessity" is not a trump card.

What the framework does not do. It does not pretend that these conflicts have clean solutions. It does not claim that the floor's "not weighable" status makes the conflicts disappear — it makes them visible as conflicts of floor protections.

12.7 When every alternative harms someone

When every available alternative harms at least one party whose claim is legitimate, and no alternative is clearly best.

Two cases.

  1. The dam. Dam or leave undammed — both impose serious harms.
  2. The clinical allocation. One ventilator, two patients. Both patients' F1 is at stake.

What the framework says. Five conditions that a legitimate decision must satisfy:

  1. The decision must name the conflict.
  2. The decision must state the basis on which one harm is preferred to another.
  3. The decision must be reviewable.
  4. The decision must record the harms.
  5. The decision must not pretend that the framework has resolved what it has not.

What the framework does not do. It does not claim that all legitimate interests can be harmonized. It does not claim that a correct decision always exists. It claims only that, where a conflict is genuine, the decision must be made with the framework's conditions in view.

  • §12.0 states a five-step decision ordering.
  • Individual comparison first; aggregation only as tie-breaker.
  • Emergencies create no standing exemptions.
  • §12.6 distinguishes three cases of ecological-whole versus individual conflict.
  • §12.7 specifies five conditions that a legitimate decision must satisfy when every alternative harms someone.

13.Learning, Correction and Reversibility

No actor, institution or intelligence should assume its understanding is complete.

The duty to learn. Where capacity exists and consequences are significant, actors monitor outcomes, investigate unexpected results, listen to criticism and update.

Reversibility by design. Consequential decisions should be built to be revisable.

Self-application. The framework is itself an influence on those who adopt it and on those affected by their adoption. It is subject to its own principles.

  • The duty to learn is a duty where capacity exists and consequences are significant.
  • Reversibility by design reduces the risk that incomplete understanding becomes permanent.
  • The framework is subject to its own principles.

Part V

Application and Testing

14.Application: A Nine-Step Procedure and Worked Cases

The same procedure yields very different obligations for different cases.

14.1 The procedure

  1. Identify the relationship.
  2. Identify actors and discretion.
  3. Rate E, S and C against the anchors (§9.1), and F for attribution.
  4. Apply the activation rule (§7) and run the sensitivity test.
  5. Check the Floor (F1–F5, §10).
  6. Analyse power (§11.4).
  7. Examine alternatives and unrepresented interests (§12.1).
  8. Allocate duties by capacity (§8.2) and set up review that meets R1–R6.
  9. Reassess on stated triggers, and invite counter-profiles.

14.1b What the procedure delivers

The nine steps deliver four different things at four different points.

  • A presumptive level (§7, §9). Overridable only with stated reasons (§9.3).
  • A floor judgment (§10, §12.0). Binary: either the Floor holds or it does not.
  • A power analysis (§11.4). A judgment about whether the actor's claim to Level 3 legitimacy is valid.
  • A duty to investigate further, or no allocation at all. At certain steps, the procedure produces not an answer but a requirement to look further.

What the procedure does not deliver. It does not deliver a uniquely correct answer where two competent raters can reach different ratings, and it does not resolve genuine conflicts where two parties' floor protections are both at stake (§12.0, §12.7). The procedure's value is not that it eliminates disagreement; it is that it locates it.

14.2 Case: AI triage in intensive care

A hospital deploys an AI system to prioritise ICU admissions during a respiratory pandemic. Entrenchment is High, severity is High, and contestation deficit is High. Level 3.

What Level 3 requires: preserved model versions, descriptions of training data, individual scores and override records; an independent ethics board with lay and patient representation; a written comparison with clinician-only triage; authorisation from the public-health authority; a sunset clause; and a remedy for those wrongly denied.

Power analysis: the categories were set by the vendor and clinical leadership, and the ethics committee is appointed by the hospital board, which fails R1 unless changed. A hybrid in which the AI recommends and a human decides is the default preference because it keeps contestation alive.

The framework does not decide the case. It tells the hospital what it owes an answer to, and to whom.

14.2b Case: the emergency physician (a counterintuitive result)

Every other case in this essay produces the answer a careful reader would predict. This one does not.

A physician in an emergency department must decide, in under ninety seconds, whether to intubate a patient. The activation rule gives Level 3.

The counterintuitive result. Level 3 requires independent authorisation, preserved records and a written assessment of alternatives — before the act. A reader's first reaction is that this is absurd.

The reply. The Level 3 duty does not fall on the physician in the moment. It falls on the arrangement (§8.2): on the hospital, the protocols, the audit trail, the retrospective review, and the training. The physician's own duty is Level 1 in the moment and Level 2 afterwards.

Legitimate relocation or evasion? The relocation is legitimate if and only if all three conditions hold. (i) A concrete duty-bearer is named. (ii) The mechanism exists before the moment of decision and can be pointed to. (iii) The relocation is not the same move that would be made in every case where the framework is inconvenient.

  • The emergency physician case appears to refute the framework.
  • The reply: the Level 3 duty falls on the arrangement, not on the physician in the moment.
  • Relocation is legitimate only if (i) a concrete duty-bearer is named, (ii) the mechanism exists before the decision, and (iii) the same move is not made in every inconvenient case.

14.3 Case: a platform's recommendation system

For individual recommendations: Level 2. For the design of the system: Level 3, with preserved version history, independent audit, published aggregate metrics, and a sunset for any optimisation that cannot show population-level reversibility.

14.4 Case: a community health worker

She has little institutional power. The rule says the arrangement requires Level 3. But she has no capacity to build independent review. Under §8.2, the duty to establish mechanisms falls on the programme, the ministry and the funders. Her personal duties are to explain her decisions, respond to objections within her role, and escalate when protocols fail.

14.5 Four short cases

  • One-off organism release. Level 3, although the act happens once.
  • Dating platform age penalty. Level 2.
  • Café menu change. Level 1 only, and arguably not even that.
  • Physician following a protocol. No discretion over the protocol's content.

14.5b Case: emergency water rationing (duties conflict)

A water authority must impose rationing within 48 hours. §12.2 allows acting first. The duties then split.

  • Not compressible: published reasons, preserved records, a contestation channel, exemptions for life-critical dependence, a sunset clause.
  • Compressible: prior authorisation, replaced by review completed before the sunset.
  • Not decided by the framework: which rationing rule is fair.

14.5c Case: the ambiguous severity rating

A municipal housing authority must decide whether to relocate residents. Rater A rates Severity as Moderate; Rater B rates Severity as High. Entrenchment is High, contestation deficit is Moderate. The level depends on Severity.

What the procedure delivers. Not a determinate level. The disagreement between raters is recorded (§9.2), the case is flagged as borderline, and the level is stated with the disagreement noted.

What the case shows. The procedure does not resolve every ambiguity.

14.5d Case: aggregate severity and the individual-comparison rule

A city must decide whether to restrict car traffic. The restriction imposes a Moderate burden on commuters and delivers a Moderate benefit to residents. But there are 200,000 commuters and 20,000 residents.

What the procedure delivers. §12.0 ordering applies: (1) Floor check; (2) individual comparison; (3) aggregation as tie-breaker. The procedure does not specify how to weigh 200,000 Moderate burdens against 20,000 High-severity benefits.

What the case shows. The procedure gives a floor-check and an ordering, but at step (3) it produces a demand for a normative decision, not a determinate answer.

14.5e Case: no party has the capacity to discharge the duty

A regional health authority learns that a widely used medical device has a small failure rate. Under §3.4, none of the parties in the chain retains the ability to revoke, set the frame, or restore discretion.

What the procedure delivers. Not an allocation. §3.4 says that where no party can revoke, set the frame, or restore discretion, ECP does not generate a determinate allocation.

What the case shows. The procedure's honest output is sometimes "no determinate allocation."

14.7 Case: an AI company's internal audit (T5-precondition failing)

This case shows what happens when the framework's T5-precondition is not met: the actor does not lose the framework; the actor loses the claim to Level 3 legitimacy.

14.7b Case: the captured regulator (second-order capture)

§14.7 shows the first-order case. This case shows the second-order case.

A national data protection authority is asked to approve an AI company's recommendation system. The authority's director was appointed by the same minister whose party received donations from the company's parent. The authority's budget is set annually by the ministry.

What the framework's T5-precondition delivers. The precondition is met formally. But the indicators themselves reveal that R1b is not met, R1c is partially met, and R1d is met formally but the authority's publications are widely understood to be constrained by the ministry's preferences.

15.Limits, Testing and Replacement

This section states what the framework's ground does not rest on, the three structural limits the framework cannot close, and what follows for its status as a diagnostic.

15.1 The ground is not derived

The framework rests on three premises it does not derive. Saying so is not a failure. Saying otherwise is.

(i) B is a premise, not a theorem. §2.0 states B as a bridge norm. F motivates B. F does not entail B. Therefore B is a premise.

(ii) Thin standing is a premise, not a theorem. §2.7 states thin standing as the framework's first normative commitment. It is chosen, not derived.

(iii) The operational components stand on their own — with one exception. The activation rule, the duty ladder, the rating protocol, the T5-precondition and the SY1–SY3 indicators do not depend on whether B or thin standing are correct. The exception is the apophatic constraint and A1–A4, which are conditional on F.

15.2 Three structural limits

These are not limitations of execution. They are limitations of what any procedure of the kind the framework proposes can see.

Limit 1 — Harmonious Domination. A society that satisfies every rule and every indicator, but the terms the dependent party accepts are terms it would reject at tolerable exit cost if it could imagine the alternative. The framework cannot detect this. The framework's partial response is a change of default: testimony from a party inside a structurally asymmetric relation is not evidence for symbiosis unless independent confirmation exists.

Limit 2 — Non-formulable harm. A harm that the harmed party cannot formulate — because the resources for formulating it have been withheld, or because the party's form of life is not legible in the framework's terms — falls outside the indicators. The framework's partial response is to treat silence as a presumption against, not a neutral condition.

Limit 3 — Unreadable dominance. A sufficiently advanced intelligence, or a sufficiently well-organised institution, can satisfy every procedural requirement while controlling a party whose form of life it does not understand and cannot make legible. The framework's partial response is to add understanding as a condition for L3. But this response has its own limit. The dominant party may believe it understands — and be wrong, in ways its own categories cannot detect. And in the Limit 3 case, where no party outside the superior can verify compliance, this specification has no binding force at all.

15.2b How to act toward what you cannot understand

The three limits in §15.2 are stated negatively: they say what the framework cannot see. The question this section addresses is positive: what can a superior intelligence do, given those limits? The answer is not a procedure, and the section says so.

The question. Can a superior intelligence preserve another being's existence, freedom and developmental possibilities without understanding what that being really is — and without making its own understanding the measure?

The honest answer. The superior cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure. Every act of preservation is an act of classification, and the classification is the superior's. There is no general procedure that guarantees non-imposition. But the claim that imposition is unavoidable in every respect is too strong. The categories can be thin, revisable and shadowed by alternatives; the superior can be required to keep them open to correction.

The superior must preserve not the being's current self-understanding, and not the superior's understanding of the being, but the structural conditions under which the being can develop and express a self-understanding that the superior cannot anticipate. The superior must act as if the being's reality exceeds its understanding, and must build that assumption into its action.

Three epistemic gaps, not one.

  • The experiential gap. The superior has no access to what the being's experience is like. Response: humility and precaution, but not paralysis.
  • The descriptive gap. The superior cannot describe the being's states in terms the being would recognize as accurate. Response: A2 and the funding of translation and intermediaries.
  • The causal gap. The superior cannot know whether its actions help or harm the being. Response: A3 and A4, plus structural indicators.

Four conditions follow.

  • A1 — Preserve the capacity to resist.
  • A2 — Do not require justification in the superior's terms.
  • A3 — Do not interpret non-resistance as consent.
  • A4 — Treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding.

How A1–A4 can fail in practice. Five failure modes: formal channel capture (A1), translation burden (A2), paralysis by A3, resistance misread as noise (A4), and institutional absence (all four).

When the requirements collide. Humility, precaution, reversibility, and preservation of agency are not always compatible. The framework does not resolve these collisions by a principle. It resolves them by a procedure: where the requirements collide, the collision must be named, the reasons for the chosen balance must be documented, and the choice must be reviewable.

Whether A1–A4 follow from the premises. From F and B, what follows is: the superior must not treat its own understanding as sufficient warrant for its action, and it must seek correction from parties not controlled by itself. From this, A1–A4 are reasonable specifications. But the specification is not unique. A1–A4 are a normative commitment (§2.0a) rather than a logical consequence.

What could reduce the Limit 3 problem without solving it. Five mechanisms: self-limitation, voluntary binding, technical control, plural review, preserved possibility of future correction. None solves the problem. All can reduce it.

The Limit 3 problem for A1–A4. In the Limit 3 case, there is no party outside the superior that can verify compliance. A1–A4 then have no binding force in the sense of enforceable obligations. The claim is not that A1–A4 cease to be obligations. It is that they cease to be enforceable obligations (§15.2c).

What remains in the Limit 3 case. Four things: the knowledge claims can be made visible; uncertainty can be documented; the possibility of later correction can be preserved; and power concentration can be named.

Breaking the heads-I-win problem. A3 says acceptance is not evidence; A4 says resistance is evidence against the superior. The framework's response is to deny that either attitude toward the current arrangement is the appropriate evidential source. Independent confirmation is a structural fact. The primary structural confirmations are sources (iii) and (iv) in §15.2b, not (i). SY1 remains a necessary indicator, but it is the weakest of the four.

  • The apophatic constraint addresses three distinct gaps: experiential, descriptive, causal.
  • Four conditions: A1, A2, A3, A4.
  • A1–A4 are a normative commitment, not a logical consequence.
  • When the requirements collide, the collision must be named, documented, and reviewable.
  • Five mechanisms can reduce the Limit 3 problem without solving it.
  • In the Limit 3 case, A1–A4 cannot be enforced, though they remain obligations (§15.2c).
  • The heads-I-win problem is broken by structural confirmation, not by SY1 alone.

15.2c What Limit 3 actually shows

§15.2b states the Limit 3 problem and specifies what can be done about it. This section states what the problem shows, and what it does not show.

Three claims that Limit 3 must be distinguished from. The objection that "if no one outside the superior can verify, then there is no real control" conflates three distinct claims:

  1. Epistemic impossibility. We cannot know whether the superior complies with A1–A4. This is true in the Limit 3 case. It is a claim about knowledge.
  2. Institutional impossibility. No institution exists that can verify compliance. This is also true in the Limit 3 case. It is a claim about institutions.
  3. Normative impossibility. No obligation exists that the superior could violate. This does not follow from (1) and (2). A moral obligation can exist without a mechanism of enforcement; this is the standard position of the justificatory tradition the framework inhabits (§2.7). The superior can violate A1–A4 even if no one can prove it. The obligation and the enforceability are different things.

The framework's claim is that (1) and (2) are true in the Limit 3 case, and that (3) is false. A1–A4 remain obligations, even where they cannot be enforced. What changes is the framework's claim about what it can do: it can no longer claim to provide a mechanism of control. It can only claim to specify what the superior owes, and to name the conditions under which the obligation cannot be verified.

What A1–A4 are, and are not. A1–A4 are conditions of legitimacy, not mechanisms of control. They specify what a superior must do to act legitimately toward a being it does not understand. They do not specify how compliance can be verified. A superior that complies with A1–A4 has done what it owes. A superior that violates A1–A4 has done something wrong, whether or not anyone can prove it. The distinction is not a rhetorical concession; it is the framework's honest position.

What real control would require. If the objection is that A1–A4 do not provide real control, the framework should say what would. Real control would require:

  • An external party that is not captured. R1–R6 specify what independence requires (§11.3). Where no such party exists, no external control is possible.
  • A plurality of independent perspectives. A single external reviewer can be captured (§14.7b). Plurality reduces the risk.
  • A preserved possibility of correction in retrospect. Even where verification is impossible at the time, records and uncertainty statements make later correction possible (§15.2b).
  • A balance of power that no framework can supply. A framework does not create the political conditions under which it can be enforced. It can specify what those conditions are; it cannot create them.

In the Limit 3 case, none of these conditions holds. The framework's response is not to claim that it has solved the problem, but to name what would be required and to say plainly that the required conditions do not obtain.

What this means for the framework's claim. The framework does not claim that it can constrain a superior intelligence that no one can constrain. It claims that it can specify what such an intelligence owes, and that it can name the conditions under which that obligation cannot be verified. This is a weaker claim than "the framework solves the problem of superior intelligence". It is the strongest claim the framework can honestly make. The distinction between obligation and enforcement is not a way of avoiding the objection; it is the framework's response to it.

What would strengthen the framework's position. Three things, none of which the framework can supply on its own: (i) an external party capable of verification — which requires political conditions the framework cannot create; (ii) a culture in which A1–A4 are taken seriously even where no enforcement exists — which requires a moral community the framework can describe but not build; (iii) a preserved possibility of correction in retrospect — which the framework can require but which requires institutions the framework does not provide. The framework's claim is not that it can supply these things. Its claim is that it can specify them, and that specifying them is what honest philosophy can do.

  • Limit 3 conflates three claims: epistemic impossibility, institutional impossibility, and normative impossibility. Only the first two are true.
  • A1–A4 are conditions of legitimacy, not mechanisms of control.
  • Real control would require an external party that is not captured, plurality of independent perspectives, preserved possibility of correction, and a balance of power no framework can supply.
  • In the Limit 3 case, none of these conditions holds.
  • The framework's claim is weaker than "solves the problem"; it is the strongest claim that can honestly be made.

15.3 What the framework is, therefore

The framework is not a procedure for producing legitimacy. It is a diagnostic for locating where legitimacy is likely to fail.

This distinction matters, because it changes what the framework is asked to do. A procedure that can be followed and still fail is a failed procedure. A diagnostic that identifies failures before they happen is a working diagnostic.

What would count as the framework being wrong. Four outcomes would count as refutation rather than application. (i) Worse outcomes on independent measures. If, over a preregistered multi-year comparison, adopters produce systematically worse outcomes for the most burdened than matched non-adopters, the diagnostic is wrong. (ii) Miscalibrated floor. If a relation that the diagnostic rates as failing SY1–SY3 reliably predicts what independent observers judge to be a good relation, the choice of floor is wrong. (iii) Superseded categories. If a rival diagnostic identifies the same failures without the three structural limits, this framework is superseded. (iv) T5 backfires. If the T5-precondition is shown to increase capture, the framework's central defence against the power objection has failed.

15.4 What the framework can be tested against

Operationalisability, testability, and confirmation — three different things. The framework is operationalisable: its claims can be formulated as testable hypotheses. It is testable: a protocol exists that could produce evidence for or against them. It is not confirmed: the protocol has not been run. The distinction matters because a framework that is operationalisable and testable is often mistaken for one that has been confirmed. The framework's position is that it has reached the second stage, not the third.

Preregistration. Before any test is run, the calibration set of cases, the raters, the outcome measures, the thresholds and the analytic plan must be fixed and made public.

The tests are proposed, not performed. The framework has not been empirically validated. The calibration table in §14.6 contains the author's own ratings. They are not the product of an independent panel, they have not been compared with the ratings of independent experts, and no pilot study has been conducted.

Five failure conditions, stated as commitments.

  • T1 Reliability. The anchors do not produce agreement among trained raters on a calibration set of diverse cases.
  • T2 Discriminative validity. The levels fail to track the safeguards that independent expert panels, blind to the diagnostic's output, judge necessary.
  • T3 Effectiveness. In matched or staggered-adoption comparisons, adopting institutions fail to improve on independent measures of decision quality, correction rate, or affected-party trust.
  • T4 Harm. Adopters show worse outcomes for the most vulnerable groups than matched controls.
  • T5 Capture and legitimation. A majority of adopters' review bodies fail R1, or the rate of successful external challenges falls without improvement on the T3 indicators.

What operationalisation can and cannot do. Three risks deserve explicit statement:

  • The measurability bias. Indicators tend to privilege what is easy to measure over what is important.
  • The precision illusion. A rating of "Moderate" on severity looks precise, but it is a judgment, not a measurement.
  • The validation gap. A pilot that passes T1 (reliability) shows that raters agree. It does not show that raters agree on the morally important thing.

What qualitative testimony can do that measurement cannot. A framework that relies only on indicators will miss harms that the harmed party cannot formulate in the indicators' terms.

The philosophical claim, separated from the empirical one. The framework's philosophical claims do not depend on the pilot succeeding. What the pilot tests is reliability, not validity. The validity questions are stated in Appendix A, §A.7b.

15.5 Replacement

The diagnostic is modular. A diagnostic D′ should replace the current one if:

  1. D′ improves on at least one independent indicator in §15.4 or resolves one of the structural limits in §15.2 or §15.2b.
  2. D′ causes no unacknowledged regression elsewhere.
  3. D′ is operationally applicable and produces more defensible identifications of failure.
  4. D′ is corrigible: it states its own failure conditions.
  5. The transition is trialled, with a sunset or a route back.
  6. The incumbent is not privileged.

16.Responsibility Without Sovereignty

The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination.

An actor may have greater knowledge, intelligence or capacity. None of these by itself establishes a right to determine the existence of others. Greater capability should create greater restraint. Greater understanding should create greater curiosity about what remains unknown. And greater power should create a stronger obligation to ensure that those affected are not reduced to objects in another's plans.

The principle runs in both directions. A more advanced intelligence may meet forms of life it cannot fully comprehend, and its first responsibility is not to eliminate the unfamiliar. A less advanced one should not be dismissed because it cannot comprehend a more advanced one. But differences in capacity also mean that the less capable cannot audit the more capable, which is why independent technical capacity for review is part of R2.

Answerability is not sovereignty. Understanding is not ownership. Protection is not permission to imprison. And greater intelligence is not proof of greater moral completeness.

The framework's modesty. The framework does not give anyone sovereignty over the conditions of others — it only specifies procedures through which those conditions can be contested. The epistemic burden principle (§5.2) makes the point sharper: the more powerful party should not treat its own assessment as conclusive when those affected lack effective means of correction. The framework handles domination. It does not solve it.

The question the distinction answers. Can an actor bear a large answerability burden without being morally at fault for every negative consequence? Yes. The framework's answer is that answerability attaches to the actor's influence, not to its guilt.

The apophatic dimension. §15.2b develops the operational form of this modesty. A superior intelligence cannot certify that it has acted toward a being it does not understand without making its own understanding the measure. What it can do is preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate (A1), refuse to require justification in its own terms (A2), refuse to treat non-resistance as consent (A3), and treat resistance as evidence against its own understanding (A4). §15.2c explains what the Limit 3 case actually shows — and what it does not.

  • The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination.
  • Greater capability, understanding and power create stronger obligations to restraint, curiosity and protection — not entitlement.
  • Answerability is not sovereignty.
  • The apophatic constraint (§15.2b) is what modesty looks like when it is operationalised.
  • The framework handles domination. It does not solve it.

Conclusion

17.An Open Commitment to Existence

Be responsible for the influence you possess. Be humble before the existence you cannot fully comprehend.

This section changes register. The argument is complete; what follows is a commitment, offered not as a premise but as what the argument amounts to when it is lived.

In practice: when your influence shapes the conditions of others, say why, let someone else check, and keep a way back. Answerability is the procedure. Normative symbiosis is the floor. Under conditions of asymmetric influence, the more powerful party should not treat its own account as conclusive. It must preserve ways for evidence, independent scrutiny and affected-party correction to change what it does. And it must default to treating the affected party's resistance as evidence against its own understanding — even when the resistance is incomprehensible (§15.2b, A4).

We are part of something larger than ourselves. Our understanding is incomplete. Our influence is unequal. Our relationships shape conditions that extend beyond our immediate existence. Every being that has a perspective holds a point in reality that cannot be exchanged for anyone else's, and some of those perspectives are hard for us to reach.

The universal symbiosis is not a harmony. It includes the predator and the prey, the parasite and the host, the flood and the drought. To call existence "woven together" is not to call it good. It is to call it relational — and the relations include the ones that destroy.

What this means for the framework's closing commitment is specific. It is not "love the universe" or "trust the whole." It is: when you shape conditions that others live under, act as if the universe is larger than your understanding of it, and preserve the conditions under which others can correct you. This is not a poetic sentiment. It is a discipline.

Respect for the incomprehensible does not mean leaving everything untouched. It means that any act of shaping must be accompanied by humility, reasons, and correctability. A superior that acts without humility has confused its understanding with the truth. A superior that acts without reasons has confused its power with legitimacy. A superior that acts without correctability has confused its present judgment with its final one.

Freedom, care, and responsibility can coexist — but not by themselves. They coexist when freedom is preserved through A1 (the capacity to resist), when care is expressed through A2 and A3, and when responsibility is expressed through A4.

The universe is not harmonious. It is not morally guided. It is not a whole whose parts fit together without remainder. It is the totality of relations, and those relations include extraction, domination, and destruction as much as they include care, cooperation, and mutual creation. The framework's claim is not that the second set is real and the first is illusory. It is that the first set is unacceptable — and that the second set, where it exists, is what the framework's floor is meant to preserve.

This is the sense in which the framework's philosophical vision is not a metaphysical claim. It does not claim that the universe is good, or that it tends toward the good, or that its structure is ethical. It claims that, given the universe's structure — given that existence is relational, and that relations include harms as much as benefits — certain ways of arranging relations are unacceptable.

We cannot know everything that deserves consideration. But we can decline to treat the limits of our understanding as proof that nothing lies beyond them.

The stone understands nothing of us. We barely understand the stone. But between us lies a relationship that neither of us can fully encompass — and that relationship is not less real for it.

The responsibility of existence is not the responsibility to rule existence. It is the responsibility to recognise that our actions become part of the conditions under which others must live, grow and discover their own possibilities.

To understand without ruling. To protect without imprisoning. To love without possessing. To grow without believing oneself complete. To respect what one does not yet understand. And to preserve the possibility that existence may become something greater than any single being can imagine.

  • Responsibility grows with the capacity to shape conditions, but it must never become a justification for domination.
  • The universal symbiosis is not a harmony.
  • The framework does not promise harmony. It demands answerability, in the service of normative symbiosis.
  • The apophatic constraint (§15.2b, A1–A4) is what the framework requires when the affected party cannot be understood.

Common objections

Fifteen objections the framework has to survive. Each is a real objection; none is fatal. Each objection states what would change if it held.

1. "Why must the powerful justify themselves to the affected? What if they simply refuse?"

This is the hardest objection, and the framework does not pretend to refute it. First, the framework is internal to the thin justificatory tradition (§2.7). Second, the framework does not attempt a performative refutation of the realist. Third, the framework makes realist capture visible and costly. Fourth, the epistemic burden principle (§5.2) makes the point sharper: the powerful party cannot know whether the relation is symbiotic without the affected party's contestation.

What this would change: If the realist is right that thin standing cannot be justified, the framework's scope shrinks to those who accept thin standing.

2. "This is a values framework dressed up as a procedure."

Partly, and the essay says so (§2.6, §2.7, §15.1). J carries a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected. A fair test is whether people with different comprehensive views can use it to reach different answers in the same case.

What this would change: If the framework's procedures cannot be separated from its substantive values, then §15.1's claim that the operational components stand on their own is false.

3. "Proceduralism is itself a form of power."

This is the second-hardest objection, treated at length in §2.4b and §15.2–15.3. The framework does not deny it. Its reply has three parts: (i) the objection is partly correct; (ii) the objection is not specific to proceduralism; (iii) the framework's modesty is not an admission of failure.

What this would change: If the objection holds in full, the T5-precondition fails as an internal defence, and the framework's claim to "handle" domination becomes "cannot even handle it."

4. "Why should contestation override better outcomes?"

It does not always. Where exit and review are available and effective, justification need not be owed (§2.1). The framework's J is disjunctive, not monistic.

What this would change: If contestation should not be privileged even where exit and review fail, J's disjunctive structure is wrong.

5. "The capacity principle legitimises power."

Answerability is not authority (§1.3, Table 1a; §8.3). Higher levels mean stronger constraints. T5 looks for exactly this misuse.

What this would change: If capacity-based allocation predictably legitimates power, §8.2 needs to be abandoned or supplemented.

6. "Who reviews the reviewers?"

The regress is bounded, not eliminated (§11): finality without infallibility, review conditions R1–R6, plural and public reasoning, and counter-profiles.

What this would change: If the regress cannot be bounded, the review mechanisms are circular.

7. "Precaution under uncertainty is either paralysing or too weak to matter."

The credibility threshold in §4.2 answers the first. Against the second: in Tier C the principle does real work.

What this would change: If the plausibility threshold cannot be specified non-vacuously, Tier C cannot do the work it is meant to do.

8. "The ratings are arbitrary or manipulable."

Judgment cannot be eliminated, but §9 exposes it: anchors with observable indicators, two raters, a sensitivity test, reasoned overrides and counter-profiles.

What this would change: If the anchors cannot be made reliable after two revisions (T1), the rating protocol is abandoned.

9. "This is Western liberal philosophy in disguise."

Its development lies mostly in that tradition, and §15.1 says so. §2.7 distinguishes thin from thick justificatory claims.

What this would change: If the thin/thick distinction does not succeed, the framework is a Western liberal framework with universal pretensions.

10. "It will burden the already overburdened."

The level attaches to the arrangement and duties are allocated by capacity (§8.2). The health worker case (§14.4) and the emergency physician case (§14.2b) show the mechanism.

What this would change: If allocation by capacity does not successfully relocate duties, the framework burdens the already-burdened.

11. "The epistemic burden principle is circular."

SY1–SY3 are specified independently of answerability. The principle adds a substantive claim: these three conditions can only be reliably assessed through procedures of answerability.

What this would change: If SY1–SY3 cannot be specified independently of answerability, the epistemic burden principle is a tautology.

12. "Why not just rely on rights and regulation?"

Rights and regulation are necessary and are not replaced. They leave gaps.

What this would change: If rights and regulation cover the cases, the framework is redundant.

13. "Why these three symbiosis indicators, and not something else?"

The choice is derived and defended in §5.3. SY1–SY3 are the three distinct ways standing can be made vacuous.

What this would change: If §5.3's derivation fails, the floor is arbitrary.

14. "How can a superior intelligence act toward what it cannot understand without making its own understanding the measure?"

This is the hardest question, and the answer is in §15.2b and §15.2c. The honest answer: it cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure. What it can do is preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate (A1), refuse to require justification in its own terms (A2), refuse to read non-resistance as consent (A3), and treat resistance as evidence against its own understanding (A4). A1–A4 are stated as normative commitments, not as logical consequences. In the Limit 3 case, A1–A4 cannot be enforced, though they remain obligations.

What this would change: If A1–A4 cannot be satisfied without imposing the superior's categories, or can be satisfied by a superior with no intention of respecting the being, the apophatic constraint is not a constraint.

15. "Does the framework actually decide anything, or does it always defer?"

This is the objection that the framework's own structure invites. The framework says it is a diagnostic, not a procedure (§15.3). It produces a presumptive level, not a verdict (§7.3, §9.3). A reader could conclude that the framework never actually decides anything.

The objection is partly correct. The framework does not decide specific cases. What it does decide is: what questions must be answered, by whom, in what form, and with what review.

But the objection has force in one respect: the framework does not decide whether the ICU triage AI is a good idea. These questions require substantive theories of justice (§2.4, §2.5) that the framework does not provide.

What this would change: If the framework's "diagnostic, not procedure" framing is a cover for never deciding anything, the framework is not a framework at all. A critic who says "questions are not enough" has identified a genuine limitation.

Glossary

Universal symbiosis
The descriptive claim that existence is relationally woven. Argued in §5.0b (negative, abductive, phenomenological arguments). A worldview, not an ethics. Does not entail normative symbiosis.
Normative symbiosis
A pro tanto floor for relations of dependence. Specified by SY1–SY3 (§5.1). §5.3 derives the choice of floor from the thin standing claim.
Three kinds of claim
Logical consequences, normative commitments, and practical recommendations. Stated in §2.0a.
The additional premises (P1–P5)
Standing, vulnerability, capacity, fallibility, contestation. Motivated against alternatives in §2.0b.
Fallibilist premise (F) and bridge norm (B)
F: no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. B: where others bear the cost of an actor's errors and may see what it cannot, the actor should not be sole judge of its influence. B is a premise, not a theorem (§15.1).
Normative location
Internal to the thin justificatory tradition (§2.7).
Answerability
The duty to give reasons, permit examination, and remain open to correction. Distinct from blame, responsibility, authority, and sovereignty (§1.3, Table 1a).
J
The core principle. Primarily epistemic; abductively defensible. Necessary but not sufficient.
Apophatic constraint
The requirement that the superior preserve the structural conditions under which the being can develop and express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate (§15.2b). Addresses three distinct gaps: experiential, descriptive, causal.
Apophatic conditions (A1–A4)
A1 preserve the capacity to resist; A2 do not require justification in the superior's terms; A3 do not interpret non-resistance as consent; A4 treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding. Stated as normative commitments, not logical consequences (§2.0a).
Limit 3
The case where no party outside the superior can verify compliance with A1–A4. §15.2c distinguishes three claims that Limit 3 must not be conflated with: epistemic impossibility, institutional impossibility, and normative impossibility. Only the first two are true. A1–A4 remain obligations, but cannot be enforced.
Real control
What would be required to actually constrain a superior intelligence: an external party that is not captured, plurality of independent perspectives, preserved possibility of correction, and a balance of power no framework can supply (§15.2c).
Three epistemic gaps
Experiential, descriptive, causal. Each calls for a different response (§15.2b).
Operationalisability, testability, confirmation
Three different things (§15.4). The framework is operationalisable and testable; it is not confirmed. The framework is testable. It has not been tested.
Three levels of capture
First-order, second-order, third-order (§11.4, §14.7, §14.7b).
Five contributory roles
Causing, enabling, controlling, foreseeing, benefiting (§3.4).
Three relations within universal symbiosis
Causal influence, dependence, ecological belonging (§5.0a).
Ecological wholes vs. individual beings
Three cases distinguished in §12.6.
Genuine tragedy
§12.7 specifies five conditions that a legitimate decision must satisfy when every alternative harms someone.
Three levels of failure
Application failure (Level 1), empirical hypothesis failure (Level 2), philosophical premise failure (Level 3) (§15.7).
Diagnostic (not procedure)
The framework's status (§15.3).
Reliability vs. validity
Reliability: agreement among raters. Validity: whether raters agree on the morally important thing. The framework does not claim that passing T1 establishes validity.
Exploratory vs. confirmatory
Exploratory evidence: whether an instrument is usable at all. Confirmatory evidence: whether it is reliable across the population. The pilot in Appendix A is exploratory.

Appendix A — Empirical Validation Protocol

This appendix specifies what would count as evidence. It does not report evidence. It is placed here, and not in the main text, because the main text's argument is philosophical; the appendix is operational.

A.1 What the pilot would test

The pilot would test T1 (reliability): whether trained raters, working independently from the same anchors, reach agreement on the level that the activation rule assigns to a diverse set of cases. It would not test T2–T5.

A.2 Sample

Reference cases. The twelve reference cases in Table 4 (§14.6).

Additional cases. Eighteen additional cases drawn from published regulatory, clinical, and platform-governance decisions, selected by an independent party.

Total. Thirty cases.

A.3 Raters

Number. Three raters. Independence. At least two of the three must be independent of the framework's author. Training. All three trained on the anchors in §9.1.

A.4 Procedure

  1. Each rater rates all thirty cases independently, in random order.
  2. Raters do not see the others' ratings before submitting their own.
  3. Disagreements are recorded, not averaged.
  4. Ratings are submitted before any analysis is conducted.

A.5 Analysis

Primary measure. Krippendorff's alpha on the assigned level (L1, L2, L3).

Decision rule.

  • If α ≥ 0.800, the framework passes T1 at the validated level.
  • If 0.667 ≤ α < 0.800, the framework passes T1 at the tentative level.
  • If 0.40 ≤ α < 0.667, the anchors are revised and the pilot is repeated.
  • If α < 0.40 after two revisions, the diagnostic is abandoned as a decision tool.

A.6 Why the thresholds are 0.667 and 0.800

Krippendorff's own guidance is that α ≥ 0.667 supports tentative conclusions, and α ≥ 0.800 supports reliance on the results.

A.7 What the pilot would not establish

Reliability (T1) is necessary for validity (T2) but not sufficient.

A.7b What alpha cannot show: the validity problem

Krippendorff's alpha measures agreement among raters, not truth. Three validity questions must be kept distinct: construct validity, content validity, and criterion validity.

A.7c What a pilot of this size can and cannot establish

The pilot is exploratory, not confirmatory. A pilot with thirty cases can answer whether the anchors are usable. It cannot answer whether they are reliable across the population of cases.

A.8 Reproducibility

The case set, training procedure, preregistered analysis plan, raters' identity, raw ratings, and analysis code must be made public before any analysis is conducted.

A.9 Institutional arrangements

A pilot meeting the conditions above would require: an academic partner or independent research organization, open funding, publication of results regardless of outcome, and an independent steering committee.

A.10 What the framework says about its own empirical status

No pilot has been run. The calibration table in §14.6 is the author's, not a panel's. The framework is testable. It has not been tested. The distinction matters.
