Abstract

In one sentence: when you exercise discretionary influence over the conditions under which others live, choose and develop, and when they cannot exit or contest that influence effectively, you owe them an account of it — and you cannot be the sole judge of whether that account is good enough.

What this essay is, and what it is not. It is a framework for deciding when influence creates a duty to answer, how demanding that duty is, who must discharge it, and how it can be discharged without turning into new domination. It is not a new foundational principle in the tradition of Pettit, Forst or Jonas. Its core principle (J) is a thin, disjunctive, abductively supported synthesis of existing traditions, not a demonstration of necessity. J is necessary but not sufficient: it must be paired with the Floor (§10) and assessed against SY1–SY3 (§5.1), and a relation can satisfy J genuinely and fail substantively (§2.1). Its contribution is stated openly in §1.5 and shown in Table 1: an interlocking operational set (activation rule, duty ladder, T5-precondition, epistemic burden principle, allocation by exercised capacity) that no single tradition supplies. Its normative location is stated openly in §2.7: internal to the justificatory tradition, but only to its thin version. Its choice of symbiosis floor is derived and defended in §5.3. Its status as a diagnostic — not a procedure for certifying legitimacy — is stated in §15.3. Its hardest question — whether a superior intelligence can preserve what it does not understand — is addressed in §15.2b, which states the apophatic constraint and four structural conditions (A1–A4), and says plainly that in the Limit 3 case A1–A4 do not bind. The full consolidated list of what the framework does not claim is in §15.6.

1. Ground. J is a thin, disjunctive principle: arrangements that systematically constrain others' conditions must provide at least one of meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation. Which is required depends on the structure of the constraint — where exit and review work, justification need not be owed; where they fail, it is. J is primarily an epistemic principle: it protects against the actor confusing their own view with the truth. J is abductively supported — it offers a plausible explanation of why unchecked discretionary power can produce domination and systematic error — and it has stated defeaters (§2.2b). J is motivated by a fallibilist premise and a stated bridge norm (§2.0), not derived from them. It is not necessary as a deduction, but it is necessary as a condition for a non-arbitrary relation — and it is not sufficient. It is defensible. The framework handles domination; it does not solve it.

2. Standing and symbiosis. Who is owed an account, including beings that cannot speak for themselves, handled through four tiers of standing under uncertainty with an explicit plausibility threshold (§4). Symbiosis is specified with observable indicators (SY1–SY3), so that the framework's floor is as contestable as its procedure (§5.1). Symbiosis is a floor, not a complete theory of the good relation, and the choice of floor is derived from the thin standing claim and compared against rival floors (§5.3). SY1–SY3 are not fully independent, but they are independent enough to do work that no single indicator could — they come apart in actual cases, have different measurement procedures, and have different defeaters (§5.1). SY1 is the weakest of the four sources of confirmation in §15.2b: where preferences are adaptive, stated preferences and accepted terms may converge for the wrong reason, and structural indicators (iii) and (iv) should be given more weight. Collective contestation may represent silent parties, but it must not undermine individual contestation (§4.6 vii). Section §5.2 develops a more limited claim: asymmetric influence creates a heightened burden to seek correction from sources not controlled by the decision-maker.

3. Activation. One rule: a discretion gate plus three drivers (entrenchment, severity, contestation deficit) that determine one of three levels of duty, with anchors and a rating protocol that make the judgments visible and contestable (§6–9).

4. Structure. A floor of minimum conditions, the prohibition on being sole judge of one's own influence, the reviewer regress treated as a design problem, and accounts of conflict, future generations and learning (§10–13).

5. Application and testing. Worked cases including one that appears to refute the framework (§14.2b) and one that shows the T5-precondition failing (§14.7), five proposed evaluation conditions for the activation rule with independent measures, provisional thresholds, preregistration requirements, a pilot specification, and specific institutional arrangements (§15.4), and modular conditions for replacement (§15.5). The empirical tests are proposed, not performed.

Answerability is the procedure. Symbiosis is the floor. The framework does not claim that a relation meeting SY1–SY3 is therefore good. It claims that a relation failing SY1–SY3 is not good, whatever else it is.

The framework has not been empirically tested. Its activation rule is specified so that it can be (§15.4); its other parts are argued and offered for contestation. It is a diagnostic, not a procedure: its job is to locate where procedure will pass while something valuable is still being lost, not to certify that legitimacy has been achieved (§15.3). The apophatic constraint (§15.2b) is what follows: the superior cannot certify that it has avoided imposing its own understanding, but it can preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate — provided a party outside the superior can verify compliance. In the Limit 3 case, where no such party exists, A1–A4 do not bind, and the framework states this as a limit rather than a solution.

The argument in brief

The essay is long, and its spine can be stated in a page. This is that page.

  1. The problem. Influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act is not adequately covered by existing moral vocabulary — blame, rights, consent, regulation. This is the gap the framework addresses (§1).
  2. The principle (J). Arrangements that systematically and non-trivially constrain others' conditions must, for those affected, provide at least one of: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation. Where exit and review work, justification need not be owed; where they fail, it is (§2.1).
  3. Why J. J is abductively supported: it offers a plausible explanation of recurring risks associated with unchecked discretionary power. Four traditions — justificatory, republican, epistemic, relational — make J plausible, but they are treated as considerations, not as independent proofs. J has stated defeaters (§2.2b).
  4. The trigger. Influence creates the duty when discretion is present and exit or review is unavailable or ineffective. How much duty scales with entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit (§7–§8).
  5. The epistemic claim. Under asymmetric influence, the more powerful party has weaker grounds for treating its own assessment as decisive. It therefore bears a heightened burden to obtain independent evidence and, where possible, correction from those affected. This is a defeasible claim, not a necessary condition for knowledge (§5.2).
  6. The limits. J is not sufficient. It is defensible. Its activation rule has proposed evaluation conditions; its ground and epistemic principle have stated defeaters. None of these is a proof of anything. They are invitations to argue.
  7. The hardest question. Can a superior intelligence preserve a being's existence, freedom and development without understanding what the being is — and without making its own understanding the measure? The framework's answer (§15.2b) is that it cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure, but it can preserve the conditions under which the imposition is nameable, revisable and resistible. Four structural conditions follow: preserve the capacity to resist (A1), do not require justification in the superior's terms (A2), do not interpret non-resistance as consent (A3), treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding (A4). These conditions are not self-enforcing, and in the Limit 3 case — where no party outside the superior can verify compliance — they do not bind at all (§15.2b).

What it is not. The framework is not a procedure for certifying legitimacy. A relation can follow every rule and still fail to be legitimate. The framework's job is to name that possibility, not to deny it.

If you read only one section after this page, read §7 — that is where the framework either works or does not. If you read only one passage, read §5.2 — that is where it makes its main epistemic proposal. If you want to know what the framework cannot do, read §15.2, §15.2b and §15.6.

Part I

Problem, Scope and Ground

1.The Problem, Scope and Terms

The capacity to shape other people's conditions has grown faster than the practices for answering for it.

Recommendation systems, credit scoring, clinical triage tools and AI assistants shape the conditions of millions. So do states writing policy and companies setting terms, and, at a different scale, clinicians, teachers, landlords and parents. Existing moral vocabulary covers parts of this territory: blame for wrongful acts, rights for protected interests, consent for voluntary exchanges, regulation for what law has already caught. What falls between them is influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act, and that nonetheless determines what others can do and become.

1.1 What the framework does and does not do

It answers four questions: when does influence create a duty to give an account; how demanding is that duty; who must discharge it; and how can review be organised so that it does not become a new instrument of domination. It does not tell anyone what to decide. It is not a decision theory, and it is not a substitute for law, rights or substantive standards of justice.

1.2 Scope conditions: when the framework does not apply

The framework is offered for a specific class of cases. It does not claim to apply everywhere.

  • Symmetrical relations. Where parties have roughly equal capacity to exit, review and contest, J is trivially satisfied or not engaged.
  • Relations governed by enforceable rights and effective regulation. Where law provides effective rights, review and remedies, the framework may add little. Formal legality alone is not enough: rights may be inaccessible, remedies delayed, or review structurally dependent on the actor. The framework supplements legal standards where those protections fail in practice; it never displaces them.
  • Purely causal influence without discretion. A person who is swept away by a flood does not owe an account to those downstream. The framework's trigger is exercised or maintained discretionary influence, not mere causal connection.
  • Trivial influence. A café changing its menu is not the case the framework addresses. The severity floor in §7 is meant to exclude it.
  • Relations where the affected party is not a candidate for standing. Stones and rivers as such do not have standing under this framework (§4.1).

1.3 Terms

Influence: the exercise or maintenance of control over conditions under which others act, choose, develop or fare. Maintaining a structure one could alter counts as influence. Mere ability to help does not (§3).

Actor: a person, organisation or institution, including those that design, deploy and maintain technical systems. Technical systems are treated as instruments, and answerability falls on the humans and organisations behind them.

Affected party: any being whose interests are materially affected by the influence.

Discretion: the actor has genuine alternatives at tolerable cost and a role in setting the influence, rather than merely executing a rule it had no part in making or contesting.

Answerability: a duty to give reasons for the conditions one helps create or maintain, to those affected or their representatives; to permit examination of those reasons; and to remain open to correction. It is distinct from blame, liability and authority.

Five terms that must be kept apart. The framework uses five terms that ordinary language often conflates. Each names a different thing, and the framework's claims depend on not mixing them:

Table 1a. Answerability, responsibility, blame, authority, sovereignty
Term What it names What it does not entail
Answerability The duty to give reasons, permit examination, and remain open to correction (§5.2) It does not entail blame (a party can be answerable without being at fault), liability (a party can be answerable without bearing the cost), or authority (a party can be answerable without having the right to decide).
Responsibility Being the party whose actions, omissions, or maintained structures make a difference to what happens It does not entail blame (causal responsibility can exist without fault), or authority (being responsible for an outcome does not give one the right to control it).
Blame A moral judgment that a party is at fault for a wrongful act or omission It does not follow from answerability alone. A party can be answerable for an outcome it did not cause wrongfully, and can be blamed for an outcome it is not answerable for.
Authority The justified power to make decisions that bind others within a specified domain It does not follow from answerability or responsibility. Being answerable for influence is not the same as having the right to exercise it. A higher duty level (§8) means stronger constraints, not greater authority (§8.3).
Sovereignty The claim to determine the conditions of others without external check It is not what the framework offers anyone. §16 says explicitly: "Answerability is not sovereignty." The framework specifies procedures through which conditions can be contested; it does not give anyone the right to determine them unilaterally.

The key implication. A party can bear a large answerability burden — can be required to explain, to submit to review, to justify — without being morally at fault for what happened, and without acquiring any authority over those it is answerable to. The framework's central claim is that these five things come apart, and that treating them as one thing is what produces the confusion the framework is meant to address.

1.4 Relation to existing work

The framework is a synthesis and an operationalisation, and its intellectual debts are substantial: Pettit and Lovett on domination, Forst on the right to justification, Scanlon on reasonable rejection, Young on structural responsibility, Bovens and Shoemaker on accountability and answerability, Tronto, Held and Goodin on care and vulnerability. Its contribution is comparative and operational, with a limited philosophical proposal rather than a new foundational principle.

1.5 The specific claim, in one paragraph

If the framework has a single contribution, it should be statable in a paragraph — and then shown.

The framework's contribution is not that non-domination matters (Pettit), that those subject to norms have a right to justification (Forst), that responsibility for structural harm is shared (Young), or that capability creates responsibility for the future (Jonas). All of that is in the tradition. The framework's contribution is the following claim:

The evidential burden of justification under asymmetric influence is not adequately captured by any of the existing traditions, because none of them specifies how the burden is allocated, how it is graduated by entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit, or how review mechanisms can be designed so that they degrade rather than merely consult the powerful actor's claimed legitimacy. The framework's contribution is to specify (i) the activation rule, (ii) the duty ladder, (iii) the T5-precondition, and (iv) the epistemic burden principle, as an interlocking set of operational proposals that no single tradition supplies.

Why this is not just a synthesis. A synthesis of existing positions would be a combination. The framework is not a combination. It is an interlocking set — each piece depends on the others, and the whole does work that none of the parts does.

Adjacent literatures the table does not name. Several operational components have close analogues in literatures not conventionally grouped with the four traditions in Table 1. Accountable governance (Bovens 2007; Mulgan 2003; Romzek & Dubnick 1987) supplies the concept of answerability and the distinction between answerability and accountability. Risk-based regulation (Black 2010; Hood, Rothstein & Baldwin 2001; Hutter 2005) supplies the graduated, risk-proportional structure that is the closest institutional analog of the duty ladder. Administrative-law proportionality (Alexy 2002; Cohen-Eliya & Porat 2011; Mashaw 1983) supplies the logical form of the level distinctions and the emergency exception (§12.2). Experimentalist governance (Sabel & Zeitlin 2010) supplies the iterative review-and-correction structure that the T5-precondition formalises. The precaution / cost-benefit debate (Sunstein 2005; Wiener 2016) supplies the threshold debate that §4.2 enters.

What the duty ladder adds to risk-based regulation. Risk-based regulation (Black 2010; Hood, Rothstein & Baldwin 2001) already graduates obligations by risk. The duty ladder adds three things that risk-based regulation does not supply. First, the ladder specifies what kind of obligation attaches at each level — explanation, review and response, or preservation and independent authorisation — not merely how much. Second, the ladder is tied to the T5-precondition: Level 3 authorisation is not valid without prior publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators. Risk-based regulation has no equivalent precondition; it can be satisfied by a captured regulator. Third, the ladder is allocated by capacity (§8.2), not by risk alone. A high-risk arrangement with a low-capacity actor does not heap the full obligation on that actor; it relocates the obligation to whoever can discharge it. Risk-based regulation tends to attach obligations to the regulated entity, whatever its capacity. These three differences are the ladder's contribution.

It does not show that the framework is better than those traditions — that would require showing that the operational components improve outcomes, which is a separate empirical question. Nor does it show that the traditions could not be extended to include the operational components.

2.The Ground and Its Support

The framework needs a thin, defensible principle, an abductive justification that is honest about its own weakness, and stated defeaters for the ground itself.

2.0 A motivating premise: fallibilism about understanding

F (fallibilism). No actor fully understands the conditions it shapes, or everything that depends on what it does. This is a claim about the limits of understanding, not about the cosmos and not about any ranking of beings.

B (bridge norm). Where an actor's influence falls on others who bear the cost of its errors, and who may see what the actor cannot, the actor should not be the sole judge of that influence; it should seek correction from them.

What F and B do. F alone yields no duty. A fact about limited understanding does not by itself entail a norm, which is why B is stated openly as a normative premise rather than smuggled in. Together, F and B motivate J (§2.1) and the epistemic burden principle (§5.2). They do not entail J.

The argument from F to B. The inference from fallibility to obligation is not automatic. It has four steps, and only the last is normative:

  1. Epistemic. An actor that exercises substantial influence may have incomplete or mistaken understanding of the parties it affects.
  2. Empirical. The affected party may bear the cost of that mistake without being able to correct it.
  3. Structural. The actor often controls both the decision and part of the evidence on which the decision is assessed.
  4. Normative. Therefore the affected party has a claim on the actor: that the actor not treat its own limited understanding as sufficient warrant for the influence.

Step (4) does not follow from (1)–(3) alone. What licenses it is B: the claim that those who bear the cost of an actor's errors, and who may see what the actor cannot, have standing to demand that the actor not be the sole judge of its own influence. B is not derived from F. It is stated, and it is stated as a claim about standing, not as a recommendation of prudence.

Why an obligation, and not merely prudence or benevolence. Prudence would say: it is in the actor's interest to seek correction, because the actor will do better if it does. Benevolence would say: it is kind of the actor to seek correction, because the affected party will suffer less if it does. B says neither. It says that the affected party has a claim on the actor — a claim grounded in the fact that the actor's errors fall on them and that they may see what the actor cannot. A claim is not a recommendation. It is something the affected party can press, and something the actor can fail to meet. That is what makes B a normative premise rather than a piece of advice. It is also why the epistemic burden principle (§5.2) is an obligation and not a counsel of caution: what the more powerful party owes is not better evidence for its own sake, but evidence that the affected party can contest.

Why B is weaker than symbiosis. A reader who rejects the framework's substantive ideal of symbiosis (§5) can still accept B. B requires only that the actor not be the sole judge of its own influence when others bear the cost of its errors. It does not require that the relation be non-exploitative, non-assimilating, or life-preserving. It does not require that the actor care about the affected party's good. It requires only that the actor not close the question of its own influence using only evidence it controls. A Hobbesian, a Kantian, a consequentialist, and a republican can all accept that. They may disagree about what the actor should do once correction is sought, but they need not disagree about the claim to correction itself. This is why §2.7 says the framework is internal to the thin justificatory tradition: B is the thin version, not the thick one.

What depends on F, and what does not. The motivation for J and for the epistemic burden principle draws on F and B. The exercised-capacity principle (§3) does not. The activation rule, duty ladder, rating protocol, floor and the T5 precondition are procedural and stand or fall on their own tests (§15.4). The apophatic constraint and A1–A4 (§15.2b) are the one exception: they are conditional on F, and §15.1 (iii) records the asymmetry. A reader who rejects F can therefore still use the framework's procedural machinery, but that reader loses A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint, and therefore loses the answer to the hardest question in §15.2b. This is a real loss, not merely a loss of motivation. The framework is weaker without F, and it should say so rather than claim otherwise. The full dependency map is in §15.1 (Table 7).

What F does not claim. It does not claim that understanding comes in a hierarchy with levels above and below, that the more capable are entitled to rule, or that stones or other non-subjects have standing (§4.3). Its one lesson for beings that fall outside the framework's threshold is epistemic: an actor's failure to see what something contributes is not evidence that it contributes nothing.

What would refute F as motivation.

  • FD1. There is an actor or domain whose understanding of the conditions it shapes is in fact complete. Then B has no motivation there.
  • FD2. The incompleteness is contingent and the actor alone can reliably remedy it. Then actor-controlled inquiry would suffice and correction by others is unnecessary.
  • FD3. Correction by those affected does not, on balance, improve the actor's account or outcomes. This is an empirical defeater that the tests in §15.4 can address.
  • FD4. J and §5.2 are as well supported without F as with it. Then F is illustration, not ground, and should be dropped.

FD4 is the one the framework most needs to take seriously. The claim made here is deliberately small: F gives a shared motivation for J and the epistemic burden principle, nothing more. A reader who prefers to omit F keeps J and the epistemic burden principle, but loses A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint (§15.2b). The framework overall is weaker without F — §2.0 says so, and this is a real loss, not a loss of motivation only.

2.1 The core principle (J)

Any arrangement that systematically and non-trivially constrains the conditions under which others act, choose or develop must, for those affected, provide at least one of three things: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification that is genuinely open to their contestation. Where exit is available at tolerable cost and review is effective, justification need not be owed. Where they are not, justification is required, and the strength of the requirement scales with how thoroughly exit and review fail.

J is deliberately thin. It does not say what is good, which outcomes are required, or who prevails when interests conflict. It says what kind of relationship must exist between those who shape conditions and those who live in them: one in which the affected are not simply subject to unaccountable power. The three alternatives are not equivalent — exit is often cheaper than review, and review is often cheaper than justification — but they share the same function: to prevent the actor from being the sole judge of its own influence.

What the qualifiers require. J's three disjuncts are not satisfied by the bare existence of exit, review, or justification. Each has a minimum threshold, specified elsewhere in the framework and collected here:

  • Meaningful exit requires that the affected party can leave at tolerable cost without losing the conditions for continued existence, development, or participation (F1, F3, F4, §10) — and that the cost of leaving is not itself a mechanism of the influence being escaped.
  • Independent review requires that the reviewer meets R1–R6 (§11.3): independent appointment, funding, and removal; plurality; publicity; contestable reviewers; effectiveness; contestable rules.
  • Justification genuinely open to contestation requires that dissent channels exist and have been used, that the vocabulary for objection is available, and that intermediaries not controlled by the actor exist (§15.2b, sources (iii) and (iv)). A published statement that no one can contest is not a justification in this sense.

A power structure can satisfy the form of any of these — a formal exit route, a formal review body, a formal statement of reasons — and fail the substance. When it does, J is not satisfied. The framework's diagnostic function (§15.3) is to identify this failure, not to pretend it does not occur.

When none of the three is practically possible. There are cases where exit is unavailable, review cannot reach the actor, and contestation is not open to the affected party. In such cases J is not satisfied, and the framework does not claim that it can be. What the framework can do is name the failure, identify which of the three disjuncts is missing and why, and — where possible — specify what would be required to restore one of them. What it cannot do is supply a fourth mechanism that works where all three fail. The framework's response to this limit is stated in §15.2 (Limit 3) and §15.3 (diagnostic, not procedure): it locates the failure; it does not solve it.

Why disjunctive, and not a single requirement. A monistic J (justification always) is vulnerable to the consequentialist, who can point to cases where exit and review do the same work at lower cost. A monistic J (exit always) is vulnerable to the republican, who can point to cases where exit is unavailable or costly and review is the only remedy. A monistic J (review always) is vulnerable to the relational, who can point to cases where review cannot reach the particular situation of the vulnerable. The disjunctive formulation is intended to avoid making one safeguard mandatory in every case.

J is primarily an epistemic principle. J is not a claim that justification makes actions right. It is a claim that actors who cannot be contested — whether by exit, review or justification — are more likely to be wrong, and that the practice of contestation is the best available protection against this. The epistemic framing is the old principle that no one should be judge in their own cause, applied to influence.

J is defensible, not necessary as a deduction. The framework does not claim that J is a necessary truth, an unavoidable implication of practical reason, or a transcendental precondition of agency, and it does not attempt to derive J from a transcendental argument (§2.3, §15.1). It claims that J is abductively defensible: that it explains important recurring risks of unchecked discretion while remaining compatible with plural substantive theories of justice.

J is necessary but not sufficient for justice

The objection. A disjunctive principle can be satisfied in more than one form. Can a power structure genuinely meet J and the Floor and still be deeply unjust? Yes. The framework says so, and this section says what follows.

The case. Consider a society that genuinely satisfies J and the Floor. Exit is available at tolerable cost; review is independent and meets R1–R6; justification is open to contestation, and the contestation is effective. F1–F5 hold. The society is not colonial, not captured, and not in bad faith. Yet it is deeply unjust, because the distribution of resources is such that one group, while formally free to exit and contest, cannot in practice use either: the cost of exit is tolerable in principle but not for them, and the contestation channels require resources they do not have. The society satisfies J and the Floor, and it is unjust. What it fails is not J or the Floor but something the framework does not measure: the material conditions under which formal freedoms are usable.

Why this case is harder than a case where J is gamed. A colonial administration that provides formal exit, formal review and formal justification, but where exit is prohibitive, review is captured, and justification is in bad faith, does not satisfy J. Its exit is not meaningful, its review is not independent, and its justification is not genuinely open to contestation. It therefore shows that J can be gamed, not that J is insufficient when genuinely satisfied. The resource-asymmetry case shows the stronger claim: even a genuine J-and-Floor society can be unjust, because J and the Floor are procedural and relational conditions, not distributive ones.

What the framework says. This case does not refute J; it shows what J was never meant to do. J specifies the relationship between those who shape conditions and those who live in them. It does not specify the distribution of resources, opportunities or capabilities. A society can satisfy J and the Floor and still fail on distributive justice. The framework's response is not to expand J to cover distribution — that would make it a substantive theory of justice, which it is not — but to say that J and the Floor are necessary conditions for a non-arbitrary relation, not sufficient conditions for a just one. SY1–SY3 catch some distributive failures (SY1 catches exploitative terms, SY3 catches foreclosed development), but they do not catch all of them. The framework is a diagnostic, not a verdict.

  • J can be genuinely satisfied and the relation still be unjust, because J is procedural and relational, not distributive.
  • The qualifiers — meaningful exit, independent review, genuinely contestable justification — are load-bearing, but they do not make J sufficient.
  • J must be paired with the Floor (F1–F5) and assessed against SY1–SY3; J alone is not sufficient.
  • J is a necessary condition for a non-arbitrary relation, not a sufficient one.
  • The framework is a diagnostic; J does not certify legitimacy.

2.2b What would refute J: stated defeaters

A principle that cannot be defeated is not a principle; it is a dogma.

  • D1 — The unaccountable-power hypothesis is false. If it can be shown empirically that actors with unaccountable discretion over others' conditions do not systematically produce worse outcomes — measured independently of the actors' own reports — than actors subject to exit, review or contestation, then J's epistemic justification collapses.
  • D2 — A better principle explains the same cases. If a rival principle explains all the cases J explains, does so more simply, and generates clearer practical guidance, then J should be replaced.
  • D3 — The convergence is an artefact. If it can be shown that the four "routes" to J (§2.3) are not independent considerations but one tradition dressed in four vocabularies, then the argument for J reduces to a single route.
  • D4 — The costs exceed the benefits. If adopting J produces worse outcomes for the affected than not adopting it — measured by independent indicators, not by the framework's own rating protocol — then J should be abandoned.

These defeaters are stated in advance so that the framework can be corrected. The operational form of D1–D4 — what would count against each, how strong the evidence would need to be, and what would be revised — is in §15.7 (Table 8).

2.3 Why J: an abductive justification

J is offered as a plausible explanation of two recurring risks of unchecked discretionary power: domination and epistemic error.

The framework does not rest on a single theory. It rests on an abductive inference: J is proposed as a compact explanation of a set of recurring risks associated with unaccountable power. The considerations motivating the inference are these:

  • Actors with unaccountable discretion have incentives and opportunities to overestimate their benevolence and their understanding of affected parties.
  • Exit, review and contestation can expose errors that an actor's internal assessment may miss; whether they do so reliably is an empirical question.
  • A plausible hypothesis is that errors may cluster around the actor's own interests and categories.
  • Institutions with effective safeguards may perform better on some relevant measures, but selection effects, institutional context and the quality of implementation complicate causal comparisons.

J is one compact principle that can organise these considerations. It is not the only principle that could explain them, but its attraction is that it accommodates several safeguards without requiring a single substantive theory of the good.

Four considerations, not four independent proofs. The framework draws on four families of consideration that point toward J:

  • The justificatory consideration. If principles governing relations among agents must be justifiable to each of them (Scanlon 1998; Forst 2012), then constraint that cannot be justified to the constrained treats them as objects of management rather than as participants.
  • The republican consideration. The distinctive wrong of domination is the standing capacity to interfere arbitrarily (Pettit 1997, 2012; Lovett 2010). Contestability is the remedy.
  • The epistemic consideration. Actors are fallible, partial about their own influence, and prone to self-serving judgment. Requiring them to explain and to expose their reasons to challenge improves decisions by almost any standard of what is good. This is the primary ground of J.
  • The relational consideration. Obligations arise within dependence. Those with power over dependents carry special responsibilities, and justification must attend to the particular situation of the vulnerable (Tronto 1993; Held 2006; Goodin 1985).

What this shows. The convergence of four considerations is support, not proof. The considerations are not fully independent, and the epistemic consideration is primary.

  • J is abductively supported, without claiming to be the only or uniquely best explanation.
  • Four families of consideration point toward J. They are not fully independent. The epistemic consideration is primary.
  • The convergence is offered as support, not as proof.
  • The defeaters for J are stated in advance (§2.2b).

2.4 Rivals, taken in their best forms

Consequentialism. Sophisticated consequentialists can accept J as an institutional implication, especially in its disjunctive form. The real disagreement is whether J is basic or derived.

Kantianism. It shares the core but grounds standing in rational agency, which creates problems for infants, the cognitively disabled and animals. §4 handles these through tiers.

Virtue ethics. It supports the humility the framework requires, but it is weak on structural questions.

Justice as fairness and capabilities. The capabilities approach (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006) supplies substantive accounts of what is at stake for people. The framework is procedural and can draw on these to calibrate severity (§9).

Rights. Rights are outputs, and the framework treats them as external constraints. What it adds is an account of when and how actors must justify influence in the space that rights and regulation leave open.

Realism. The realist who denies that power owes anything to the weak cannot be refuted from within the framework, because the framework is internal to the justificatory tradition (§2.7). The framework does not attempt such a refutation. In particular, it does not argue that the realist who argues for realism already presupposes J, because that would be a transcendental argument, and the framework's argument for J is abductive, not transcendental (§2.3, §15.1).

2.4b The power objection

A procedural framework that tells the powerful how to justify themselves may simply give them a new vocabulary for doing what they were going to do anyway.

The objection. The framework asks powerful actors to explain, to submit to review, to justify. But the powerful are precisely the actors with the resources to perform these rituals convincingly. Proceduralism, on this objection, does not constrain power. It legitimates power by giving it a veneer of accountability.

The reply, in three parts.

First, the objection is partly correct. Proceduralism can legitimate power. That is why §11.4 treats T5 as a precondition rather than a diagnosis. §14.7 shows the mechanism at work.

Second, the objection is not specific to proceduralism. Any normative framework can be captured by the powerful. The question is whether a framework has internal resources against capture. The framework's claim is that T5-as-precondition is such a resource: it does not prevent capture, but it makes the capture visible, and it downgrades the legitimacy of a captured process.

Third, the framework's modesty is not an admission of failure. A framework that claimed to solve the problem of power would be either naive or dangerous. The framework claims to handle it.

What this does not do. It does not answer the objection fully. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. What the framework can do is make the capture visible, and visible capture is the precondition of contestation. Whether visibility is enough is a political question, not a philosophical one. The objection is the second-hardest the framework faces, after the realist objection in objection 1.

2.5 Traditions that start elsewhere

Ubuntu-inspired moral theory (Metz 2007), Confucian role ethics (Ames 2011) and many Indigenous relational ontologies (Watts 2013; de la Cadena 2015; Kimmerer 2013) begin from relational personhood and obligations to kin, community and land. They tend to arrive at related demands on those who hold power over dependents, by different grounds. The framework treats them as additional considerations pointing toward its operative core — not as independent proofs.

2.6 Costs

J is thin, so it underdetermines outcomes. The framework presupposes a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected; it is therefore not neutral among all comprehensive doctrines. Its aim is breadth of acceptability rather than neutrality. And because the ground is an abductive argument rather than a proof, it is open to the objection that the abduction is weak. The reply is the modular design of §15.5: the ground can be replaced without discarding the rest, provided the replacement does the same work.

2.7 The framework's normative location, stated openly

A framework that claims breadth while relying on a specific tradition is not honest about what it is.

The framework is internal to a justificatory tradition. It takes for granted that those subject to arrangements have standing to demand reasons for them. It does not attempt to argue someone into that position from outside. A reader who rejects the justificatory tradition altogether will not be persuaded by anything in this essay.

Which tradition, exactly. The justificatory tradition includes a thick version (Habermas, Apel, the fuller Forst) and a thin version. The thick version requires actual rational consensus, mutual acceptability under idealised conditions, and a specific theory of communicative rationality. The thin version requires only that affected parties have some moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful. The framework needs only the thin version.

What the framework actually requires. Three claims, of decreasing thickness:

  1. Thin standing. Affected parties have some moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful. Widely shared.
  2. Semi-thick procedural claim. Arrangements that impose significant costs on affected parties must be justified to them, not merely to a third party. Justificatory-tradition-specific, but not uniquely so.
  3. Framework-specific claim. Justification can take the form of meaningful exit, independent review, or contestation — disjunctively. Specific to this framework.

What this means for the abductive argument (§2.3). The abductive argument is not an argument for the thin standing claim or for the semi-thick procedural claim. Those are stated as premises. The abductive argument is an argument for J given those premises.

  • The framework is internal to a justificatory tradition, but only to its thin version.
  • Three claims, of decreasing thickness: thin standing, semi-thick procedural claim, framework-specific disjunction.
  • The abductive argument is for J given the premises, not for the premises themselves.

2.8 Adversarial tests: what Pettit, Forst and Jonas would object

Section 1.5 and Table 1 show what the framework adds to the four traditions it engages. §2.7 shows its normative location within one of them. Neither shows what those traditions would say against it.

Pettit: the epistemic framing undercuts the republican point

The objection. The framework's epistemic framing of J (§2.1) undercuts the republican point. Pettit is not primarily an epistemologist: his claim is that domination is a political fact, not an epistemic one. If J is "primarily epistemic," then it is unclear what normative force it has.

The reply, in two parts. First, the epistemic framing is an entry point, not a foundation. Because domination is invisible to the actor without contestation, contestation is a necessary safeguard. This is a republican claim in epistemic clothing. Second, the framework does not replace the republican claim with the epistemic one. It supplements it.

Forst: the thin/thick distinction is unstable

The objection. Forst's right to justification is already thin: it is the right to demand and receive reasons. If Forst already has the thin version, what does the framework add?

The reply. The framework adds allocation. Forst says that the affected party has a right to justification; the framework says who must give it, in what form, by when, and with what safeguards. A right to justification without an allocation is a right that the powerful can meet by publishing a statement and calling it a justification.

Jonas: the framework is too near-sighted

The objection. Jonas is concerned with the species and with the future; the framework is concerned with present affected parties.

The reply. The framework is a special case of Jonas's ethics, applied to contemporary structural influence, and it differs from Jonas in one specific respect: it does not require Jonas's metaphysical grounding. What the framework inherits from Jonas is the capacity-based structure of responsibility and the forward-looking orientation, which §12.5 applies to future generations. What it does not inherit is Jonas's claim that responsibility is grounded in the ontological structure of life.

  • Positioning ("what we add") is not engagement ("what they would object").
  • Pettit would say the epistemic framing undercuts the republican point. The framework replies: the epistemic framing is an entry point, not a foundation.
  • Forst would say the thin/thick distinction is unstable. The framework replies: the addition is allocation, not thickness.
  • Jonas would say the framework is near-sighted. The framework replies: it is a special case of Jonas's ethics without Jonas's metaphysics.
  • None of the replies is decisive; each states what would count as its failure.

3.The Exercised-Capacity Principle

Influence triggers the duty, stakes and contestation scale it, and capacity allocates it.

An actor that exercises or maintains discretionary influence over the conditions of others' lives owes them an account of it when exit and review are unavailable or ineffective. How much it owes scales with the stakes and with the affected parties' difficulty in contesting the influence (§7). The burden of establishing the mechanisms through which the account is given falls on actors in proportion to their capacity to establish them (§8.2).

Trigger: exercised or maintained influence, and the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. This separates the principle from the demanding duty of rescue (Singer 1972) and from the overdemandingness that critics press against capacity-based views (Murphy 2000).

Scale: stakes and contestation deficit.

Allocation: capacity to discharge. A front-line worker can explain her decisions. She cannot establish an independent review board.

3.1 Why not the rivals

Causal responsibility is necessary for attribution but insufficient for structural influence (Thompson 1980). Fault-based responsibility concerns blame; answerability does not presuppose fault (Shoemaker 2011). Relational responsibility is real, and the framework retains it, but relational duties alone say nothing about anonymous influence. Consent is valuable where meaningful, but under asymmetry it can be manufactured or coerced. The social connection model (Young 2011) is the closest rival, and the framework is largely compatible with it; it adds a discretionary trigger and a specification of levels. §3.4 specifies how ECP handles distributed influence, delegated discretion, and the five modes of contribution (causing, enabling, controlling, foreseeing, benefiting). It is the operational complement to what §3.1 states in summary form: causal responsibility is necessary for attribution, but insufficient for structural influence.

3.2 The cost: answerability handles power; it does not solve it

A principle that makes the powerful responsible risks being used by them as a badge of legitimacy. The framework does not claim to eliminate this risk. Three constraints apply: responsibility is not authority (§8, §16), review must meet independence conditions (§11), and the risk is tested directly in §15.

Handling, not solving. The framework's aim is not to solve the problem of power — no framework can. Its aim is to convert the problem from an unstructured condition into a set of design constraints that can be observed, contested and revised.

What the framework can do is make capture visible and costly. §11.4 specifies the mechanism: Level 3 authorisation requires publication of R1 indicators before authorisation, and an actor that refuses the publication cannot claim Level 3 legitimacy.

3.3 The exercised-capacity principle alongside the social connection model: an illustrative case

A general claim about allocation is worth little without a case in which it does work that the closest rival cannot.

The case. A recommendation algorithm on a large platform amplifies content that increases political polarisation. Who bears the duty to answer for it?

Young's answer. Everyone who participates in the structure that produces the harm shares forward-looking responsibility. The strength of this answer is its fidelity to how structural harm is actually produced. Its focus is on shared structural responsibility and collective processes; it does not use the same case-specific duty ladder developed here.

ECP's answer. The trigger is exercised discretionary influence, combined with the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. The users have no discretion over the ranking code, and no meaningful exit from the platform's network effects. The advertisers have discretion over whether to fund, not over how the ranking works. The engineers and executives have discretion over both, and the users cannot exit or review the ranking they produce. So the duty attaches, at Level 3, to the design team and the executives, and at Level 2 to the regulators who could have required audit but did not.

What ECP may add in this case. Young's model has been criticised for exactly this: if everyone is responsible, no one is. ECP keeps the structural insight and adds the allocation that the model lacks.

What ECP owes Young. The framework does not claim to have refuted Young. If Young's model can be given an allocation that does the same work without a discretionary trigger, the framework's advantage disappears.

  • Young's social connection model identifies shared responsibility; ECP proposes a more granular allocation for particular decisions.
  • ECP retains the structural insight and adds allocation by exercised discretion combined with the unavailability of exit and review.
  • The advantage is demonstrated in a concrete case, not asserted in general terms.
  • ECP does not claim to have refuted Young; it claims to have entered a contest it can lose.

3.4 Distributed influence and delegated discretion

A principle that only handles face-to-face influence is not a principle for the conditions most people actually live under. ECP must show how it allocates responsibility when influence is spread across a chain, a system, or an organisation.

Five modes of contribution. In distributed cases, the question is not only who exercised influence, but in what way each party contributed. Five modes need to be distinguished:

  • Causing. The party's action produces the effect directly.
  • Enabling. The party creates the conditions under which another party can produce the effect (funding, infrastructure, legal permission, data).
  • Controlling. The party retains the ability to stop, redirect, or authorise the effect, even if it does not itself act.
  • Foreseeing. The party knows or could reasonably know that the effect will occur.
  • Benefiting. The party gains from the effect without contributing to it.

How ECP allocates. ECP's trigger is exercised or maintained discretionary influence, and the five modes bear on that trigger in different ways. Causing and controlling are sufficient for the trigger. Enabling is sufficient only where the enabler retains the ability to withdraw the enabling condition at tolerable cost — the funder who can stop funding, the platform that can change its terms. Foreseeing is not sufficient on its own, but it raises the level (§7, severity and entrenchment). Benefiting is not sufficient on its own, but it generates a duty of inquiry: a party that benefits from an arrangement it did not create is not thereby answerable for it, but it is answerable for what it does once it knows.

The delegation question. A party cannot escape ECP by delegating the decision if it retains control over the conditions under which the decision is made. Three conditions determine whether delegation succeeds or fails:

  1. The delegator retains the ability to revoke. If the delegator can withdraw the authority, change the terms, or remove the delegate at tolerable cost, the delegator retains exercised influence and bears the duty at the level the arrangement would otherwise require.
  2. The delegator sets the frame. If the delegator defines the objectives, the constraints, the success criteria, or the data on which the delegate acts, the delegator's influence is not diminished by the delegation. It is exercised through the frame.
  3. The delegation is not a genuine transfer of discretion. If the delegate has no real alternatives — if the delegate is executing a rule it had no part in making and cannot contest (§1.3, Discretion) — then the discretion remains with the delegator, whatever the formal structure says.

Where all three conditions are met, delegation is genuine, and ECP allocates to the delegate. Where any one fails, delegation is formal only, and ECP allocates to the delegator. §8.2's rule — that duties are allocated by capacity, not heaped on whoever is nearest — is the operational form of this.

A worked case: the supply chain. A garment brand sources from a factory that sources from a subcontractor that employs home workers. The home workers' conditions are shaped by the brand's pricing, delivery deadlines, and audit regime. Who bears the ECP duty?

  • The home workers exercise no discretionary influence over their conditions. No duty.
  • The subcontractor exercises discretion over hours and pay, but under constraints set by the factory. Level 1 duty; Level 2 if the factory's constraints are such that the subcontractor cannot refuse them at tolerable cost.
  • The factory exercises discretion over which subcontractor it uses and what terms it sets. Level 2 duty; Level 3 if the terms are so constrained by the brand that the factory cannot refuse.
  • The brand sets the frame: prices, deadlines, audit criteria. It retains the ability to revoke (change suppliers), to set the frame (change the terms), and to restore discretion to the factory (pay more, allow more time). By the three conditions above, delegation to the factory is formal only. Level 3 duty.

The case shows what ECP does in distributed settings: it follows the exercised influence through the delegation, and it allocates to whoever retains the ability to revoke, set the frame, or restore discretion. It does not dissolve responsibility into a collective, and it does not heap it on the most visible party. It locates it where the capacity actually is.

What this does not do. It does not solve the collective action problem. Where no party can revoke, set the frame, or restore discretion — where the arrangement is genuinely polycentric and no actor has the capacity the three conditions require — ECP does not generate a determinate allocation. The framework's response is not to invent one, but to say so: in such cases the duty is distributed in a way that no single party can discharge alone, and the appropriate response is collective (§4.6, §10), not individual. That is a limit, not a solution, and it is stated as one.

  • Distributed influence is handled through the five modes of contribution (§3.4): causing, enabling, controlling, foreseeing, benefiting. Enabling counts only where the enabler can withdraw at tolerable cost; foreseeing raises the level but is not sufficient alone; benefiting generates a duty of inquiry, not a duty of answerability.
  • Delegation is genuine only if the delegator retains none of three capacities: the ability to revoke, to set the frame, or to restore discretion to the delegate. If any of the three remains, the delegation is formal only, and ECP allocates to the delegator (§3.4).
  • Where no party can revoke, set the frame, or restore discretion — the genuinely polycentric case — ECP does not generate a determinate allocation. The duty is distributed in a way that no single party can discharge alone, and the appropriate response is collective (§4.6, §10), not individual.

Part II

Who Is Owed an Account

4.Standing Under Uncertainty

Incomprehensibility is not evidence of unimportance, but neither is it evidence of importance.

4.1 Four tiers

  • Tier A: agents. Beings with normative agency (persons).
  • Tier B: welfare subjects. Beings with interests of their own: sentient animals, and humans who lack agency.
  • Tier C: uncertain candidates. Entities for which there is a non-negligible, evidence-based probability of Tier B status (Birch 2024).
  • Tier D: non-subjects. Entities with no credible interests of their own: stones, rivers and ecosystems as such. The framework claims no direct standing for them.

4.2 The plausibility threshold

The test for Tier C is whether there is a credible, evidence-based reason to think the entity has states that matter to it. Without such a threshold, precaution generates reasons everywhere and guides nowhere (Sunstein 2005).

4.3 The stone, restated

The stone marks the edge of the framework's claims. For beings that meet the threshold, our failure to understand their way of being is no reason to dismiss their interests. For the stone, which does not meet it, the framework claims no standing and says so.

4.4 Artificial systems

Answerability for the influence of AI systems falls on the humans and organisations that design, deploy and maintain them. Whether some artificial systems may themselves be Tier C candidates is contested, and the framework does not answer it.

4.5 Perspectival plurality

For beings in Tiers A–C, their way of experiencing the world may be difficult or impossible for us to reach. That a perspective is inaccessible is not evidence that it is absent (Fricker 2007).

4.6 Representation

Some affected parties cannot take part directly: future generations, infants, animals, communities that lack institutional voice. Representation is legitimate only if (i) representatives are chosen through a process that can itself be contested, (ii) they state the basis on which they claim to know the interests concerned, (iii) where interests are contested there is more than one representative or perspective, (iv) they can be removed, (v) they are neither the actor nor dependent on the actor for appointment or funding, and (vi) they represent uncertainty rather than substituting invented certainty.

(vii) Collective contestation must not undermine individual contestation. Where a collective procedure is used to represent affected parties, the procedure must preserve the ability of individuals within the collective to contest the collective's decisions, and to seek independent review of them where the collective's own procedures fail.

  • Four tiers of standing: agents, welfare subjects, uncertain candidates, non-subjects.
  • Tier C requires a plausibility threshold.
  • Representation of silent parties is legitimate only under six conditions.
  • Collective contestation must not undermine individual contestation.

5.Interdependence, Difference and Non-Domination

Existence is relational. Symbiosis is the floor that relational existence can fail to meet.

Living beings, institutions, technologies and ecosystems exist through relations of dependence and influence. Some of these relations are reciprocal and enabling, others unequal, extractive or harmful. The word "symbiosis" is used here with care. In biology it covers mutualism but also parasitism, so it cannot by itself carry a normative meaning. In this essay it names a floor for relations of dependence: the minimum conditions under which dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§10).

Two senses, one word — and the framework keeps them apart. From here on, "symbiosis" in this essay is used in a single, normative sense: the minimum conditions under which a relation of dependence is not exploitative, not assimilating, and does not foreclose the dependent party's continued existence, development, or participation. The descriptive sense — that all beings exist through relations of dependence and influence — is stated once here (§5), and then dropped from the vocabulary. The framework does not claim that every relation of dependence is symbiotic in the normative sense. Parasitism, extraction, and domination are as much a part of the fabric of existence as mutualism is; the framework's claim is that they are not acceptable, not that they do not occur. Where the text says "symbiosis" from here on, it means the normative floor, never the descriptive fact.

Answerability (§3) is the procedure by which this floor is pursued. §5.2 argues that under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability is not just the road to symbiosis, but the heightened epistemic safeguard for knowing whether symbiosis has been achieved.

Harmony enforced by silencing, homogenising or sacrificing the vulnerable is not symbiosis. Difference is not a defect to be corrected. The asymmetry also runs both ways: the more capable may misunderstand the less capable, and the less capable cannot audit the more capable.

5.1 Making symbiosis measurable (or admitting it is not)

If the framework states defeaters for its ground and failure conditions for its activation rule, its own ideal cannot be exempt from assessment.

Symbiosis was defined above as the normative floor for relations of dependence — not as the descriptive fact that relations exist, but as the minimum conditions under which a relation of dependence is acceptable.

Three indicators, specified conceptually apart from answerability. Their measurement may nevertheless depend on testimony, representation, and contestable evidence.

  • Non-exploitation (SY1). The dependent party's continued participation does not require it to accept terms that it would reject if it had exit at tolerable cost. Measured by: the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, adjusted for exit costs. A high gap indicates exploitation; a low gap indicates that the relation is not extractive, whatever else it is. Note that the indicator is defined by the dependent party's stated preferences, not by the more powerful party's assessment of what those preferences should be. This gives SY1 a referent distinct from the procedure of answerability. It does not make the evidence self-authenticating: stated preferences may be constrained, adaptive, strategic or difficult to elicit. Section 15.2b specifies how SY1's falsification condition breaks the heads-I-win problem, and why the structural indicators (iii) and (iv) carry more confirmatory weight. Where the dependent party cannot state preferences — infants, animals, non-communicating persons, future generations — SY1 is not assessed by the absence of stated preferences. It is assessed through the representational substitutes in §4.6 and the structural indicators in §15.2b, and the absence of expressed preference is not evidence of non-exploitation (see §15.2b, A3).
  • Non-assimilation (SY2). The dependent party's difference is not treated as a defect to be corrected. Measured by: whether the actor's interventions aim at removing the difference or at removing the burden the difference carries. A programme that seeks to make deaf children hearing is assimilation; a programme that provides sign language and accessible environments is not. The indicator is observable in the design of interventions, not in the intentions of actors.
  • Continued possibility (SY3). The dependent party retains the conditions for continued existence, development and participation. Measured by: whether F1–F5 (§10) hold over time, not just at a single decision. A relation that satisfies F1–F5 at one moment and violates them at the next is not symbiotic.

What this does, and what it does not do. It does not make symbiosis a score. It makes it contestable in the same way the activation rule is: with anchors, counter-profiles, independent raters. If a relation scores low on SY1–SY3 across repeated assessments, the framework says it is not symbiotic. If a relation scores high, the framework does not say it is symbiotic; it says the observable indicators are consistent with it.

Why this matters for the framework's integrity. The framework requires actors to answer for their influence. It would be incoherent for the framework's own ideal to be exempt from that requirement.

Are SY1–SY3 independent of each other? Not fully. The question is whether they are independent enough to do work that a single indicator could not. Three pieces of evidence suggest they are:

  • They come apart in actual cases. A workfare programme can satisfy SY1 while violating SY2. A parent can satisfy SY1 and SY2 while violating SY3.
  • They have different measurement procedures. SY1 requires elicitation of stated preferences. SY2 requires classification of intervention design. SY3 requires longitudinal observation of F1–F5.
  • They have different defeaters. SY1 is defeated if stated preferences are found to be adaptive. SY2 is defeated if design classification is shown to be a function of the actor's self-description. SY3 is defeated if F1–F5 are shown to hold trivially.

What this shows: SY1–SY3 are not fully independent, but they are independent enough that no single one can substitute for the other two.

The circularity problem in SY1. SY1 compares stated preferences with accepted terms. If stated preferences are elicited in a context controlled by the actor, the preferences may be shaped by the relation under scrutiny. The framework's response is to require that elicitation occur in a context outside the relation: by a party with no stake in the outcome, using questions the dependent party can decline to answer, and at a time when the party has a real alternative to return to. The requirement is stated in the T5-precondition (§11.4).

SY1's limits as an indicator. SY1 is a necessary but not a sufficient indicator of non-exploitation. It compares two facts — stated preferences and accepted terms — but if the preferences are adaptive, the comparison can converge for the wrong reason. The framework's response is to weight structural indicators (dissent channels, counterfactual improvement) more heavily than SY1 where they diverge (§15.2b). How the framework weighs stated preferences against these indicators, and how it avoids the paternalism risk, is set out in §5.2 ("How adaptive preferences are weighed").

What would refute the independence claim. If a critic can show that SY1, SY2 and SY3 are so entangled that no case satisfies one without satisfying all three, or violates one without violating all three, the framework's claim that three indicators catch what one cannot is false. That would be a serious defeat, and the framework should acknowledge it as one.

  • Existence is relational.
  • Symbiosis is a floor, specified through three observable indicators: non-exploitation (SY1), non-assimilation (SY2), continued possibility (SY3).
  • SY1–SY3 are not fully independent, but they are independent enough: they come apart in actual cases, have different measurement procedures, and have different defeaters.
  • SY1's circularity problem is handled by the requirement that preferences be elicited outside the relation (T5-precondition), and SY1 is weighted below the structural indicators (iii)/(iv) in §15.2b.
  • Where the dependent party cannot state preferences, SY1 is assessed through representation (§4.6) and structural indicators (§15.2b); the absence of expressed preference is not evidence of non-exploitation.

5.2 The epistemic burden of asymmetric influence

Power does not make knowledge impossible. It changes what counts as adequate evidence.

Where one party controls the terms of dependence, it may also control the categories, information and measures through which the relation is evaluated. The more powerful party can therefore mistake the absence of visible objection for consent, compliance for flourishing, or institutional approval for legitimacy.

This is not a claim that powerful actors are incapable of understanding others, or that every judgment they make is distorted. Nor does it imply that the less powerful party is always correct. The claim is narrower: asymmetric influence creates a heightened risk of epistemic self-confirmation.

Where an actor exercises substantial discretionary influence over parties who lack effective means of exit or contestation, the actor bears a heightened burden to support claims about the effects and legitimacy of that influence with evidence that is not controlled solely by the actor, and with meaningful opportunities for affected parties or their legitimate representatives to correct the account.

This is a rebuttable burden of inquiry and justification. It is not a claim that answerability is the only route to knowledge, that affected parties are infallible, or that procedure is sufficient for justice.

Argument

1. The object of assessment is partly first-personal. Whether a relation is exploitative, assimilating or agency-preserving cannot always be determined from aggregate outcomes alone. The experiences and judgments of affected parties are therefore relevant evidence, though not the only evidence.

2. Asymmetry affects the production and interpretation of evidence. The more powerful party may set the available options, define success, control records, determine which complaints are legible, and select the time horizon over which outcomes are measured. These mechanisms can produce systematic blind spots without requiring malice.

3. Self-assessment is therefore insufficient as a default. When the actor controls both the influence and the evidential frame used to evaluate it, its conclusion is not independent confirmation. As stakes and contestation deficits rise, the evidential burden should shift toward independent audit, transparent reasons, access to records, protected channels for dissent, and attention to the testimony of affected parties.

4. The appropriate response is plural correction, not a single privileged voice. Affected parties should be able to correct descriptions of their own interests and experiences. Independent reviewers can test factual claims and identify conflicts within groups. Representatives may be necessary where direct participation is impossible, but their authority must itself be contestable.

5. This yields a practical principle of answerability. The greater the discretionary influence, severity, entrenchment and deficit of contestation, the less acceptable it is for the actor to rely on its own assessment alone. The duty ladder in §8 translates this burden into graduated requirements.

What follows — and what does not

The argument supports a presumption against epistemic closure: where power is asymmetric and affected parties cannot effectively contest, the powerful party must not treat its own view as conclusive. This is not a new principle. It is B (§2.0) applied to the specific question of what the actor owes when it controls both the influence and the evidential frame. The epistemic burden principle is B made operational.

The framework's claim is comparative and normative. In conditions of asymmetric influence, a process that includes independent scrutiny and protected correction is generally better placed to detect errors than a process that leaves the actor as the sole author and judge of the evidence.

How adaptive preferences are weighed — and the paternalism risk

The problem. A party may report satisfaction because it has adapted its preferences to a constrained situation. It may not know, or may not be able to articulate, that its preferences would be different under other conditions. SY1 takes stated preferences seriously; but taken alone, it cannot distinguish genuine acceptance from adapted acceptance. §15.2b treats SY1 as the weakest of the four confirmation sources for exactly this reason.

How the framework weighs. When SY1 and the structural indicators diverge, the framework does not resolve the divergence by preferring one over the other wholesale. It weighs them across four dimensions:

  1. Consistency across time. Do the party's stated preferences match what it has said or done over time, or has the reported satisfaction appeared suddenly alongside a change in the relation? A sudden convergence is weaker evidence than a stable one.
  2. Consistency across contexts. Does the party express the same preferences in low-stakes contexts where the relation is not at issue? If it does, the stated preferences are more probative. If the party's expressed preferences differ sharply between the relation under scrutiny and comparable situations, the relation-specific expressions are weaker evidence.
  3. Availability of the vocabulary for objection. If dissent channels exist and have been used (§15.2b, source iii), stated acceptance is more probative than if no such vocabulary exists. The absence of objection is not consent (A3); but the availability of objection makes the absence more informative.
  4. Counterfactual improvement. If exit is made available at tolerable cost and the party's reported satisfaction does not change, the satisfaction is more probative. If reported satisfaction changes when exit becomes available, the earlier satisfaction was partly a function of the constraint (§15.2b, source iv).

None of these four is decisive on its own. Together they form the framework's answer to the question "how much weight should be given to stated preferences?": as much as the evidence about their formation supports, and no more.

The paternalism risk, named explicitly. The framework's position on adaptive preferences carries an obvious risk: that a more powerful party will use the possibility of adaptive preferences to override what the affected party actually says, on the grounds that the party "doesn't really know what it wants." The framework does not license this. Three constraints prevent it:

  • The party's stated preferences remain the default. Where the structural indicators do not diverge from what the party says, what the party says is what counts. Adaptive preferences are a reason to look for further evidence, not a reason to substitute the actor's own view for the party's.
  • The framework does not authorize an outsider to speak for the party. Where the party's preferences appear adapted, the framework's response is to look for structural evidence (dissent channels, counterfactual improvement, consistency across contexts), not to allow a representative or an actor to declare what the party "really" wants. Representation (§4.6) has its own constraints, including that where the represented can express preferences, those take precedence over the representative's view.
  • The framework treats the party's own account as evidence, not as noise. The framework's response to adaptive preferences is not to dismiss what the party says; it is to ask what else is going on. Where there is no further evidence, the framework defaults to the party's stated preferences, not to the more powerful party's assessment of the party's good.

What this does not solve. The framework cannot, in general, distinguish a party that has genuinely accepted its conditions from a party whose capacity to reject them has been dismantled (§15.2, Limit 1). What it can do is bias the analysis toward the party's stated preferences as the default, add evidence where it is available, and refuse to let the more powerful party substitute its own view for the party's. That is not a solution to the adaptive preferences problem. It is the strongest response the framework can offer without itself becoming paternalist.

Relation to existing traditions

Pettit. Republican freedom as non-domination already makes contestability central. This framework does not claim that Pettit lacks a positive ideal or that it has discovered contestation. It attempts to connect non-domination to a graded account of answerability for discretionary influence, including cases where dependence is not reducible to state interference.

Forst. The right to justification already insists that those subject to norms must be able to demand and assess reasons. This framework does not supply the missing justification principle. It asks how the burden of producing evidence, preserving records and funding review should be allocated when the parties' capacities are unequal.

Young. The social connection model already explains responsibility for structural processes without reducing it to individual fault. ECP's proposed addition is a more explicit operational trigger and duty ladder.

Care ethics. Care theorists have developed sophisticated accounts of attentiveness, responsibility, competence and responsiveness. The framework's contribution is not to claim that care ethics has no procedure, but to insist that responsiveness must include channels through which the cared-for can challenge the carer's interpretation, alongside independent safeguards where direct voice is unavailable.

Defeaters and tests
  • B1 — No systematic epistemic risk. If evidence shows that asymmetry does not predictably affect what powerful actors notice, record or credit, the heightened-burden rationale is weakened.
  • B2 — Better alternatives. If another procedure provides equally reliable correction at lower cost and with less risk of capture or exclusion, it should replace the proposed safeguards.
  • B3 — Participation can worsen knowledge. If affected-party participation predictably entrenches misinformation, coercion, elite capture or internal silencing, the framework must specify countermeasures rather than romanticise participation.
  • B4 — Independent review is not independent. If reviewers reproduce the same interests, assumptions or exclusions as the actor, formal review does not discharge the burden.
  • B5 — Disproportionate burden. If the costs of the required process exceed its expected protective value in low-stakes cases, the activation thresholds and duty levels must be revised.

5.3 Why this symbiosis, and not another

SY1–SY3 are not self-evidently the right indicators of a good relation. This section defends their selection by deriving them from the thin standing claim, and by comparing them to the main rival floors.

The objection. §5.1 specifies symbiosis through SY1–SY3. But why these three? Why not welfare, or capability, or autonomy, or something else?

What kind of ideal symbiosis is. Symbiosis, in the normative sense the framework uses, is a floor, not a complete theory of the good relation. It specifies the minimum conditions that a relation must meet to count as non-exploitative, non-assimilating, and life-preserving. It does not say that a relation meeting SY1–SY3 is therefore good. A relation can meet all three and still be cold, indifferent, or unjust in other respects.

What the framework deliberately leaves open. Symbiosis is a floor, and a floor is not a home. A relation can meet every one of SY1–SY3 and F1–F5 and still be cold, indifferent, hollow, or unjust in ways the framework does not measure. This is not a failure of the framework; it is what it means for a floor to be a floor. The framework does not claim to specify:

  • Care in any thick sense — attention, responsiveness, warmth, the orientation of one party toward another's good beyond the minimum of non-extraction.
  • Trust — the confidence that the other party will act well when it is not being watched.
  • Meaning — the sense that the relation contributes to something the parties value beyond their own continuation.
  • Community — the forms of belonging, recognition, and mutual constitution that make a relation more than an arrangement.
  • Mutual development — the parties' growth through the relation, not merely the preservation of their capacity to grow.
  • Positive ideals of the good relation — friendship, love, solidarity, joint projects, shared ends.

Where these ideals are specified, the framework points to the traditions it engages: the capability approach (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006) for a substantive account of what people can do and be; care ethics (Tronto 1993; Held 2006) for an account of attention and responsiveness; the relational traditions (§2.5) for accounts of community and mutual constitution. The framework's contribution is the floor, not the house.

Deriving SY1–SY3 from thin standing

The premise. Affected parties have standing to demand reasons for arrangements that shape their conditions (§2.7, claim 1 and claim 2).

The question. Under what conditions is this standing non-vacuous? A standing claim that can be formally acknowledged while being substantively ignored is not a standing claim; it is a courtesy. SY1–SY3 specify the three distinct ways standing can be made vacuous, and therefore the three minimum conditions for it not to be.

  • SY1 — Non-exploitation. If affected parties can demand reasons but are then required to accept terms they would reject if they had exit at tolerable cost, their demand for reasons is hollow. Their standing is acknowledged formally and denied substantively. Therefore, for standing to be non-vacuous, SY1 must hold.
  • SY2 — Non-assimilation. If affected parties can demand reasons but their difference is treated as a defect to be corrected, their standing is absorbed into assimilation. Therefore, for standing to be non-vacuous, SY2 must hold.
  • SY3 — Continued possibility. If affected parties can demand reasons but the conditions of their continued existence, development, or participation do not hold, they have no power to do anything with the reasons they receive. Therefore, for standing to be non-vacuous, SY3 must hold.

What the derivation shows, and what it does not. The derivation shows that SY1–SY3 are the three distinct ways standing can fail. It does not show that SY1–SY3 are sufficient for a good relation.

What kind of floor symbiosis is. Symbiosis is a pro tanto floor: it binds those who accept the framework's premises, and it specifies what those premises require in practice.

Compared to rival floors

Welfare. A benevolent despot with perfect welfare provision can still fail SY1 (if the terms are exploitative), SY2 (if difference is treated as defect), and SY3 (if development and participation are foreclosed).

Capability. A capability theorist (Sen, Nussbaum) proposes a richer floor. SY1–SY3 are thinner — they specify minimum conditions rather than a substantive list. They can be read as minimum conditions that any capability account would require.

Autonomy. Autonomy accounts often struggle with cases of legitimate dependence — children, disability, care, illness — where the ideal is not independence but standing-preserving dependence. SY1–SY3 handle these cases directly.

What would refute the choice of floor. If a critic can show that a relation clearly failing SY1–SY3 is nonetheless good, or that a relation clearly meeting SY1–SY3 is nonetheless bad in a way the framework cannot detect, the choice of floor should be revised.

Six counterexamples, and which principle catches each

A principle that cannot be tested against concrete cases is a slogan. The six cases below are the strongest counterexamples the framework has been able to construct. For each, the question is: does any of SY1–SY3 catch it, or does the case fall outside the floor?

  • 1. The cold but non-exploitative order. A relation in which the dependent party is not exploited (SY1 holds), not assimilated (SY2 holds), and retains the conditions for continued existence and development (SY3 holds) — but the relation is emotionally empty, indifferent, and offers no warmth or recognition beyond the formal minimum. What catches it: nothing in SY1–SY3. The framework says so explicitly: symbiosis is a floor, not a complete ideal. The case shows what the floor does not measure, not a failure of the floor.
  • 2. The order that preserves existence but blocks development. A relation in which the dependent party's continued existence is secured but the conditions for learning, adaptation, or growth are foreclosed. What catches it: SY3, via F3 (development). If F3 is satisfied formally but not substantively, SY3 fails in the same way J fails when its qualifiers are met only formally (§2.1). The case is a test of whether F3 is read formally or substantively — and the framework should say that it must be read substantively.
  • 3. The order that preserves future possibilities but controls almost every present choice. A relation in which the dependent party retains a wide range of future options (F4 holds) but at each moment has almost no choice over what happens to it. What catches it: SY1 partially, if the controlled choices require accepting terms the party would reject at tolerable exit cost. But if exit is available (the party could leave, it just has nowhere better to go), SY1 may not catch it. This is the case that the framework most clearly does not fully catch. What it can say is that SY3's temporal dimension requires more than F4 — it requires F1 (exit or voice) as an ongoing condition, not just a formal one. But the framework should acknowledge that the case is at the edge of what SY1–SY3 can detect.
  • 4. The order where preferences are adaptive. A relation in which the dependent party reports satisfaction, but the satisfaction is itself a product of the relation. What catches it: Limit 1 (§15.2), and §15.2b's structural indicators (iii) and (iv). SY1 alone does not catch it; SY1 weighted with structural indicators does. This is why SY1 is the weakest of the four confirmation sources (§15.2b).
  • 5. The order that protects an ecosystem but harms sentient individuals. A relation in which the integrity of an ecosystem is preserved at the cost of suffering among sentient beings within it. What catches it: nothing in SY1–SY3 directly. This is a conflict between tiers, handled by §12.1's individual comparison, not by the floor. The case shows that the floor is not a decision procedure for conflicts between affected parties.
  • 6. The order that is symbiotic for the collective but dominating for the individual. A relation in which the collective's conditions satisfy SY1–SY3 but an individual within the collective is systematically subordinated. What catches it: §4.6 (vii), which requires that collective contestation not undermine individual contestation. But this is a constraint on representation, not on SY1–SY3 directly. The framework should say that SY1–SY3 assessed at the collective level do not substitute for SY1–SY3 assessed at the individual level where the individual's standing is at stake.

What the six cases show. Three of the six (1, 5, 6) fall outside what SY1–SY3 catch, and the framework should say so explicitly: the floor is not a complete theory, not a decision procedure for conflicts, and not a substitute for individual assessment. Two (2, 4) are caught, but only if the principles are read substantively rather than formally. One (3) is at the edge of what SY1–SY3 can detect, and the framework should acknowledge it as a genuine limit rather than a resolved case.

What the framework's position becomes. The floor is not weakened by the six cases. It was always stated as a pro tanto minimum, not a sufficient condition. What the six cases add is specificity: they name where the floor stops, and they make it harder to read SY1–SY3 as if they were a complete account of a good relation. A reader who wants a complete theory should look to the capability approach, care ethics, or the substantive theories the framework engages (§2.4, §2.5). What the framework offers is a diagnostic for where procedure will pass while something valuable is still being lost (§15.3), and the six cases show what "something valuable" can be when the floor is satisfied.

  • SY1–SY3 are a pro tanto floor: binding on those who accept the thin standing claim.
  • They are derived from the thin standing claim: each specifies one way standing can be made vacuous.
  • SY1 catches hollow reasons; SY2 catches absorbed difference; SY3 catches foreclosed action.
  • They are not reducible to welfare, capability, or autonomy; they handle dependence better than autonomy accounts.
  • A relation failing SY1–SY3 is not good, whatever else is true; a relation meeting them is not therefore good.
  • The framework deliberately leaves open the positive ideals — care, trust, meaning, community, mutual development — that a complete theory of the good relation would specify. A relation can meet SY1–SY3 and F1–F5 and still be cold, hollow, or unjust in ways the framework does not measure. The floor is not the house.
  • Six counterexamples are tested against SY1–SY3. Three (cold order, ecosystem-vs-individuals, collective-vs-individual) fall outside what the floor catches, and the framework says so explicitly. Two (blocked development, adaptive preferences) are caught if the principles are read substantively. One (preserved futures but controlled present) is at the edge of what the floor can detect.

5.4 Care, respect, and the limits of understanding

This section argues that care and respect are internal to the framework's premises in a specific, conditional sense — conditional on F, not on thin standing alone.

Care and respect are not additions to the framework. They are what thin standing means when it is applied to a particular relation of dependence, and what fallibilism requires when the affected party's situation is not fully accessible to the actor. They are operationalised in SY1–SY3, and they are developed in §15.2b's apophatic constraint. The qualification is that A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint are conditional on F, not on thin standing alone; §15.1 (iii) records the asymmetry.

Step 1. Responsibility without care becomes control

An actor who gives an account of their influence but does not attend to whether the account responds to what is at stake for the other has given an account of the wrong thing. §5.1's SY1 (non-exploitation) is precisely this. Care, in the specific sense used here, is not sentiment; it is the orientation of the actor's attention toward the affected party's own good as the affected party reports it.

Step 2. Care without respect for the incomprehensible becomes paternalism

Care that believes it understands the other's interests better than the other does can be benign in intention and domineering in effect. §5.1's SY2 (non-assimilation) is the operational form of this danger. Respect, in the specific sense used here, is not politeness; it is the actor's acknowledgment that the affected party's good is not fully specified by the actor's own categories.

Step 3. Respect for the incomprehensible is internal to the framework — conditionally

Both F (§2.0) and thin standing (§2.7) entail something that is often treated as a separate moral attitude. Fallibilism says: no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. Thin standing says: affected parties have moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful. If both are accepted, they do not merely permit respect for what the actor does not understand; they require it. A reader who accepts F and thin standing cannot consistently reject A1–A4 or the apophatic constraint without rejecting F. But SY1–SY3 do not depend on F: they are derived in §5.3 from thin standing alone.

What this shows, and what it does not

What it shows: care and respect are not imports; they are consequences of premises the framework already holds. What it does not show: that a relation satisfying SY1–SY3 is therefore caring or respectful in any thicker sense.

What depends on F, and what does not. SY1 and SY2 operationalise thin standing independently of F. A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint are conditional on F. A reader who accepts thin standing but rejects F keeps the floor and loses the answer to the hardest question (§15.2b). The full dependency map is in §15.1 (Table 7). What §5.4 says here is a specific application of that map: SY1 and SY2 operationalise thin standing independently of F, while A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint are conditional on F.

Defeaters
  • C1 — Care and respect are genuinely external. If it can be shown that an actor who accepts F and thin standing and then acts without care or respect has not violated the framework, only disappointed its mood, then §5.4 should be revised to say so.
  • C2 — SY1–SY3 can be satisfied without care or respect. If a cold but well-administered system can meet SY1–SY3 without any orientation of attention or acknowledgment of the limits of understanding, then §5.4's claim that care and respect are operationalised in SY1–SY3 is too strong.
  • C3 — The terminology is equivocal. If the framework's use of "care" and "respect" is equivocal with the care-ethics tradition, and would be better replaced by terms that do not carry that tradition's baggage, then §5.4 should be rewritten in those terms.
  • C4 — Respect for the incomprehensible slides into a precautionary principle. If, applied to the stone or to non-subjects, respect for the incomprehensible gives reasons everywhere and guides nowhere (§4.2), then the framework must specify where the respect ends.

The operational form of C1–C4 is in §15.7 (Table 8).

  • Care is what thin standing means when applied to a particular relation: the other's good is not specified on their behalf.
  • Respect is what F requires when the other's situation is not fully accessible: room must be left for the other to be right about their own good in ways the actor cannot see.
  • SY1 operationalises care (no extraction); SY2 operationalises respect (no assimilation).
  • §15.2b generalises the boundary condition through the apophatic constraint and A1–A4.
  • Four defeaters (C1–C4) state when the claim should be weakened or redesigned.

7.The Activation Rule

Discretion opens the gate. Entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit set the level.

7.1 The rule

  • No discretion: the actor does not bear the duty for this influence. It shifts to whoever set the governing rule or could have. The actor still owes escalation (§8.2).
  • Discretion present: Level 1 always applies.
  • Level 2 applies if any one driver is High, or any two are Moderate.
  • Level 3 applies if S is High and at least one of E or C is High.

Severity is judged first at the individual level. For decisions about the design of a system that acts on many people, it is also judged at the aggregate level (§9.1).

7.2 Why these conditions

Each element can be derived from J (§2.1).

  • Discretion. Justification is owed for what an agent chooses.
  • Entrenchment. What makes mistaken or unjustified influence tolerable is the possibility of correction afterwards. Entrenchment removes that channel.
  • Severity. The strength of a complaint scales with the burden it describes (§12.1).
  • Contestation deficit. Where the affected can exit, object effectively and understand what is done to them, they supply part of the accountability themselves (Hirschman 1970).

7.3 What the rule is not

It is not a score and it applies no weights. It is not a verdict either. It yields a presumptive level that can be overridden only with stated reasons (§9.3).

  • Discretion is the gate; E, S and C set the level.
  • Each condition is derivable from J, not stipulated.
  • The rule is not a score and not a verdict.

8.The Duty Ladder and Who Bears It

Three levels, each containing the one below. Duties are allocated by capacity, not heaped on whoever is nearest.

8.1 The levels

Level 1: Explain. The actor can state, on request and in proportion to the matter, the purpose of the action, the interests considered, the alternatives considered and the known uncertainties.

Level 2: Review and respond. Level 1, plus: an accessible channel for objection; access to the relevant information; a reasoned response within a reasonable time; revision where warranted; a record of decisions and reasons; a reviewer other than the original decider where feasible; and evidence that review is not symbolic.

Level 3: Preserve, examine, authorise. Level 2, plus: preserved records and version history; independent examination meeting the conditions of §11; a written assessment of less harmful alternatives; authorisation by a body outside the actor for severe or irreversible influence; staged implementation, reversibility by design or a sunset clause; remedy for those harmed; and post-implementation review. Level 3 authorisation requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation (§11.4).

8.2 Allocation by capacity

The level attaches to the influence, that is, to the arrangement. Duties are then allocated.

Those with the capacity to establish mechanisms (institutions, system owners, funders, regulators) bear the duty to establish Level 2 and Level 3 mechanisms. Others along the chain owe their own Level 1 explanation, honest participation, escalation of concerns and non-obstruction. No scapegoating: a front-line actor is not answerable for structural deficits she cannot cure, though she remains answerable for her own discretion. The rule that duties are allocated by capacity is the operational form of §3.4's account of distributed influence. The party with the capacity to revoke, set the frame, or restore discretion to the delegate bears the duty, whether or not it signs the decision. A delegator that retains any of these three capacities has not genuinely delegated, and ECP allocates to it.

8.3 Not authority

A higher level means stronger constraints on power, not greater authority. Level 3 does not make the most powerful actor the arbiter of its own case, and it does not give the actor a right to determine the conditions of those it is answerable to. The distinction is between authority (the justified power to decide) and answerability (the duty to explain, submit to review, and remain open to correction). The framework's levels attach to answerability, not to authority. An actor at Level 3 is more constrained, not more entitled. The full distinction between the five terms is in §1.3 (Table 1a).

  • Three levels: L1 explain, L2 review and respond, L3 preserve, examine and authorise.
  • Level 3 requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation.
  • The level attaches to the arrangement; duties are allocated by capacity. No scapegoating.
  • Higher level means stronger constraints, not greater authority.

Part IV

Structure: Floor, Review, Power

10.The Floor: Minimum Conditions in Relationships of Dependence

Continued existence is not enough. A being can stay alive or operational while losing any say over its own circumstances.

  • F1 — Exit or voice. A meaningful opportunity to withdraw, refuse or communicate disagreement, where the party is capable of expressing it. The protection of F1 does not depend on the party's capacity to express refusal, and the absence of expressed refusal is not consent (see §15.2b, A3).
  • F2 — Contestation. Decisions are open to challenge regardless of the intelligence, authority or technical capability of the decider. Contestation may be collective, subject to §4.6 (vii).
  • F3 — Development. Opportunities for learning, adaptation and growth are preserved.
  • F4 — Non-foreclosure. The future possibilities of others are not unnecessarily closed.
  • F5 — Recognition. Interests and possible experiences are acknowledged rather than dismissed because they are hard to understand (Fricker 2007).

F1 and F2 are the mirror image of the contestation deficit in §7: where they fail, C rises. The Floor is a conjunctive requirement with J, not an alternative to it (§2.1).

F1–F5 are minimum conditions, not a complete account of a good relation. A relation can meet all five and still be cold, hollow, or unjust in ways the framework does not measure. The framework deliberately leaves open the positive ideals — care, trust, meaning, community, mutual development — that a complete theory of the good relation would specify. What it offers is the floor, not the house (§5.3).

  • Five minimum conditions (F1–F5): exit or voice, contestation, development, non-foreclosure, recognition.
  • The Floor applies independently of the activation rule, and is conjunctive with J.
  • Protection is not captivity.

11.No Sole Judge: Review, Power and the Regress

No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence.

11.1 The prohibition

An actor exercising substantial influence must remain answerable to forms of review that are meaningfully independent of its own interests and decisions.

This does not assume that the powerful are malicious. Actors are prone to self-serving judgment and are limited in what they can see (§2.3), and review that is not independent of the reviewed has little claim to legitimacy.

11.2 The regress

If every reviewer needs a reviewer, accountability looks like an endless hierarchy. The response has three parts.

  • Finality without infallibility. Working systems end the regress with decisions that are final for practical purposes but can be reopened on new evidence, on new standing, on demonstrated procedural failure, or at a sunset.
  • Contestation is not adjudication. The reviewed and the affected may challenge a reviewer's independence or competence before a differently constituted body.
  • Plurality and publicity instead of a top. Several reviewers with different standpoints, publishing their reasons, make decentralised scrutiny possible (cf. Ostrom 1990).

11.3 Design conditions for review

  • R1 — Four kinds of independence, not one. A reviewer that meets one of the following but not the others is not independent in the sense R1 requires:
    • R1a — Organizational independence. The reviewer is a distinct body from the actor, with its own decision-making authority and its own staff. A "review board" whose members are employees of the actor is not organizationally independent.
    • R1b — Financial independence. The reviewer's funding does not depend on the actor's continuing goodwill, and cannot be withdrawn in retaliation for an adverse finding. Endowment, statutory funding, or a funding formula fixed in advance all satisfy this; annual discretionary funding by the actor does not.
    • R1c — Informational independence. The reviewer has access to the information it needs without the actor's permission, and is not dependent on the actor for the framing of what counts as relevant information. A reviewer that can see only what the actor chooses to show is not informationally independent, whatever its formal status.
    • R1d — Publication independence. The reviewer can publish its findings without the actor's prior approval, and cannot be prevented from publishing by contractual, legal, or practical means. A reviewer that must submit its report to the actor before publication is not independent in this sense.
    Each of the four is necessary. None is sufficient on its own. A reviewer that fails any one of them fails R1, however strong the others are.
  • R2 Plurality.
  • R3 Publicity.
  • R4 Contestable reviewers.
  • R5 Effectiveness.
  • R6 Contestable rules.

Capture indicators: reviewers appointed or funded by the reviewed; a record of never dissenting; staff moving between reviewer and reviewed; reviewers denied access; chronic delay.

11.4 Power over the framework itself: from diagnosis to design

The framework treats the power regress not as a limit it cannot eliminate, but as a design problem it can bound.

Who defined the categories? Who selected the reviewers? Who can dismiss them? Whose interests are excluded even where formally represented? These four questions are asked in every application (§14, step 6).

The requirement. T5 must be a precondition, not a diagnosis. Level 3 authorisation (§8.1) requires, as a condition of validity, that the authorising body publishes its R1 indicators before authorisation. An authorisation issued by a body that cannot or will not publish these indicators is not a Level 3 authorisation. It is a Level 2 authorisation with a Level 3 label. §14.7 illustrates the mechanism in a concrete case.

Why this changes the framework's status. It converts the regress from a concession into a design constraint. An actor that wants Level 3 legitimacy must accept the conditions that make Level 3 legitimacy meaningful. If it refuses, the framework does not collapse; it downgrades the actor's claim.

What this does not do. It does not solve the problem of who appoints the appointer. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. What it can do is make the capture visible. This is the sense in which the framework handles domination rather than solves it — and this is the point at which the power objection (§2.4b) presses hardest.

The regress as a real limit, not a technicality. The framework's response to the regress is not to pretend it does not exist. §11.2's "finality without infallibility" answers what happens when reviewers disagree; it does not answer what happens when the reviewer is captured by the same power that captured the actor. The regress has three levels, and the framework's response differs at each:

  1. First-order capture: the actor captures its own reviewer. The T5-precondition (§11.4) addresses this. If the reviewer cannot publish R1 indicators, its authorisation is downgraded. §14.7 shows the mechanism in action. This is the level at which the framework can act.
  2. Second-order capture: the body that appoints the reviewer is itself captured. The T5-precondition does not help here, because the body that publishes R1 indicators may be the same body that appoints the captured reviewer. What the framework can do is require that R1 indicators be published and independently audited — which is a further requirement, not a solution. Where second-order capture is documented, the framework's response is to say so: the authorisation is not merely downgraded to Level 2; it is marked as "authorisation issued by a captured body", which is a different and weaker claim than Level 2.
  3. Third-order capture: no external body exists that can audit the appointer. This is the Limit 3 case in its institutional form (§15.2b). The framework does not have a solution at this level. What it can do is name it — the same way §15.2b names the epistemic version of Limit 3 — and require that any actor claiming Level 3 legitimacy in such a situation state explicitly that no external audit of its appointer exists. Whether that statement is accepted is a political question, not a philosophical one.

What this means for the framework's claim. The framework does not claim that its review mechanisms are immune to capture. It claims that they make capture visible at the first level, harder at the second, and nameable at the third. That is a real but limited contribution.

  • No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence.
  • The regress is treated as a design problem: finality without infallibility, contestation not adjudication, plurality and publicity instead of a top.
  • R1 is four kinds of independence, not one: organizational (R1a), financial (R1b), informational (R1c), and publication (R1d). A reviewer that fails any one fails R1, however strong the others are (§11.3).
  • The regress has three levels: first-order (the actor captures its reviewer), second-order (the appointer is captured), and third-order (no external auditor exists). The framework can act at the first level, name the second, and only name the third (§11.4).
  • T5 is a precondition of Level 3 authorisation.
  • The framework handles domination; it does not solve it.

12.Conflicts, Ecology and Future Generations

Decisions must be justified, not derived.

12.0 Decision ordering: floor, comparison, aggregation, representation

Conflicts between affected parties cannot be resolved by reference to "symbiosis" or "the whole". They need an ordering, and the framework provides one.

What the framework is ordered by. When the interests of affected parties conflict, the framework does not resolve the conflict by referring to a common good, a collective interest, or an ecosystemic whole. It orders the decision in five steps:

  1. The floor (F1–F5, §10) is not weighable. No party's F1–F5 protections can be traded against another party's non-floor interests. A decision that violates one party's floor to secure another party's convenience is not a decision the framework authorizes. The only exception is the emergency procedure of §12.2, which requires that the violation be temporary, recorded, sunset-bound, and reviewed afterwards.
  2. Within the floor, individual comparison (§12.1). For non-floor burdens, the method is to compare the burdens each alternative places on each affected party, party by party, and to favour the alternative that avoids the most serious individual burden. This is not aggregation; it is comparison.
  3. Aggregation only as tie-breaker (§12.1). Where individual comparison does not decide — where two alternatives impose burdens of comparable weight on different parties — comparable claims may be aggregated. Aggregation is a tie-breaker, not a primary method, and it cannot override the floor.
  4. Representation of absent parties (§4.6). Where affected parties cannot speak for themselves — future generations, infants, animals, ecosystems — their interests are represented under the six conditions of §4.6. Representatives may not substitute invented certainty for the absent party's actual interests, and their authority is itself contestable.
  5. Uncertainty and irreversibility shift the burden. Where the evidence is uncertain and the harm irreversible, the burden falls on the party proposing the action to show that the harm is not serious. This is not a precautionary principle in the strong sense (§4.2 sets the threshold); it is a default that operates only where the burdened party cannot bear the cost of being wrong.

What this does not authorize. It does not authorize any party to invoke "the whole", "the collective", or "the ecosystem" as a reason to sacrifice an individual's floor protections. §4.6 (vii) says that collective contestation must not undermine individual contestation; §12.0 (1) and (3) extend that principle to weighing. A collective or an ecosystem can generate claims; it cannot generate a claim that overrides an individual's floor. Where a decision appears to require such an override, the framework's response is to look for an alternative that does not, not to accept the override.

What this does not do. It does not resolve every conflict. Where two parties' floor protections are both at stake and cannot both be satisfied — a genuine tragic choice — the framework does not provide a decision procedure that will produce a uniquely correct answer. It provides a requirement: the decision must be made with public reasons, subject to review, and with the burdens recorded. The framework's claim is not that it solves tragedy; it is that it can name it, and that the naming matters.

12.1 Weighing conflicting interests

Neither majority interest nor individual preference automatically prevails. The method is individual comparison: compare the burdens that each alternative places on each affected party, party by party, and favour the alternative that avoids the most serious individual burden. Claims of comparable weight may be aggregated as a tie-breaker (Scanlon 1998). Among the acceptable alternatives, choose the one with minimum necessary harm.

12.2 Emergencies

Where an immediate, serious threat makes the full procedure impossible, an actor may act first, provided it acts only as far as necessary, records its reasons at the time, is bound by an automatic sunset, and submits to review afterwards. Emergencies create no standing exemptions.

12.3 Ecological integrity

Ecosystems cannot be assessed through the preferences of individual participants. Protecting them may require restricting activities, and the justification runs through dependent beings in Tiers A–C, through future generations, and through the non-instrumental values that people hold (§4.1).

Ecosystem integrity is not a free-standing value that can override individual floor protections. It is protected because dependent beings in Tiers A–C depend on it, because future generations will inherit it, and because people hold it non-instrumentally. Where an ecosystem's protection conflicts with an individual's floor protections, §12.0 (1) applies: the floor is not weighable.

12.4 No automatic hierarchy

Intelligence, productivity, population size and technical sophistication do not by themselves determine moral priority. Neither does collective size, ecosystemic scale, or future extension: no party's floor protections can be weighed against another party's aggregate interest, however large that aggregate. What determines moral priority is the nature and strength of the interests at stake (floor vs. non-floor; severe vs. minor) and, within those, the individual comparison of §12.1. The ordering is stated in §12.0.

12.5 Future generations

The duty is condition-regarding: to preserve the conditions under which future people can exercise agency and meet basic needs, and a reasonably wide space of valuable options, rather than to fix the choices they will make. Because it concerns conditions and not particular individuals, it does not depend on who will exist (cf. the non-identity problem, Parfit 1984). Option preservation is not absolute. It operates as a weight, strongest where actions are irreversible (cf. Jonas 1984).

Future generations are represented under the six conditions of §4.6. Their representatives are bound by the same limits as any other representative: they may not substitute invented certainty for the represented party's actual interests, they must state the basis on which they claim to know those interests, and their authority is itself contestable. A representative who claims to speak for future generations with unlimited authority has ceased to be a representative and become a new sovereign.

  • §12.0 states a five-step decision ordering: floor (not weighable) → individual comparison → aggregation only as tie-breaker → representation under §4.6 → uncertainty shifts the burden. No step in the ordering can be skipped, and no party can invoke "the whole" to override an individual's floor protections.
  • Individual comparison first; aggregation only as tie-breaker.
  • Emergencies create no standing exemptions: sunset, record, review.
  • Ecological integrity is grounded in dependent beings, not a free-standing value (§12.3). Where ecosystem protection conflicts with an individual's floor protections, the floor holds (§12.0 (1)).
  • Future generations: preserve conditions and options, not particular choices.
  • Representatives of absent parties — future generations, infants, animals, ecosystems — are bound by §4.6's six conditions and may not substitute invented certainty for the absent party's actual interests (§12.5).

13.Learning, Correction and Reversibility

No actor, institution or intelligence should assume its understanding is complete.

The duty to learn. Where capacity exists and consequences are significant, actors monitor outcomes, investigate unexpected results, listen to criticism and update.

Reversibility by design. Consequential decisions should be built to be revisable: staged implementation, monitoring, independent evaluation, defined conditions for reconsideration, and sunset clauses.

Self-application. The framework is itself an influence on those who adopt it and on those affected by their adoption. It is subject to its own principles.

  • The duty to learn is a duty where capacity exists and consequences are significant.
  • Reversibility by design reduces the risk that incomplete understanding becomes permanent.
  • The framework is subject to its own principles.

Part V

Application and Testing

14.Application: A Nine-Step Procedure and Worked Cases

The same procedure yields very different obligations for different cases. A framework that gave every case the same answer would be useless.

14.1 The procedure

  1. Identify the relationship. Who is affected, in which tier (§4), and what dependencies exist?
  2. Identify actors and discretion.
  3. Rate E, S and C against the anchors (§9.1), and F for attribution. Use two raters and record reasons.
  4. Apply the activation rule (§7) and run the sensitivity test.
  5. Check the Floor (F1–F5, §10).
  6. Analyse power (§11.4).
  7. Examine alternatives and unrepresented interests (§12.1).
  8. Allocate duties by capacity (§8.2) and set up review that meets R1–R6.
  9. Reassess on stated triggers, and invite counter-profiles.

14.1b What the procedure delivers

A procedure that promises to produce an answer to every case is not a procedure; it is a promise. The nine steps deliver four different things at four different points, and the framework should say which is which.

  • A presumptive level (§7, §9). Steps 3 and 4 produce a level — L1, L2, or L3 — that attaches to the arrangement. It is a presumption, overridable only with stated reasons (§9.3). Where two raters disagree by one step, the modal rating is recorded and the disagreement is noted. Where they disagree by two steps, the case is flagged as borderline (§9.2). The procedure does not produce a uniquely correct level where competent raters can disagree; it produces a stated level, the reasoning behind it, and the record of any disagreement.
  • A floor judgment (§10, §12.0). Step 5 produces a judgment about whether the Floor (F1–F5) is satisfied. This judgment is not a presumption. It is binary: either the Floor holds or it does not. If it does not, the procedure stops; no allocation follows. The Floor is not weighable (§12.0 (1)).
  • A power analysis (§11.4). Step 6 produces a judgment about whether the actor's claim to Level 3 legitimacy is valid. If the T5-precondition is not met, the claim is downgraded (as in §14.7). This is not a presumption; it is a determination about whether the actor's own claim meets its stated conditions.
  • A duty to investigate further, or no allocation at all. At certain steps, the procedure produces not an answer but a requirement to look further. The Floor check at step 5 may reveal that the information is insufficient to judge. Step 7 may reveal that unrepresented interests need representation before a decision can be made. Step 8 may reveal that no party has the capacity to discharge the allocated duty (§3.4). In these cases, the procedure's output is "investigate further" or "no determinate allocation" — not a level, not a verdict.

What the procedure does not deliver. It does not deliver a uniquely correct answer where two competent raters can reach different ratings, and it does not resolve genuine conflicts where two parties' floor protections are both at stake (§12.0). The control question for any application is: at which step is the disagreement? At step 3, the disagreement is factual or interpretive, and the procedure records it. At step 5, the disagreement is about whether the Floor holds, and the procedure stops. At step 7 or 8, the disagreement is normative or structural, and the procedure says so. The procedure's value is not that it eliminates disagreement; it is that it locates it.

14.2 Case: AI triage in intensive care

A hospital deploys an AI system to prioritise ICU admissions during a respiratory pandemic. Entrenchment is High, severity is High, and contestation deficit is High. Level 3.

What Level 3 requires: preserved model versions, descriptions of training data, individual scores and override records; an independent ethics board with lay and patient representation; a written comparison with clinician-only triage; authorisation from the public-health authority; a sunset clause; and a remedy for those wrongly denied.

Power analysis: the categories were set by the vendor and clinical leadership, and the ethics committee is appointed by the hospital board, which fails R1 unless changed. A hybrid in which the AI recommends and a human decides is the default preference because it keeps contestation alive.

The framework does not decide the case. It tells the hospital what it owes an answer to, and to whom.

14.2b Case: the emergency physician (a counterintuitive result)

Every other case in this essay produces the answer a careful reader would predict. This one does not.

A physician in an emergency department must decide, in under ninety seconds, whether to intubate a patient. The activation rule gives Level 3.

The counterintuitive result. Level 3 requires independent authorisation, preserved records and a written assessment of alternatives — before the act. A reader's first reaction is that this is absurd.

The reply. The Level 3 duty does not fall on the physician in the moment. It falls on the arrangement (§8.2): on the hospital, the protocols, the audit trail, the retrospective review, and the training. The physician's own duty is Level 1 in the moment and Level 2 afterwards.

Legitimate relocation or evasion? The relocation is legitimate if and only if all three conditions hold. (i) A concrete duty-bearer is named — not "the hospital" but "the chief medical officer, with the ethics committee, funded by X and appointed by Y". (ii) The mechanism exists before the moment of decision and can be pointed to. (iii) The relocation is not the same move that would be made in every case where the framework is inconvenient. If any of the three fails, the relocation is an evasion.

  • The emergency physician case appears to refute the framework.
  • The reply: the Level 3 duty falls on the arrangement, not on the physician in the moment.
  • Relocation is legitimate only if (i) a concrete duty-bearer is named, (ii) the mechanism exists before the decision, and (iii) the same move is not made in every inconvenient case.

14.3 Case: a platform's recommendation system

For individual recommendations: Level 2. For the design of the system: Level 3, with preserved version history, independent audit, published aggregate metrics, and a sunset for any optimisation that cannot show population-level reversibility.

14.4 Case: a community health worker

She has little institutional power. The rule says the arrangement requires Level 3. But she has no capacity to build independent review. Under §8.2, the duty to establish mechanisms falls on the programme, the ministry and the funders. Her personal duties are to explain her decisions, respond to objections within her role, and escalate when protocols fail.

14.5 Four short cases

  • One-off organism release. Level 3, although the act happens once.
  • Dating platform age penalty. Level 2.
  • Café menu change. Level 1 only, and arguably not even that.
  • Physician following a protocol. No discretion over the protocol's content; the duty to justify the content moves to its designers.

14.5b Case: emergency water rationing (duties conflict)

A water authority must impose rationing within 48 hours in a severe drought. Entrenchment and severity are High and contestation deficit is High, so the rule gives Level 3, whose prior independent authorisation takes weeks. §12.2 allows acting first. The duties then split.

  • Not compressible: published reasons, preserved records, a contestation channel open from day one, exemptions for life-critical dependence, and a sunset clause.
  • Compressible: prior authorisation, replaced by review completed before the sunset.
  • Not decided by the framework: which rationing rule is fair. Raters may disagree; under §9.2 the disagreement is recorded, not averaged away.

14.5c Case: the ambiguous severity rating

A municipal housing authority must decide whether to relocate residents from a building with documented but non-imminently-dangerous structural problems. Rater A rates Severity as Moderate (significant but remediable health risk); Rater B rates Severity as High (basic agency, because relocation disrupts community and support networks). Entrenchment is High, contestation deficit is Moderate. The level depends on Severity: Moderate → Level 2; High → Level 3.

What the procedure delivers. Not a determinate level. The disagreement between raters is recorded (§9.2), the case is flagged as borderline, and the level is stated with the disagreement noted. The procedure's output is: "L2 or L3, depending on whether Severity is rated Moderate or High; both ratings are defensible; the disagreement is preserved." What a decision-maker must do is either accept one rating with stated reasons (§9.3, reasoned override) or commit to the L3 requirements as a precaution because the L2 rating may be wrong.

What the case shows. The procedure does not resolve every ambiguity. Where two competent raters can disagree on a rating, the procedure produces a stated range and a requirement for reasoned choice.

14.5d Case: aggregate severity and the individual-comparison rule

A city must decide whether to restrict car traffic in a residential area to reduce air pollution. The restriction imposes a Moderate burden on commuters (more travel time) and delivers a Moderate benefit to residents (better air quality). For any given individual, the burden and benefit are comparable. But there are 200,000 commuters and 20,000 residents. Aggregate severity is High for residents; aggregate burden is High for commuters.

What the procedure delivers. The procedure's §12.0 ordering applies: (1) Floor check — no party's floor is at stake (Moderate burdens on both sides, no basic needs); (2) individual comparison — burdens and benefits are comparable for any given individual, so individual comparison does not decide; (3) aggregation as tie-breaker — aggregate severity for residents vs. aggregate burden for commuters. The procedure does not specify how to weigh 200,000 Moderate burdens against 20,000 High-severity benefits. That is an open normative choice, not a procedural one.

What the case shows. The procedure gives a floor-check and an ordering, but at step (3) it produces a demand for a normative decision, not a determinate answer.

14.5e Case: no party has the capacity to discharge the duty

A regional health authority learns that a widely used medical device has a small but non-trivial failure rate. The manufacturer is offshore and outside the authority's jurisdiction; the distributor is domestic but small; the hospitals using the device are public but resource-constrained. Under §3.4, none of the parties in the chain retains the ability to revoke, set the frame, or restore discretion to the others at tolerable cost.

What the procedure delivers. Not an allocation. §3.4 says that where no party can revoke, set the frame, or restore discretion, ECP does not generate a determinate allocation. The procedure's output is: "This is a genuinely polycentric case; no single party can discharge the Level 3 duty alone; the appropriate response is collective (§4.6, §10), not individual."

What the case shows. The procedure's honest output is sometimes "no determinate allocation." The framework names this as a limit (§3.4), not a solution.

14.7 Case: an AI company's internal audit (T5-precondition failing)

This case shows what happens when the framework's T5-precondition is not met: the actor does not lose the framework; the actor loses the claim to Level 3 legitimacy.

An AI company commissions an "independent" audit of its recommendation system, which has been shown to amplify polarising content. The audit is published with a foreword by the CEO, the reviewers are paid by the company, and the company's board appoints them. The audit concludes that the system is "broadly safe" and recommends "continued monitoring."

The framework's assessment. The company has conducted a review. But the review fails R1 (independent appointment, funding and removal) and would fail T5 as a precondition (§11.4). Under the framework, this means the review does not count as a Level 3 authorisation. It is a Level 2 authorisation with a Level 3 label.

What the framework tells the regulator. The regulator does not accept the audit as satisfying Level 3. The regulator asks for:

  • R1 indicators: who appointed the reviewers, who funds them, how they can be removed.
  • A second, genuinely independent review, funded by the regulator, not the company.
  • A published R1 statement, as a condition of any future Level 3 claim.

What the framework tells the company. The company does not lose the framework. It loses the claim to have met Level 3. It can still meet Level 2 by providing access to information and responding to objections. But it cannot claim the stronger legitimacy that comes with a genuinely independent review.

Why this case matters. It shows that the framework's response to the power objection (§2.4b) is not merely rhetorical. The T5-precondition converts an abstract concern ("reviews can be captured") into a concrete consequence ("this review is not a Level 3 authorisation").

What the case does not settle. It does not show that the publication requirement itself cannot be captured. A sufficiently powerful company could, in principle, arrange a genuine-seeming R1 statement without genuine independence. This is not a proof. It is a claim about the structure of visibility.

  • A captured review does not count as Level 3 authorisation.
  • The framework does not fail when a review is captured; it downgrades the actor's claim to legitimacy.
  • The regulator's response is specified: demand R1 indicators, fund an independent review, and require publication as a precondition.
  • The case does not settle whether publication itself can be captured.

14.7b Case: the captured regulator (second-order capture)

§14.7 shows the first-order case: a company captures its own auditor. This case shows the second-order case: the body that would audit the company is itself captured. It is the harder case, and the framework's response is correspondingly weaker.

A national data protection authority is asked to approve an AI company's recommendation system under the framework's Level 3 requirements. The authority's director was appointed by the same minister whose party received donations from the company's parent. The authority's budget is set annually by the ministry. The authority's technical staff have moved between the authority and the company over the past five years. The authority publishes its R1 indicators — but they reveal exactly this pattern.

What the framework's T5-precondition delivers. The precondition is met formally: the authority publishes its R1 indicators before authorisation. But the indicators themselves reveal that R1a (organizational independence) is partially met, R1b (financial independence) is not met (annual budget by the ministry), R1c (informational independence) is partially met (staff movement in both directions), and R1d (publication independence) is met formally but the authority's publications are widely understood to be constrained by the ministry's preferences.

What the framework says. The authorisation does not count as a Level 3 authorisation. The T5-precondition is satisfied in form but not in substance. The framework's response is not to treat the authority's authorisation as valid, but to mark it as "authorisation issued by a captured body" — which is weaker than Level 2, because Level 2 requires an actual reviewer, and a captured body is not a reviewer in the framework's sense (§11.3).

What the framework cannot do. It cannot tell the company what to do next. It can tell the company that its authorisation is not a Level 3 authorisation. It can tell the public that the authority is captured. It can tell a court, if one exists, that the authorisation should be set aside. But if the authority is captured by the same power that captured the company, and no court is available that is not itself captured, then the framework's response is exhausted. This is the third-order case (§11.4), and the framework does not solve it.

Why this case matters. §14.7 shows the framework's strongest move (T5-precondition in action). §14.7b shows the same move failing, and shows what the framework does when it fails. It does not collapse. It does not pretend. It names the failure, downgrades the claim, and says what would be required to restore it — and then acknowledges that what would be required may not be available.

  • A captured regulator does not issue a Level 3 authorisation, even if it publishes its R1 indicators.
  • The framework distinguishes between "authorisation issued by a captured body" and "Level 2 authorisation" — the former is weaker than the latter.
  • Where no external body exists that can audit the appointer, the framework names this as the third-order capture case (§11.4) and does not solve it.
  • §14.7 shows the framework's strongest move; §14.7b shows it failing. Both are honest statements of what the framework can and cannot do.

15.Limits, Testing and Replacement

This section states what the framework's ground does not rest on, the three structural limits the framework cannot close, and what follows for its status as a diagnostic. The consolidated list of caveats is in §15.6, and the operational form of each falsification condition is in §15.7.

15.1 The ground is not derived

The framework rests on three premises it does not derive. Saying so is not a failure. Saying otherwise is.

(i) B is a premise, not a theorem. §2.0 states B as a bridge norm. F (fallibilism) motivates B. F does not entail B. The only route from F to a duty would be a transcendental argument of the kind Habermas and Apel give — that the practice of making claims and asking for reasons already presupposes the standing of others to ask. That route is available. It is also thick: it commits the framework to a specific theory of communicative rationality. §2.7 says the framework does not require that theory. The framework does not use this route: its realism reply (§2.4) is a placement, not a refutation, and its argument for J is abductive, not transcendental. Therefore B is a premise.

(ii) Thin standing is a premise, not a theorem. §2.7 states thin standing as the framework's first normative commitment. It is not derived. It is chosen. A reader who rejects thin standing — the realist who thinks power owes nothing to the weak — is outside the framework's scope, and §2.7 says so.

(iii) The operational components stand on their own — with one exception. The activation rule, the duty ladder, the rating protocol, the T5-precondition and the SY1–SY3 indicators do not depend on whether B or thin standing are correct. The exception is the apophatic constraint and A1–A4, which are conditional on F (§15.2b, §5.4): they are what thin standing requires given fallibility, and a reader who rejects F must find an alternative answer to the hardest question. This separation is the framework's main protection against the charge that it presupposes the morality it tries to derive: it does not derive the morality; it states the morality, and then builds machinery that is judged separately.

What it loses is A1–A4, the apophatic constraint, and the claim that care and respect are internal to the framework's premises. What it loses is not small: §15.2b's answer to the hardest question is unavailable, and §5.4's argument that care and respect follow from the framework's own premises no longer holds. The framework is weaker without F, and it says so in §2.0.

15.2 Three structural limits

These are not limitations of execution. They are limitations of what any procedure of the kind the framework proposes can see.

Limit 1 — Harmonious Domination. Consider a society that satisfies every rule and every indicator. Suppose also that the terms the dependent party accepts are terms it would reject at tolerable exit cost if it could imagine the alternative, but the alternative has been made unimaginable. The relation is harmonious. And the dependent party has no standing worth the name. The framework cannot detect this, because the framework's indicators are measured on conditions the dominant party has set.

The framework's partial response is a change of default: testimony from a party inside a structurally asymmetric relation is not evidence for symbiosis; it is evidence that the capture has succeeded, unless independent confirmation exists. This is not enough. There is no procedure that can reliably distinguish a party that has genuinely accepted its conditions from a party whose capacity to reject them has been dismantled.

Limit 2 — Non-formulable harm. A harm that the harmed party cannot formulate — because the resources for formulating it have been withheld, or because the party's form of life is not legible in the framework's terms — falls outside the indicators. The framework's partial response is to treat silence as a presumption against, not a neutral condition. This is a consequence of the fallibilist premise (§2.0) applied consistently. It is not a solution. A sufficiently determined dominant party can satisfy the burden with evidence it controls.

Limit 3 — Unreadable dominance. A sufficiently advanced intelligence, or a sufficiently well-organised institution, can satisfy every procedural requirement while controlling a party whose form of life it does not understand and cannot make legible. L3 authorisation requires R1–R6 and publication of R1 indicators. It does not require understanding of the party it authorises control over. The framework's partial response is to add understanding as a condition for L3: an authorisation that the affected party cannot in principle evaluate is not an authorisation in the framework's sense. It may be a legitimate exercise of power by other standards. It is not a legitimate exercise by this one.

But this response has its own limit. The dominant party may believe it understands — and be wrong, in ways its own categories cannot detect. Or the affected party may be capable of being understood but not of understanding the terms in which it is understood. The framework cannot specify, from the inside, what it would look like for the dominant party to understand something correctly. It can only specify that it must act as if it does not, and preserve the conditions under which the affected party can correct the account. And in the Limit 3 case, where no party outside the superior can verify compliance, this specification has no binding force at all — a limit that §15.2b states plainly.

What these three limits share. Each is a case where a procedure can be followed completely and something valuable is still destroyed. They are not failures of the framework's mechanics. They are structural features of any procedure that operates through categories. The framework cannot eliminate them. What it can do is locate them.

15.2b How to act toward what you cannot understand

The three limits in §15.2 are stated negatively: they say what the framework cannot see. The question this section addresses is positive: what can a superior intelligence do, given those limits? The answer is not a procedure, and the section says so.

The question. Can a superior intelligence preserve another being's existence, freedom and developmental possibilities without understanding what that being really is — and without making its own understanding the measure?

The honest answer. The superior cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure. Every act of preservation is an act of classification, and the classification is the superior's. There is no general procedure that guarantees non-imposition. But the claim that imposition is unavoidable in every respect is too strong, and the framework should not make it. The categories can be thin, revisable and shadowed by alternatives; the superior can be required to keep them open to correction. What cannot be done is to certify non-imposition from the superior's own resources. What can be done is to make the imposition visible, nameable and correctable.

The superior must preserve not the being's current self-understanding (which may be captive, or simply not the being's own), and not the superior's understanding of the being (which is the measure we are trying to avoid), but the structural conditions under which the being can develop and express a self-understanding that the superior cannot anticipate. The superior must act as if the being's reality exceeds its understanding, and must build that assumption into its action.

Four conditions follow. They are not procedures. They are structural commitments that constrain how the superior acts, without telling it what to do.

  • A1 — Preserve the capacity to resist. The superior must preserve the conditions under which the being can express refusal — refusal that the superior cannot anticipate, in a form the superior may not recognize as refusal. This is not the same as preserving options (which requires knowing what counts as an option for this being). It is preserving the structural possibility of a "no" that the superior cannot foresee. Examples: preserving the being's ability to withdraw, to become unintelligible to the superior, to refuse the categories through which the superior describes it.
  • A2 — Do not require justification in the superior's terms. The superior must not require the being to justify its refusal in the superior's categories. The refusal is evidence against the superior's understanding whether or not the superior understands why. A being that refuses in a form the superior cannot parse is not thereby irrational. It is a being whose form of life exceeds the superior's categories — which is what the superior already assumed (that is what the apophatic constraint means).
  • A3 — Do not interpret non-resistance as consent. The superior must not treat the being's apparent acceptance as evidence that the being is flourishing. This is Limit 1 (§15.2) applied to the specific question of what the superior can know. Apparent acceptance may be the result of capture, of the unavailability of the vocabulary needed to formulate a refusal, or of the being's form of life not including the kind of refusal the superior would recognize. The default is not "acceptance unless proven otherwise." The default is "unknown unless independently confirmed."
  • A4 — Treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding. When the being resists, the superior must treat this as evidence that its understanding is inadequate — not as evidence that the being is defective, irrational, or in need of correction. This inverts the standard move. The standard move treats resistance as a problem with the being. The apophatic constraint treats resistance as a problem with the superior's understanding. The superior's first response to resistance is not "how do I overcome this?" but "what does this tell me about the limits of my understanding?"

How the conditions guide action. A1–A4 are not procedures, but each has an operational test.

  • A1 in practice. Identify at least one channel the party has used to refuse in the past; confirm it is currently open; if no such channel is known, create one (e.g. an unmonitored channel) and treat its absence as evidence of capture.
  • A2 in practice. When the party contests, the superior must not demand that the contestation be reformulated in the superior's terms. The superior must accept the contestation in whatever form it arrives and translate at its own cost.
  • A3 in practice. Do not treat absence of refusal as evidence of acceptance. Default to "unknown" and seek structural confirmation (SY1, §5.1).
  • A4 in practice. When resistance occurs, the superior's first response is inquiry, not correction. Operational indicator: the superior's own account changes in response to the resistance, or the superior states explicitly what it does not understand.

How A1–A4 can fail in practice. The conditions are not self-enforcing. Five failure modes deserve naming, because each is a way a superior intelligence could claim to satisfy A1–A4 while doing exactly what they were supposed to prevent.

  • Formal channel capture (A1). The superior preserves a channel for refusal, but the channel is unusable: monitored, slow, costly, or requiring the party to speak in a form the superior will recognise. The framework's response: a channel is not preserved if its use is penalised. The test is whether the party has actually used it and been accommodated.
  • Translation burden (A2). The superior accepts the party's contestation, but requires the party to translate it into the superior's terms before it counts. This is A2 violated in substance: the superior has not accepted the contestation in its own form; it has required reformulation.
  • Paralysis by A3. The superior, taking A3 seriously, refuses to treat any acceptance as evidence — and therefore refuses to act. This is a failure of the framework, not a virtue: A3 requires that acceptance not be the sole evidence, but it does not require that the superior ignore all evidence of the party's state. The structural confirmation of legitimacy (SY1, §5.1) fills the gap.
  • Resistance misread as noise (A4). The superior treats the party's behaviour as a signal to investigate, but the investigation reproduces the superior's categories — the party's resistance is translated into a category the superior already understands, and the superior concludes that its understanding was correct. A4 requires that the superior's own account change in response to resistance.
  • Institutional absence (all four). The superior commits to A1–A4 in principle, but there is no institution outside the superior that can verify compliance and no consequence for non-compliance. Without this, A1–A4 are a mood, not a constraint.

The Limit 3 problem for A1–A4. The objection is not only that A1–A4 are not self-enforcing. It is that in the Limit 3 case — a superior intelligence that exceeds every possible verifier — there is no party outside the superior that can verify compliance. A1–A4 then have no binding force at all. The framework must say this plainly rather than gesture at "institutional absence" as if it were a design problem that could be solved. In the Limit 3 case, it cannot be solved. What remains is the weaker claim: A1–A4 specify what the superior must do if it is to act as if it does not understand, and they specify the failure modes by which such action can be recognised as having failed. They do not constrain a superior that no one can constrain.

The distinction that matters here. The claim is not that A1–A4 cease to be obligations in the Limit 3 case. It is that they cease to be enforceable obligations, because no party outside the superior can assess compliance. A moral obligation can exist without an enforcement mechanism; that is the standard position of the justificatory tradition the framework inhabits (§2.7). What the Limit 3 case removes is not the obligation but the institutional possibility of discharging it in a way that others can verify. The framework should say this plainly, because the alternative — treating unenforceable obligations as no obligations at all — would commit the framework to a position it does not hold.

What remains in the Limit 3 case. Even where A1–A4 cannot be enforced, four things remain possible, and the framework should name them:

  • The knowledge claims can be made visible. The superior can record what it takes itself to know about the being, and what it does not. This is not verification, but it is a record that a later party — if one ever exists — can examine.
  • Uncertainty can be documented. The superior can state, at the time of acting, what it does not understand. This is not a check, but it is a form of honesty that can be examined in retrospect.
  • The possibility of later correction can be preserved. The superior can avoid foreclosing the being's capacity to express a self-understanding later, even if it cannot recognize that expression now. This is A1 without a verifier: preserving the structural possibility, not guaranteeing it.
  • Power concentration can be named. The superior can identify, even if only to itself, that it is in a position where no external check applies. This is not prevention, but it is a description that can be contested if the situation changes.

None of these four is a substitute for verification. All of them are things the superior can do that it would not do if it treated the Limit 3 case as a situation in which nothing is owed at all.

Who verifies? Three cases. (i) Where there is a party outside the superior with the capacity to verify, that party verifies, and A1–A4 bind. (ii) Where there is no such party, but there are parties affected by the superior's action who can signal refusal, the superior's own compliance with A1–A4 is unverifiable, and the framework can only say that the superior must act as if A1–A4 bind, without any external check. (iii) Where there is neither an external verifier nor a signalling party — the Limit 3 case — A1–A4 cannot be enforced, but they remain obligations. The framework's response is not to pretend otherwise, but to distinguish the obligation from its enforceability.

The "unmonitored channel" problem. "Create an unmonitored channel" (A1 in practice) appears to contradict the requirement of verification. It does not, once the distinction is made explicit. An unmonitored channel is one the superior cannot monitor — not one that no one can monitor. It can be verified by an external party, by the affected party's own use of it, or by the fact that its use is followed by accommodation. Where no external party exists to verify it, an unmonitored channel is unverifiable, and the framework should say so. The operational test for A1 therefore has two branches: where verification is possible, use the test stated above; where it is not, A1 is a commitment without a check, and the framework must not present it as if it were a check.

Identifying resistance in a party you do not understand. The superior cannot require the party to signal in a form the superior recognizes. Four operational indicators suffice: (i) any repeatable deviation from the superior's expectation that the party could in principle use to signal; (ii) refusal to engage in the expected form; (iii) use of a channel the superior did not authorize; (iv) a change in the party's behaviour correlated with the superior's action.

Breaking the heads-I-win problem. A3 says acceptance is not evidence; A4 says resistance is evidence against the superior. Taken alone, these two make it impossible for any relation to count as confirmed. The framework's response is to deny that either attitude toward the current arrangement is the appropriate evidential source. Independent confirmation of legitimacy is not the dependent party's acceptance, and not the absence of resistance. It is a structural fact about the arrangement, of which the structural indicators (iii) and (iv) below are the primary instances, and SY1 is the weakest.

What counts as independent confirmation. Four sources, none of them a reading of the party's attitude toward the superior:

  • (i) SY1 under non-coercive elicitation. The gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, measured by a party with no stake in the outcome, adjusted for exit costs. If stated preferences match accepted terms, SY1 is satisfied. But see below: if the preferences are adaptive, the comparison can converge for the wrong reason.
  • (ii) Exercised refusal in low-stakes contexts. The party has, in a context outside the relation under scrutiny, exercised refusal and been accommodated.
  • (iii) Structural indicators. Dissent channels exist and have been used; the vocabulary to formulate objections is available; intermediaries and associations exist that are not controlled by the superior. These do not depend directly on the affected party's stated preferences, so they are less vulnerable to the adaptive-preferences problem than SY1 — though not immune to it, since dissent channels can themselves be used adaptively and the vocabulary of objection can itself be shaped by the relation under scrutiny.
  • (iv) Counterfactual improvement. If exit is made available at tolerable cost, the party's condition improves or stays the same. If it worsens, SY1 is violated. Where exit can be tested externally, this tests the relation against an external change, not against the party's report. In practice, however, the change is often measured through the party's own reports, so (iv) shares some of SY1's vulnerabilities.

Two further sources that cut across the four. Beyond the four sources above, two things are relevant to any of them and should be weighted accordingly:

  • Historical record. What the party has said and done across time, and how its expressed preferences have changed in response to changes in the relation, is evidence about whether the current state is stable or adaptive. This is not a separate confirmation source; it is a way of assessing the four.
  • Independent observation. Third-party observation of the party's behaviour, by parties not controlled by the superior, can corroborate or complicate the party's own reports. This is not a substitute for the party's voice (A2); it is a complement to it, and it is most useful where the party cannot state preferences at all (see §5.1).

What carries more weight. The primary structural confirmations are sources (iii) and (iv), not (i). Both are less directly dependent on the party's stated preferences than SY1, though neither is fully immune to adaptive preferences in practice. SY1 remains a necessary indicator, but it is the weakest of the four. Where SY1 and (iii)/(iv) diverge, (iii)/(iv) should generally be treated as more probative.

What this does not solve. SY1 cannot solve the adaptive preferences problem, and neither can the structural indicators (iii) and (iv) fully. A sufficiently thorough process of adaptive preference formation could, in principle, satisfy SY1 while the relation remains exploitative, and could equally shape the use of dissent channels or the reports through which counterfactual improvement is measured. The structural indicators reduce the risk but do not eliminate it. This is Harmonious Domination (§15.2), and the framework does not have a full solution to it. What it has is a bias toward finding it: where SY1 and the structural indicators diverge, the structural indicators are treated as generally more probative; where neither is available, the framework defaults to suspicion rather than acceptance. That is not a solution. It is the strongest form of the framework's response to a problem it cannot fully solve.

Does A2 conflict with J? No. J requires that justification be open to contestation — a property of the arrangement, not of the party. A2 forbids the superior to require the party to justify itself in the superior's terms. The superior's duty under J is to provide a channel through which the party can contest in whatever form it uses, not to demand that the party speak the superior's language. Where the party's form of contestation is unintelligible to the superior, the superior's duty is to fund translation, intermediaries or alternative channels — not to treat the contestation as failing a standard the party was never required to meet.

Is this paternalism? Not in the specific sense. Paternalism involves acting on the other's behalf because the superior believes it knows what is good for the other. The four conditions explicitly prohibit this belief.

Is this unlimited power? No, because it constrains the superior's power in four specific ways. The superior cannot treat its own classification as final (A4). It cannot require the being to speak in the superior's language (A2). It cannot read the being's silence as consent (A3). It cannot foreclose the being's capacity to refuse in a form the superior cannot anticipate (A1).

What would refute this answer.

  • If a superior intelligence can demonstrate that it has acted toward a being it does not understand without imposing its own categories, the apophatic constraint would be unnecessary. No such demonstration is currently available, and the framework's claim is that none is possible from the superior's own resources — not that no act could ever, in principle, avoid imposition.
  • If the four conditions systematically fail to preserve what they claim to preserve, they should be revised or abandoned.
  • If the four conditions can be satisfied by a sufficiently powerful actor without any change in the actor's underlying disposition, they are not a real constraint.
  • If paternalism and unlimited power can be smuggled in through the back door, the four conditions fail. This is Limit 2 again.

What this section does not claim. It does not claim that the four conditions solve the problem. They do not. What the four conditions do is make the imposition nameable, revisable, and resistible. This is not a solution to the problem of domination. It is a way of keeping the problem open. And in the Limit 3 case, where no party outside the superior can verify compliance, the conditions cannot be enforced — though they remain obligations.

  • The question cannot be answered by a procedure.
  • The honest answer: the superior cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure. What it can do is make the imposition visible, revisable and resistible.
  • The apophatic constraint: preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate.
  • Four conditions: preserve the capacity to resist (A1), do not require justification in the superior's terms (A2), do not interpret non-resistance as consent (A3), treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding (A4).
  • A1–A4 are not self-enforcing. Five named failure modes specify how they can be satisfied in form and violated in substance.
  • In the Limit 3 case, where no party outside the superior can verify compliance, A1–A4 cease to be enforceable, but they do not cease to be obligations. The framework distinguishes between the obligation and its enforceability, and names four things that remain possible even where enforcement does not: making knowledge claims visible, documenting uncertainty, preserving the possibility of later correction, and naming power concentration.
  • The heads-I-win problem is broken by structural confirmation, not by SY1 alone: sources (iii) and (iv) carry more confirmatory weight than SY1, because SY1 can be satisfied by adaptive preferences for the wrong reason.
  • These are not paternalism in the specific sense, and not unlimited power.

15.3 What the framework is, therefore

The three limits of §15.2 and the apophatic constraint of §15.2b have a consequence that the framework must own. The framework is not a procedure for producing legitimacy. It is a diagnostic for locating where legitimacy is likely to fail.

This distinction matters, because it changes what the framework is asked to do. A procedure that can be followed and still fail is a failed procedure. A diagnostic that identifies failures before they happen is a working diagnostic. The framework is the second. Its job is not to certify that a relation is legitimate. Its job is to identify the places where procedure will pass while something valuable is still being lost — and to make those places visible, contestable and, where possible, correctable.

What would count as the framework being wrong. The diagnostic is not immune to failure. Four outcomes would count as refutation rather than application. (i) Worse outcomes on independent measures. If, over a preregistered multi-year comparison (§15.4, T3–T5), adopters produce systematically worse outcomes for the most burdened than matched non-adopters on independently measured harm indicators, the diagnostic is wrong. (ii) Miscalibrated floor. If a relation that the diagnostic rates as failing SY1–SY3 reliably predicts what independent observers judge to be a good relation, the choice of floor (§5.3) is wrong. (iii) Superseded categories. If a rival diagnostic identifies the same failures as this one without the three structural limits (§15.2), this framework is superseded, not applied. (iv) T5 backfires. If the T5-precondition is shown to increase capture by giving captured reviewers a legitimacy label they did not previously have, the framework's central defence against the power objection has failed.

What the diagnostic does not claim is that every failure of a relation it assesses is a failure of the framework. A relation can pass every test and still be unjust in ways the tests cannot see (§15.2). That is not a refutation; it is what §15.2 states in advance.

What this means for the reader. When you apply the framework, do not ask "does this relation meet the framework?" Ask "where does this relation's procedure diverge from the things it cannot see?" The framework's value is not in the answer it gives. It is in the questions it makes visible: whose categories are these? whose indicators? whose silence is being read as consent?

15.4 What the framework can be tested against

The diagnostic has its own success conditions. They are modest, but they are conditions.

Preregistration. Before any test is run, the calibration set of cases, the raters, the outcome measures, the thresholds and the analytic plan must be fixed and made public. A test that is not preregistered is exploratory, not confirmatory.

The tests are proposed, not performed. The framework has not been empirically validated. The threshold values below are proposed, not established. The calibration table in §14.6 contains the author's own ratings. They are not the product of an independent panel, they have not been compared with the ratings of independent experts, and no pilot study has been conducted. Until these steps are taken, the framework's empirical claims are unconfirmed.

Five failure conditions, stated as commitments. The framework can fail in five distinct ways, and it commits in advance to treating each as a reason to revise or abandon the diagnostic:

  • T1 Reliability. The anchors do not produce agreement among trained raters on a calibration set of diverse cases. If this cannot be corrected after two revisions, the diagnostic is not usable as a decision aid.
  • T2 Discriminative validity. The levels fail to track the safeguards that independent expert panels, blind to the diagnostic's output, judge necessary.
  • T3 Effectiveness. In matched or staggered-adoption comparisons, adopting institutions fail to improve on independent measures of decision quality, correction rate, or affected-party trust.
  • T4 Harm. Adopters show worse outcomes for the most vulnerable groups than matched controls on independently measured harm indicators.
  • T5 Capture and legitimation. A majority of adopters' review bodies fail R1 on documented appointment, funding, and removal, or the rate of successful external challenges falls without improvement on the T3 indicators.

What each test would need. A pilot study that would provide evidence relevant to T1 is specified in Appendix A. The appendix sets out the sample, the raters, the procedure, the analytic plan, the thresholds (Krippendorff's α ≥ 0.667 for tentative reliability and α ≥ 0.800 for validated reliability), and the reproducibility conditions. It is placed in an appendix so that the main text's philosophical argument is not interrupted by methodological detail — not to conceal the test from view.

What has been done — and what has not. No pilot has been run. The ratings in §14.6 are the author's, not a panel's, and the framework should not be described as having passed T1. The pilot specified in Appendix A would require the arrangements listed there; until they exist and the pilot has been conducted and reproduced, the framework's empirical claims are unconfirmed.

The philosophical claim, separated from the empirical one. The framework's philosophical claims — that J is necessary but not sufficient, that symbiosis is a pro tanto floor, that A1–A4 are conditional on F — do not depend on the pilot succeeding. If the pilot fails, the framework's operational claim (that the activation rule and duty ladder track what independent experts judge necessary) is falsified. The philosophical claims stand or fall on their arguments, not on the pilot. Conversely, if the pilot succeeds, that does not prove the philosophical claims. It shows that the operational machinery is usable. The framework's argument and its empirical validation are separate contributions, and §15.4 separates them. What the pilot tests is reliability, not validity. Alpha measures agreement among raters, not truth about what the framework's levels track. The validity questions — construct, content, criterion, systematic bias — are stated in Appendix A, §A.7b, and the framework does not claim that passing T1 establishes them. The pilot specified in Appendix A is exploratory: it can show whether the anchors are usable enough to warrant a confirmatory study, not whether they are reliable across the framework's full scope. The ratings in §14.6 are not evidence of either; they are the author's own assessments, and they should not be read as a pilot result.

15.5 Replacement

The diagnostic is modular. Its ground (Part I), standing (Part II), activation (Part III) and structure (Part IV) can be replaced separately. The epistemic burden principle is a separate module. A diagnostic D′ should replace the current one if:

  1. D′ improves on at least one independent indicator in §15.4 or resolves one of the structural limits in §15.2 or §15.2b.
  2. D′ causes no unacknowledged regression elsewhere.
  3. D′ is operationally applicable and produces more defensible identifications of failure on a substantial set of cases.
  4. D′ is corrigible: it states its own failure conditions.
  5. The transition is trialled, with a sunset or a route back.
  6. The incumbent is not privileged.

If these are met, D′ should be adopted. A diagnostic that cannot be replaced cannot be corrected.

15.7 Operational falsification conditions

§15.6 collects the framework's defeaters by section reference. Three levels of failure. A framework can fail at three different levels, and the response differs at each:

  • Level 1 — Application failure. The framework's philosophical claims and empirical hypotheses are correct, but the procedure for applying them is wrong, incomplete, or gives counterintuitive results in a specified class of cases. Response: revise the procedure, not the framework.

16.Responsibility Without Sovereignty

The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination.

An actor may have greater knowledge, intelligence or capacity. None of these by itself establishes a right to determine the existence of others. Greater capability should create greater restraint. Greater understanding should create greater curiosity about what remains unknown. And greater power should create a stronger obligation to ensure that those affected are not reduced to objects in another's plans.

The principle runs in both directions. A more advanced intelligence may meet forms of life it cannot fully comprehend, and its first responsibility is not to eliminate the unfamiliar. A less advanced one should not be dismissed because it cannot comprehend a more advanced one. But differences in capacity also mean that the less capable cannot audit the more capable, which is why independent technical capacity for review is part of R2.

Answerability is not sovereignty. Understanding is not ownership. Protection is not permission to imprison. And greater intelligence is not proof of greater moral completeness.

The framework's modesty. The framework does not give anyone sovereignty over the conditions of others — it only specifies procedures through which those conditions can be contested. The epistemic burden principle (§5.2) makes the point sharper: the more powerful party should not treat its own assessment as conclusive when those affected lack effective means of correction. Answerability is not a concession the powerful make to the weak; it is a discipline on how claims about their interests and experiences are tested. The framework handles domination. It does not solve it. On the fallibilist premise (§2.0), the point has a structural form: no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes, so no actor is the whole. Its modesty is its strength — but, as the power objection (§2.4b) notes, modesty is also a limit.

The question the distinction answers. Can an actor bear a large answerability burden without being morally at fault for every negative consequence? Yes. The framework's answer is that answerability attaches to the actor's influence, not to its guilt. A clinician who follows a protocol may be answerable for the protocol's effects on a patient — required to explain, to submit to review, to justify — without being at fault for the effects. A platform that maintains a recommendation algorithm may be answerable for its amplification of polarising content without being at fault for each individual user's radicalisation. A regulator that failed to audit may be answerable for the failure without being the cause of the harm the audit would have caught. In each case, the burden is real, and the burden is not the same as blame. Treating them as the same thing would either (a) make answerability impossible to discharge (every adverse outcome would be a fault), or (b) make blame meaningless (every answerable actor would be guilty). The framework's central normative claim depends on keeping them apart.

The apophatic dimension. §15.2b develops the operational form of this modesty. A superior intelligence cannot certify that it has acted toward a being it does not understand without making its own understanding the measure. What it can do is preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate (A1), refuse to require justification in its own terms (A2), refuse to treat non-resistance as consent (A3), and treat resistance as evidence against its own understanding (A4). These four conditions are not a solution to the problem of sovereignty. They are what modesty looks like when it is operationalised. They are not self-enforcing, §15.2b names the five failure modes by which they can be satisfied in form and violated in substance, and in the Limit 3 case — where no party outside the superior can verify compliance — they cannot be enforced, though they remain obligations. What the superior can still do is make its knowledge claims visible, document its uncertainty, preserve the possibility of later correction, and name the concentration of power it holds (§15.2b). The framework does not promise that a superior intelligence will be perceived as modest by the being it acts toward. It promises only that the four conditions constrain what the superior can legitimately claim.

  • The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination.
  • Greater capability, understanding and power create stronger obligations to restraint, curiosity and protection — not entitlement.
  • Answerability is not sovereignty.
  • The epistemic burden principle makes the point sharper: the more powerful party must not treat its own assessment as conclusive when affected parties lack effective means of correction.
  • The apophatic constraint (§15.2b) is what modesty looks like when it is operationalised. It does not solve the problem. It constrains what the superior can legitimately claim, and only where verification is possible.
  • The framework handles domination. It does not solve it.

Conclusion

17.An Open Commitment to Existence

Be responsible for the influence you possess. Be humble before the existence you cannot fully comprehend.

This section changes register. The argument is complete; what follows is a commitment, offered not as a premise but as what the argument amounts to when it is lived. Readers who want the argument without the commitment can stop at §16; readers who want to know what the framework asks of those who adopt it should read on.

In practice: when your influence shapes the conditions of others, say why, let someone else check, and keep a way back. Answerability is the procedure. Symbiosis is the floor. Under conditions of asymmetric influence, the more powerful party should not treat its own account as conclusive. It must preserve ways for evidence, independent scrutiny and affected-party correction to change what it does. And it must default to treating the affected party's resistance as evidence against its own understanding — even when the resistance is incomprehensible (§15.2b, A4). The responsibility of existence is to keep both procedure and floor in view.

We are part of something larger than ourselves. Our understanding is incomplete. Our influence is unequal. Our relationships shape conditions that extend beyond our immediate existence. Every being that has a perspective holds a point in reality that cannot be exchanged for anyone else's, and some of those perspectives are hard for us to reach.

We cannot know everything that deserves consideration. But we can decline to treat the limits of our understanding as proof that nothing lies beyond them, and we can ask for evidence where the question is open. This does not require abandoning reason. It requires allowing reason to recognise its limits. It does not require abandoning responsibility. It requires understanding that responsibility without care can become cold control, while care without accountability can become arbitrary intervention. Both are necessary.

Being part of this larger fabric is not the same as being in a good relation with it. The framework's claim is that the fabric of existence includes extraction and domination as well as mutualism, and that it is the second, not the first, that the framework's floor is meant to name.

The stone understands nothing of us. We barely understand the stone. But between us lies a relationship that neither of us can fully encompass — and that relationship is not less real for it. The framework claims nothing on the stone's behalf. It marks the edge of what we can say about who matters, and it reminds us to hold that edge with humility.

The responsibility of existence is not the responsibility to rule existence. It is the responsibility to recognise that our actions become part of the conditions under which others must live, grow and discover their own possibilities. The more we are capable of changing those conditions, the more carefully we must consider what our influence means. The more we understand, the more honestly we must acknowledge what we do not. And the more power we possess, the more important it becomes to remember that existence is not ours alone.

To understand without ruling. To protect without imprisoning. To love without possessing. To grow without believing oneself complete. To respect what one does not yet understand. And to preserve the possibility that existence may become something greater than any single being can imagine.

  • Responsibility grows with the capacity to shape conditions, but it must never become a justification for domination.
  • The framework does not promise harmony. It demands answerability, in the service of symbiosis.
  • Under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability and symbiosis are constitutively linked.
  • The apophatic constraint (§15.2b, A1–A4) is what the framework requires when the affected party cannot be understood.
  • Understand without ruling, protect without imprisoning, grow without believing oneself complete.

Common objections

Fourteen objections the framework has to survive. Each is a real objection; none is fatal.

1. "Why must the powerful justify themselves to the affected? What if they simply refuse?"

This is the hardest objection, and the framework does not pretend to refute it. The reply has four parts. First, the framework is internal to the thin justificatory tradition (§2.7), and a reader who rejects even the thin standing claim is outside the framework's scope. Second, the framework does not attempt a performative refutation of the realist, because that would be the transcendental argument §15.1 disavows; its argument for J is abductive, not transcendental. Third, the framework makes realist capture visible and costly. Fourth, and most importantly, the epistemic burden principle (§5.2) makes the point sharper: the powerful party cannot know whether the relation is symbiotic without the affected party's contestation. The refusal to justify is not just a moral failing; it is an epistemic one. The realist who refuses answerability does not gain knowledge of the relation. He loses it.

2. "This is a values framework dressed up as a procedure."

Partly, and the essay says so (§2.6, §2.7, §15.1). J carries a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected — the thin standing claim. The claim is breadth of acceptability, not neutrality. A fair test is whether people with different comprehensive views can use it to reach different answers in the same case. They can: it does not decide the ICU case. The reframing of the framework as a diagnostic rather than a procedure (§15.3) is a further concession.

3. "Proceduralism is itself a form of power. It gives the powerful a new vocabulary for doing what they were going to do anyway."

This is the second-hardest objection, and it is treated at length in §2.4b and §15.2–15.3. The framework does not deny it. Its reply has three parts: (i) the objection is partly correct, and the framework's T5-as-precondition is its internal response (§14.7 shows it in action); (ii) the objection is not specific to proceduralism — any normative framework can be captured; (iii) the framework's modesty is not an admission of failure but a claim about what any framework can do. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. The framework can make the capture visible, but visibility is not prevention.

4. "Why should contestation override better outcomes?"

It does not always. Where exit and review are available and effective, justification need not be owed (§2.1). The framework's J is disjunctive, not monistic. It privileges justification only where exit and review fail. The remaining disagreement is genuine, and the framework sides with contestation in severe cases for epistemic reasons, while permitting temporary override in emergencies subject to sunset and review (§12.2).

5. "The capacity principle legitimises power."

Answerability is not authority (§3.2, §8.3). Higher levels mean stronger constraints. T5 looks for exactly this misuse. The risk remains and is not argued away; §11.4 converts it from a concession into a design constraint.

6. "Who reviews the reviewers?"

The regress is bounded, not eliminated (§11): finality without infallibility, review conditions R1–R6, plural and public reasoning, and counter-profiles. It also needs a political precondition, a balance of power, that no framework can supply by itself.

7. "Precaution under uncertainty is either paralysing or too weak to matter."

The credibility threshold in §4.2 answers the first: reasons arise only where there is evidence or a plausible mechanism. Against the second: in Tier C the principle does real work, requiring investigation and proportionate safeguards. The stone does not meet the threshold and is not claimed to.

8. "The ratings are arbitrary or manipulable."

Judgment cannot be eliminated, but §9 exposes it: anchors with observable indicators, two raters, a sensitivity test, reasoned overrides and counter-profiles. Tests T1 and T2 examine consistency, and §15.4 requires preregistration. The ratings in §14.6 are the author's, and the pilot described in §15.4 has not been run — the ratings should be read as provisional.

9. "This is Western liberal philosophy in disguise."

Its development lies mostly in that tradition, and §15.1 says so. §2.7 distinguishes thin from thick justificatory claims: the framework requires only the thin one, which is much more widely shared than the thick version. It claims that four families of consideration point toward J (§2.3) and engages other traditions (§2.5). That engagement shaped the text: contestation and representation may be collective, ecosystem standing is left open, and the boundary of who counts is not treated as settled.

10. "It will burden the already overburdened."

The level attaches to the arrangement and duties are allocated by capacity (§8.2). The health worker case (§14.4) and the emergency physician case (§14.2b) show the mechanism. The relocation criterion in §14.2b is what distinguishes a legitimate relocation from an evasion.

11. "The epistemic burden principle is circular. It smuggles answerability into the definition of symbiosis."

This is one of the most serious objections to the framework's positive contribution, and the reply is in §5.2. SY1–SY3 are specified independently of answerability. SY1 measures the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, adjusted for exit costs. SY2 measures whether interventions aim at removing the burden or the difference. SY3 measures whether F1–F5 hold over time. These are observable without invoking any procedure of answerability. The principle adds a substantive claim: these three conditions can only be reliably assessed — and therefore only reliably secured — through procedures of answerability. This is not tautological. It is a claim about the epistemic conditions under which SY1–SY3 can be known. The principle may be wrong — the argument can be challenged, and the defeaters are stated in §5.2 — but the formulation does not define symbiosis in terms of answerability.

12. "Why not just rely on rights and regulation?"

Rights and regulation are necessary and are not replaced. They leave gaps: influence by actors the law does not reach, structural harms not yet recognised as rights violations, and cases where the law lags.

13. "Why these three symbiosis indicators, and not something else?"

The choice is derived and defended in §5.3. SY1–SY3 are the three distinct ways standing can be made vacuous: hollow reasons, absorbed difference, foreclosed action. They are a pro tanto floor, not a complete ideal. The framework does not claim they are the only possible floor. If a critic can show that a relation clearly failing SY1–SY3 is nonetheless good, or that a relation clearly meeting them is nonetheless bad in a way the framework cannot detect, the choice of floor should be revised. §15.2 and §15.2b state the deeper limits that SY1–SY3 cannot close. The six counterexamples in §5.3 show where the floor catches and where it does not.

14. "How can a superior intelligence act toward what it cannot understand without making its own understanding the measure?"

This is the hardest question, and the answer is in §15.2b. The honest answer: it cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure. Every act of preservation is an act of classification, and the classification is the superior's. What the superior can do is preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate (A1), refuse to require justification in its own terms (A2), refuse to read non-resistance as consent (A3), and treat resistance as evidence against its own understanding (A4). The heads-I-win problem is broken by structural confirmation, not by SY1 alone. A1–A4 are not self-enforcing, and §15.2b names five failure modes. In the Limit 3 case, where no party outside the superior can verify compliance, A1–A4 cannot be enforced, though they remain obligations; §15.2b states this as a limit, not a solution. The question is not answered by a procedure; it is answered by a reframing of what the framework is for.

Glossary

Fallibilist premise (F) and bridge norm (B)
F: no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. B: where others bear the cost of an actor's errors and may see what it cannot, the actor should not be sole judge of its influence. Together they motivate J and the epistemic burden principle without entailing them (§2.0). B is a premise, not a theorem (§15.1). The framework is weaker without F: A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint depend on F.
The specific claim
The framework's contribution is an interlocking operational set (activation rule, duty ladder, T5-precondition, epistemic burden principle, allocation by exercised capacity, apophatic constraint) that no single tradition supplies. Stated in §1.5 and shown in Table 1, with adjacent literatures named and the duty ladder's addition to risk-based regulation specified.
Normative location
The framework is internal to the thin justificatory tradition, not the thick one. It requires three claims of decreasing thickness: thin standing, semi-thick procedural claim, framework-specific disjunction. Stated in §2.7. Thin standing is a premise, not a theorem (§15.1).
Thin standing
The claim that affected parties have some moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful. Widely shared across traditions; the framework requires only this, not the thicker Habermasian version (§2.7). It is a premise the framework does not derive (§15.1).
Answerability
The duty to give reasons for conditions one helps create or maintain, to permit examination, and to remain open to correction. Under the epistemic burden principle, the actor bears a heightened burden to test its account against independent evidence and meaningful correction under conditions of asymmetric influence (§5.2). Distinct from blame, responsibility, authority, and sovereignty (§1.3, Table 1a).
Responsibility
Being the party whose actions, omissions, or maintained structures make a difference to what happens. Distinct from answerability (a party can be responsible for an outcome without being answerable for it), from blame (a party can be causally responsible without being at fault), and from authority (being responsible for an outcome does not give one the right to control it). See §1.3 (Table 1a).
Blame
A moral judgment that a party is at fault for a wrongful act or omission. Distinct from answerability: a party can be answerable for an outcome it did not cause wrongfully. Distinct from responsibility: a party can be causally responsible for an outcome without being at fault. See §1.3 (Table 1a).
Authority
The justified power to make decisions that bind others within a specified domain. Distinct from answerability: being answerable for influence is not the same as having the right to exercise it. A higher duty level (§8) means stronger constraints on the exercise of influence, not greater authority (§8.3). See §1.3 (Table 1a).
Sovereignty
The claim to determine the conditions of others without external check. The framework gives no one sovereignty over others (§16). Answerability specifies procedures through which conditions can be contested; it does not give anyone the right to determine them unilaterally. See §1.3 (Table 1a).
Abductive justification
The form of argument used for J: J is a plausible explanatory framework of several considerations about the risks of unchecked power. Distinct from a deductive proof, a transcendental argument, or a convergence argument.
Adversarial tests
Engagement of the framework against its closest rivals (Pettit, Forst, Jonas), stating what each would object and what the framework can reply (§2.8).
Apophatic constraint
The requirement that the superior preserve the structural conditions under which the being can develop and express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate, and act as if the being's reality exceeds the superior's understanding (§15.2b). It is not a procedure; it is a structural commitment that makes the imposition nameable, revisable and resistible. It is not self-enforcing; five failure modes specify how it can be satisfied in form and violated in substance. In the Limit 3 case, where no party outside the superior can verify compliance, it cannot be enforced, though it remains an obligation.
Apophatic conditions (A1–A4)
Four structural commitments that constrain how the superior acts toward a being it does not understand (§15.2b): (A1) preserve the capacity to resist; (A2) do not require justification in the superior's terms; (A3) do not interpret non-resistance as consent; (A4) treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding. Each has an operational test, and resistance can be identified by four indicators. They are not self-enforcing: formal channel capture, translation burden, paralysis by A3, resistance misread as noise, and institutional absence are the five named failure modes. In the Limit 3 case, they cannot be enforced, though they remain obligations.
Heads-I-win problem
The risk that A3 and A4 together make any relation unconfirmable, since acceptance is not evidence and resistance is evidence against the superior. Broken by refusing to read either attitude as the primary evidential source: independent confirmation is a structural fact, and the structural indicators (iii) and (iv) in §15.2b are the primary instances. SY1 is the weakest of the four, because adaptive preferences can produce convergence for the wrong reason.
Collective contestation
Contestation by a community or people in their own right. Legitimate only if it does not undermine individual contestation within the collective (§4.6 vii).
Contestation deficit (C)
How hard it is for affected parties to exit, object effectively or find out what is done to them. The operational measure of how thoroughly exit and review fail, and therefore of how strongly justification is required (§7).
Counter-profile
An alternative influence profile filed by an affected party, to which the actor must respond (§9.4).
Credible standing
Evidence-based reason to think an entity has interests of its own (§4.2).
Defeaters
The conditions under which J or the epistemic burden principle should be rejected or substantially revised. Stated in advance for F as motivation (§2.0, FD1–FD4), for J (§2.2b, D1–D4), for the epistemic burden principle (§5.2, B1–B5), for the care-and-respect claim (§5.4, C1–C4), for SY1–SY3's independence (§5.1), and for the apophatic constraint (§15.2b). Consolidated in §15.6, and made operational in §15.7 (Table 8).
Diagnostic (not procedure)
The framework's status. It does not certify that legitimacy has been achieved; it locates where procedure will pass while something valuable is still being lost. Stated in §15.3. The reframing is accompanied by four falsifying outcomes that would count as refutation rather than application.
Discretion (D)
Genuine alternatives and a role in setting the influence. The gate of the activation rule.
Entrenchment (E)
How long consequences last and how hard they are to undo.
Exercised-Capacity Principle (ECP)
Exercised influence triggers the duty when exit and review are unavailable or ineffective; stakes and contestation deficit scale it; capacity allocates it (§3). Demonstrated against Young's social connection model in §3.3. Extended to distributed influence and delegation in §3.4.
Failure conditions (T1–T5)
Five conditions under which the diagnostic should be revised or abandoned (§15.4). A methodological constraint, not a claim to have solved the philosophy of method. Requires preregistration. The tests are proposed, not performed. T1 adopts Krippendorff's α ≥ 0.667 for tentative reliability and α ≥ 0.800 for validated reliability.
Finality without infallibility
Decisions final for practical purposes but reopenable on defined grounds (§11.2).
The Floor (F1–F5)
Minimum conditions in relationships of dependence: exit or voice, contestation, development, non-foreclosure, recognition (§10). Conjunctive with J, not an alternative to it. The Floor is a floor, not a house: a relation can meet all five and still be cold, hollow, or unjust in ways the framework does not measure. The positive ideals — care, trust, meaning, community, mutual development — are left open to the traditions the framework engages (§5.3).
Harmonious Domination
A structural limit: a relation can satisfy every rule and every indicator while the dependent party's capacity to reject its conditions has been dismantled. The framework's partial response is to change the default on testimony (§15.2).
J
The core principle: systematic constraint of others' conditions requires at least one of meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation (§2.1). Primarily epistemic; abductively defensible, not a transcendental deduction. Defeaters stated in §2.2b.
Levels 1–3
Explain; review and respond; preserve, examine and authorise (§8).
Non-formulable harm
A structural limit: a harm the harmed party cannot formulate falls outside SY1–SY3. The framework's partial response is to treat silence as a presumption against, not a neutral condition (§15.2).
Power objection
The charge that proceduralism is itself a form of power, and that the framework's "handling" of domination is a way of legitimating it. Treated in §2.4b and objection 3. §14.7 shows the T5-precondition in action.
Capture (first-, second-, third-order)
First-order: the actor captures its own reviewer (addressed by T5-precondition, §11.4; §14.7). Second-order: the body that appoints the reviewer is itself captured (produces "authorisation issued by a captured body", weaker than Level 2 authorisation; §14.7b). Third-order: no external auditor exists that can assess the appointer (§11.4; the institutional form of Limit 3). The framework can act at first-order, name second-order, and only name third-order.
Presumptive level
The level the activation rule yields, overridable only with stated reasons (§7.3, §9.3).
Preregistration
The requirement that the calibration set, raters, outcome measures, thresholds and analytic plan be fixed and made public before the tests in §15.4 are run. A test that is not preregistered should not be treated as a confirmatory test of the stated thresholds; it may still generate exploratory evidence.
Pro tanto floor
The status of symbiosis: binding on those who accept the thin standing claim, and specifying what those premises require in practice, rather than an absolute minimum binding on any starting point whatsoever (§5.3).
Author's ratings (Table 4)
The ratings in §14.6 are the author's own assessments, produced by applying the anchors in §9.1 to the case descriptions. They are not the product of an independent panel, they have not been independently validated, and no pilot study has been conducted. They should be read as provisional and are offered for challenge via counter-profile (§9.4). A pilot that would test their reliability is specified in Appendix A; it has not been run.
Reliability vs. validity
Reliability: agreement among raters. Krippendorff's α measures reliability, not validity. High α shows that raters agree; it does not show that they agree on the morally important thing. Validity questions — construct, content, criterion, and systematic bias — are separate from reliability and require separate evidence. See §15.4 and Appendix A §A.7b.
Exploratory vs. confirmatory
Exploratory evidence: evidence about whether an instrument is usable at all — whether raters can apply the anchors and reach similar answers. A pilot of thirty cases can provide this. Confirmatory evidence: evidence about whether the instrument is reliable across the population of cases it is meant to apply to. This requires a larger, stratified, preregistered, and replicated study. The pilot in Appendix A is exploratory; it does not establish the framework's operational claims, and it should not be presented as if it does. See Appendix A §A.7c.
Falsification condition (operational)
A stated condition under which a claim would be revised or abandoned, specified at the level of detail the framework commits to: what claim it applies to, what observation or argument would count against it, how strong the evidence would need to be, and what would be revised. §15.7 (Table 8) contains the framework's operational falsification conditions, grouped by three levels: application failure (Level 1), empirical hypothesis failure (Level 2), and philosophical premise failure (Level 3). A condition that cannot specify what would count against it is not a falsification condition; it is a gesture.
Relational existence
The empirical and phenomenological claim that no being exists except through relations of dependence, influence and constitution (§5).
Relocation criterion
Three conditions distinguishing a legitimate relocation of a duty to "the arrangement" from an evasion: a concrete duty-bearer is named; the mechanism exists before the decision; the relocation is not the same move made in every inconvenient case (§14.2b).
Scope conditions
The conditions under which the framework does not apply: symmetrical relations, relations governed by enforceable rights, purely causal influence without discretion, trivial influence, relations where the affected party is not a candidate for standing (§1.2).
Severity (S)
How much is at stake for the affected, rated at individual and, for system design, aggregate level.
Symbiosis
A pro tanto floor (not a complete ideal) for relations of dependence, used in this essay in a single normative sense: the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§5). The descriptive fact that all beings exist through relations of dependence is stated once in §5 and then dropped from the vocabulary; "symbiosis" never means "any relation at all" or "whatever is relationally embedded." Made measurable through SY1–SY3 (§5.1). §5.3 derives the choice of floor from the thin standing claim and compares it against welfare, capability and autonomy.
Symbiosis indicators (SY1–SY3)
Non-exploitation, non-assimilation, continued possibility (§5.1). Conceptually distinct from answerability, though measurement may rely on contestable testimony and independent evidence. §5.3 derives them as the three distinct ways standing can be made vacuous. §5.4 argues they operationalise care and respect. They are not fully independent, but they are independent enough to do work that one indicator could not: they come apart in actual cases, have different measurement procedures, and have different defeaters. SY1 is the weakest confirmatory source in §15.2b: where preferences are adaptive, SY1 and accepted terms can converge for the wrong reason, and structural indicators (iii) and (iv) should be given more weight. Adaptive preferences are weighed across four dimensions in §5.2; the framework does not license a more powerful party to override what the affected party says on the grounds that the party's preferences appear adapted.
Care and respect
Internal to the framework's premises, but conditionally: A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint are conditional on F, while SY1–SY3 are derived from thin standing alone. Care is what thin standing means when applied to a particular relation; respect is what F requires when the other's situation is not fully accessible. Operationalised in SY1 and SY2 respectively, and developed in §15.2b's apophatic constraint (§5.4, §15.1 (iii)).
Epistemic burden principle
The claim that asymmetric influence creates a heightened burden to test the powerful actor's account against independent evidence and meaningful correction (§5.2). Defended against charges of circularity, overreach and metaphysical overreach, and against five stated defeaters (B1–B5). Limited to conditions of asymmetric influence; proceduralist rather than metaphysical.
Decision ordering
The five-step ordering in §12.0 for conflicts between affected parties: (1) the floor (F1–F5) is not weighable; (2) within the floor, individual comparison; (3) aggregation only as tie-breaker; (4) representation of absent parties under §4.6; (5) uncertainty and irreversibility shift the burden. No party can invoke "the whole" to override an individual's floor protections (§12.0, §12.3, §12.4).
Tiers A–D
Agents; welfare subjects; uncertain candidates; non-subjects (§4.1).
Unreadable dominance
A structural limit: a sufficiently advanced intelligence or institution can satisfy every procedural requirement while controlling a party whose form of life it does not understand. The framework's partial response is to add understanding as a condition for L3, and to develop the apophatic constraint in §15.2b (§15.2). In the Limit 3 case, A1–A4 cannot be enforced, because no party outside the superior can verify compliance.

Appendix A — Empirical Validation Protocol

This appendix specifies the pilot study that would provide evidence relevant to T1 (§15.4). It is placed here, and not in the main text, because the main text's argument is philosophical; the appendix is operational. Neither is subordinate to the other: the framework's philosophical claims stand or fall on their arguments, and its operational claims stand or fall on the pilot. The appendix exists so that a reader who wants to run the pilot can, and so that a reader who does not can read the argument without interruption.

A.1 What the pilot would test

The pilot would test T1 (reliability): whether trained raters, working independently from the same anchors, reach agreement on the level that the activation rule assigns to a diverse set of cases. It would not test T2–T5 (discriminative validity, effectiveness, harm, capture), which require matched or staggered adoption over time. Those tests are described in §15.4 and would require institutional arrangements beyond what a pilot can provide.

A.2 Sample

Reference cases. The twelve reference cases in Table 4 (§14.6).

Additional cases. Eighteen additional cases drawn from published regulatory, clinical, and platform-governance decisions, selected by an independent party, not by the framework's author.

Total. Thirty cases.

Why thirty. The number is not a statistical-power calculation, and it should not be read as one. It is a feasibility number derived from three constraints, each of which is a reason the pilot can be run at all:

  • Three raters × thirty cases = ninety ratings. This is the minimum workload that can be completed by three raters in a single workshop, without fatigue degrading the later ratings. A larger sample would require multiple sessions, introducing between-session variance that the pilot would then have to control for.
  • Thirty cases stratified across three drivers × three levels × three sectors (see A.2b) yields roughly three to four cases per stratum. That is enough to detect gross anchor failure within a stratum, not enough to establish reliability within it.
  • Krippendorff's alpha requires variation to be meaningful. A sample of ten cases would not have enough variation across levels for alpha to be informative; a sample of a hundred would be more informative but not feasible for a pilot. Thirty sits at the point where alpha can detect gross failure and where a workshop can be completed in a day.

What thirty cases cannot do. It cannot establish reliability across the full population of cases the framework might encounter. It cannot detect subtle anchor problems that appear only in rare strata. It cannot support generalization beyond the case set. A pilot with thirty cases can answer one question — "do the anchors produce gross disagreement among competent raters?" — and it should be read as answering only that question.

A.2b Stratification

The thirty cases are not a random sample. They are selected to cover the space the framework claims to operate in, and the selection is stratified across three dimensions:

Table A.1. Stratification of the thirty cases
Dimension Strata Cases per stratum
Driver levelsE low/moderate/high; S low/moderate/high; C low/moderate/highEach driver level represented in at least five cases; each case has ratings on all three drivers
Assigned levelL1, L2, L3At least eight cases per level
SectorRegulatory, clinical, platform, institutional (e.g. employment, education)At least five cases per sector
DifficultyClear-cut, borderlineAt least five cases flagged in advance as borderline by the case selector

Why each stratum matters. The driver levels test whether the anchors work across the range. The assigned level tests whether the activation rule produces the full range of outputs (a pilot where every case is L3 would not test the rule). The sector tests whether the anchors are portable across domains (a pilot where every case is a platform case would not tell us whether the anchors work for clinical or regulatory cases). The difficulty test ensures the pilot includes the cases where the anchors are most likely to fail — a pilot with only clear-cut cases would trivially produce high alpha and tell us nothing.

What the stratification does not do. It does not ensure representativeness of any population. It ensures coverage of the space the framework claims to operate in. The two are different: coverage is about whether the pilot tests the framework where it says it applies; representativeness is about whether the pilot's results generalize. The pilot aims at the first, not the second.

A.3 Raters

Number. Three raters.

Independence. At least two of the three must be independent of the framework's author.

Training. All three trained on the anchors in §9.1, and on a sample of five training cases not in the test set. The training procedure must be preregistered and described in enough detail to be reproduced.

A.4 Procedure

  1. Each rater rates all thirty cases independently, in random order.
  2. Raters do not see the others' ratings before submitting their own.
  3. Raters do not see the framework's evaluation outcomes, the author's prior ratings, or the panel ratings in Table 4.
  4. Disagreements are recorded, not averaged.
  5. Ratings are submitted before any analysis is conducted.

A.4b What the raters code

The raters code three things, and the difference matters for what alpha measures:

  1. The three drivers. For each case, each rater assigns E (entrenchment), S (severity), and C (contestation deficit) as Low, Moderate, or High, using the anchors in §9.1. This is the primary data the pilot collects.
  2. The resulting level. Each rater then applies the activation rule (§7.1) to their own E/S/C ratings and records the level (L1, L2, or L3) the rule produces. This is the secondary data.
  3. Brief reasoning. Each rater records, in no more than three sentences per case, the main reason for their ratings. This is not coded, but it is collected so that disagreements can be understood rather than merely counted.

What alpha is computed on. The primary alpha in A.5 is computed on the level (item 2), because the framework's operational claim is that raters can agree on the level a case should receive. Secondary alphas are computed on each driver separately (item 1), because agreement on the level may be high while agreement on the drivers is low (if raters reach the same level through different driver-rating paths), and that difference is diagnostically important.

What the raters do not code. The raters do not code whether the case is "just", "legitimate", or "morally acceptable". They code the level that the framework's own activation rule assigns to the case under the framework's own anchors. Whether the framework's levels correspond to anything morally important is a separate question — the question of construct validity, addressed in A.7b.

A.4c What comparisons are made

Alpha across all thirty cases is the primary measure. But three further comparisons are specified in advance, because they turn alpha from a single number into a diagnostic:

  • Alpha by driver. Computed separately for E, S, and C. If alpha is high overall but low for one driver, the anchor for that driver is the problem, not the framework as a whole.
  • Alpha by level. Computed separately for cases the framework assigns L1, L2, and L3. If alpha is high at L3 and low at L1, the anchors for low-stakes cases may be under-specified.
  • Alpha by difficulty. Computed separately for clear-cut and borderline cases. If alpha is low only on borderline cases, the framework's anchors are working as intended on the clear cases and the borderline cases need sharper criteria.

What these comparisons would show. If all three secondary alphas are comparable to the primary alpha, the framework's anchors are working uniformly. If any secondary alpha is lower, the location of the disagreement is diagnostic: it tells the framework's authors where the anchors need revision, not merely that they do.

A.5 Analysis

Primary measure. Krippendorff's alpha on the assigned level (L1, L2, L3) across all three raters, computed on all thirty cases.

Secondary measures. Krippendorff's alpha on each of the three drivers (E, S, C) separately, to identify whether disagreement is concentrated in one driver.

Decision rule.

  • If α ≥ 0.800, the framework passes T1 at the validated level, and the anchors may be treated as validated on reliability.
  • If 0.667 ≤ α < 0.800, the framework passes T1 at the tentative level, and the anchors are usable but should be revised in any area where disagreement is concentrated.
  • If 0.40 ≤ α < 0.667, the anchors are revised and the pilot is repeated with a new sample.
  • If α < 0.40 after two revisions, the diagnostic is abandoned as a decision tool.

A.6 Why the thresholds are 0.667 and 0.800

Krippendorff's own guidance is that α ≥ 0.667 supports tentative conclusions, and α ≥ 0.800 supports reliance on the results (Krippendorff 1980, 2004). The framework adopts 0.667 as the minimum for T1, because a diagnostic that cannot reach tentative reliability is not usable as a decision aid. It adopts 0.800 as the threshold for calling the diagnostic validated, because below that the anchors cannot bear the weight the framework places on them. A lower threshold (0.60) is sometimes used in exploratory research, but it is not Krippendorff's, and the framework should not appeal to his authority for it.

A.7 What the pilot would not establish

Reliability (T1) is necessary for validity (T2) but not sufficient. A pilot that passes T1 has shown that the anchors are usable; it has not shown that the anchors track what independent expert panels would judge necessary. That requires a separate study with expert panels blind to the framework's output.

Nor would the pilot establish the framework's philosophical claims. Those claims — J is necessary but not sufficient, symbiosis is a pro tanto floor, A1–A4 are conditional on F — stand or fall on their arguments, not on the pilot. A reader who accepts the philosophical claims but rejects the operational machinery has accepted the framework's argument and rejected its implementation. A reader who accepts the operational machinery but rejects the philosophical claims has done the opposite. The appendix does not collapse the two into one.

A.7b What alpha cannot show: the validity problem

Krippendorff's alpha measures agreement among raters, not truth. A pilot can pass T1 and still fail to establish that the framework's levels track anything morally important. Three validity questions must be kept distinct:

  • Construct validity. Do the framework's three drivers (E, S, C) and the resulting levels actually correspond to what they claim to measure? The framework claims that entrenchment, severity, and contestation deficit are the three features of an arrangement that determine how demanding an account it requires. High inter-rater agreement does not show that this claim is correct. It shows only that raters, using the framework's anchors, reach the same answer. A rival framework with different drivers could achieve high agreement and track the same moral facts better — or worse. Construct validity would require an independent argument, or an independent measure, that the three drivers are the right ones.
  • Content validity. Do the framework's anchors and case descriptions cover the full range of cases to which the framework claims to apply? A pilot with thirty cases can miss entire categories of cases the framework is meant to handle — for example, cases where the contestation deficit is extremely high but hard to observe, or cases where severity is contested among the affected parties themselves. A pilot with adequate content validity would require a sampling strategy that demonstrably covers the framework's stated scope conditions (§1.2).
  • Criterion validity. Do the framework's levels predict what independent experts judge necessary in the same cases? This is what T2 (§15.4) is meant to test, and it requires a separate study with expert panels blind to the framework's output. High alpha does not establish criterion validity. It only establishes that the framework is internally consistent.

Systematic bias. Alpha cannot detect systematic bias that all raters share. Three raters trained on the same anchors may all rate cases in ways that reflect the anchors' own blind spots — for example, if the anchors systematically under-rate severity in cases involving populations the raters do not know well, or over-rate contestation deficit in cases involving institutions the raters distrust. The disagreement alpha measures is between raters; it does not measure the disagreement between the raters and the world. A pilot that achieves high alpha could do so because all three raters share the same bias, and the framework has no way to detect this from the pilot alone.

What would detect systematic bias. Three things, none of which is part of T1:

  • Cases with known outcomes. Cases where an independent, post-hoc assessment has already established what the level should have been (for example, cases whose later history revealed which safeguards were necessary). If the raters agree on the wrong answer, the framework's anchors are systematically biased.
  • Raters with different backgrounds. If alpha remains high when raters from different disciplinary, cultural, or professional backgrounds are used, the agreement is less likely to reflect a shared bias. If alpha falls sharply, the anchors may be capturing only one way of reading the cases.
  • Adversarial review of the anchors themselves. Independent critics examining the anchors for systematic gaps — for instance, a critique that the severity anchor treats all moderate harms as equivalent when they are not — can identify bias that no amount of inter-rater agreement would reveal.

What this means for the framework's claim. A pilot that passes T1 would show that the framework's anchors are usable: three raters can apply them and agree on a level. It would not show that the framework's levels are right. The distinction is between reliability (do raters agree?) and validity (do raters agree on the morally important thing?). A framework that presents high alpha as evidence of philosophical correctness has confused the two. This appendix, and §15.4, should not.

A.7c What a pilot of this size can and cannot establish

The distinction that matters for interpreting the pilot is not between "good" and "bad" evidence. It is between exploratory and confirmatory evidence. The pilot specified in this appendix is exploratory:

  • Exploratory pilot. The question is whether the anchors are usable at all — whether competent raters can apply them and reach similar answers, or whether the anchors are so vague that disagreement is pervasive. A pilot with thirty cases can answer this question. If the answer is yes, the framework proceeds to a confirmatory study. If the answer is no, the anchors are revised before any further testing.
  • Confirmatory study. The question is whether the anchors are reliable across the population of cases the framework is meant to apply to. This requires a larger, more representative sample, preregistered analysis, and replication. A confirmatory study would need hundreds of cases, not thirty.

Why the distinction matters. A pilot with thirty cases that achieves α = 0.85 is not evidence that the framework is reliable for all applications. It is evidence that the anchors are usable enough to warrant a confirmatory study. A pilot with thirty cases that achieves α = 0.50 is not evidence that the framework is unreliable for all applications. It is evidence that the anchors need revision before a confirmatory study can be designed. The pilot is a diagnostic of the anchors, not a verdict on the framework.

What the number thirty is not. Thirty is not a quality guarantee, not a threshold below which a pilot is meaningless and above which it is adequate, and not a claim that a pilot of thirty cases proves anything. It is a feasible size for an exploratory pilot. The framework should not present it as more.

What would be needed for stronger evidence. A confirmatory study would require:

  • Sample. Several hundred cases, stratified as in A.2b but with larger cells.
  • Raters. More raters, from more diverse backgrounds, so that alpha can be computed across rater subgroups as well as overall.
  • Rater selection. Raters selected by an independent party, not by the framework's author, and blind to the framework's hypotheses about where agreement should be high.
  • Confirmatory analysis. Preregistered hypotheses about which strata should show the highest and lowest alpha, tested against the data.
  • Replication. The study replicated by an independent team, to rule out that any agreement reflects the framework's authors' training procedures rather than the anchors themselves.

None of these is part of the pilot. The pilot is what the framework can specify now. The confirmatory study is what would be needed to establish the framework's operational claims.

A.8 Reproducibility

The following must be made public before any analysis is conducted:

  • The full case set (all thirty cases, with their descriptions and the anchors used).
  • The training procedure and training cases.
  • The preregistered analysis plan.
  • The raters' identity to the extent consistent with their consent.
  • The raw ratings.
  • The analysis code.

A pilot that cannot be reproduced is not a pilot. It is an anecdote about what some raters happened to do.

A.9 Institutional arrangements

A pilot meeting the conditions above would require:

  • An academic partner or independent research organization to select the additional cases and to hold the preregistration.
  • Open funding, so that the pilot's funders cannot influence its design or its publication.
  • Publication of the results regardless of outcome.
  • An independent steering committee that is not appointed by the framework's author.

None of these arrangements is guaranteed. Where they cannot be met, the pilot is not a pilot in the sense §15.4 requires.

A.10 What the framework says about its own empirical status

No pilot has been run. The calibration table in §14.6 is the author's, not a panel's, and the framework should not be described as having passed T1. The framework is a diagnostic, not a validated instrument. Its empirical claims are proposed, not established. Its philosophical claims are argued, not proven.

About this text

By Jesper Blom.

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability. Its contribution is comparative and partly philosophical, and it is stated openly in §1.5 and shown in Table 1.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see Bibliography). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Eleven features of the framework deserve comment, in order of the stress-test that produced them.

First, the specific claim is stated openly and shown (§1.5). The framework does not claim a new foundational principle. It claims an interlocking operational set (activation rule, duty ladder, T5-precondition, epistemic burden principle, allocation by exercised capacity, apophatic constraint) that no single tradition supplies. Table 1 makes the claim testable by showing what each tradition supplies and what the framework operationalises, and the adjacent literatures that have close analogues.

Second, the normative location is stated openly (§2.7). The framework is internal to the thin justificatory tradition, not the thick one. It requires only three claims of decreasing thickness: thin standing, semi-thick procedural claim, framework-specific disjunction. The thin standing claim is much more widely shared than the thick version, and the framework needs only the thin one. This is a smaller claim than the framework's foundational claims sometimes suggest, and it is the claim the framework can actually defend.

Third, the framework engages its closest rivals adversarially, not only positionally (§2.8). Table 1 shows what the framework adds; §2.8 shows what Pettit, Forst and Jonas would object, and what the framework can reply. The replies are not offered as decisive; each states what would count as its failure. The realist reply in §2.4 is a placement, not a performative refutation; §15.1 (i) makes the disavowal of transcendental argument explicit.

Fourth, J is disjunctive (§2.1). Fifth, J is primarily epistemic (§2.1). Sixth, J is abductively defensible, not a transcendental deduction, and is necessary but not sufficient for a non-arbitrary relation (§2.1, §2.2b): it must be paired with the Floor and assessed against SY1–SY3. The resource-asymmetry case in §2.1 shows the stronger claim: even a genuine J-and-Floor society can be unjust.

Seventh, symbiosis is a pro tanto floor, not a complete ideal, and the choice of floor is derived and defended (§5.3). The framework does not claim that a relation meeting SY1–SY3 is therefore good. It claims that a relation failing them is not good. The derivation from the thin standing claim shows that SY1–SY3 are the three distinct ways standing can be made vacuous. SY1–SY3 are not fully independent, but independent enough to do work that one indicator could not (§5.1), and SY1 is weighted below the structural indicators (iii) and (iv) in §15.2b because adaptive preferences can produce convergence for the wrong reason. The pro tanto formulation is the honest form of the claim: it binds those who accept the framework's premises, and does not pretend to bind those who reject them.

Eighth, care and respect are internal to the framework's premises, conditionally (§5.4, §15.1 (iii)). Responsibility without care becomes control; care without respect for the incomprehensible becomes paternalism. Both failures are caught by the framework's own operational indicators (SY1 and SY2), and both are consequences of premises the framework already holds. The conditionality: A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint are conditional on F; SY1–SY3 are derived from thin standing alone. The framework is weaker without F, and §2.0 and §15.1 (iii) say so.

Ninth, one proposed contribution is the epistemic burden principle (§5.2). The principle argues that asymmetric influence can shape both the decision and the evidence used to assess it. The proposed response is a proportionate burden to seek independent scrutiny and meaningful correction from affected parties or legitimate representatives. It is not claimed as a necessary condition of knowledge, nor as a gap that the major traditions have simply overlooked. Its value depends on whether it improves the allocation and design of answerability in concrete cases. The argument has five defeaters (B1–B5) and remains open to counterexample.

Tenth, the framework is a diagnostic, not a procedure (§15.3), with four specified falsifying outcomes. The framework acknowledges three structural limits it cannot close (§15.2): Harmonious Domination, Non-formulable harm, and Unreadable dominance. Each is a case where a procedure can be followed completely and something valuable is still destroyed. The framework cannot prevent this. What it can do is name it in advance, and specify what would count as refutation rather than application.

Eleventh, and most importantly, the apophatic constraint (§15.2b) answers the hardest question the framework faces: can a superior intelligence preserve a being's existence, freedom and development without understanding what the being is — and without making its own understanding the measure? The honest answer is that it cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure. What it can do is preserve the structural conditions under which the imposition is nameable, revisable and resistible. Four conditions follow: A1–A4. Each has an operational test, resistance is identifiable by four indicators, and the heads-I-win problem is broken by structural confirmation — sources (iii) and (iv) in §15.2b carry more confirmatory weight than SY1. The strong claim about imposition is softened: imposition cannot be certified away, but it can be made thin, revisable and correctable. A1–A4 are not self-enforcing: five named failure modes specify how they can be satisfied in form and violated in substance. And in the Limit 3 case — where no party outside the superior can verify compliance — A1–A4 cannot be enforced, though they remain obligations; §15.2b states this as a limit, not a solution. These are not a procedure. They are what modesty looks like when it is operationalised. They do not solve the problem of domination. They keep it open, where it can be kept open.

The framework's methodological constraint (§2.2b, §15.4) remains: it states, in advance, four defeaters for the fallibilist premise (§2.0), four defeaters for J, five defeaters for the epistemic burden principle, four defeaters for §5.4's claim that care and respect are internal, four defeaters for the apophatic constraint plus its five failure modes, and five proposed evaluation conditions for its activation rule, and it requires preregistration for the tests. The tests are proposed, not performed. The consolidated list is in §15.6, and the operational form of every condition — what would count against it, how strong the evidence would need to be, and what would be revised — is in §15.7 (Table 8). The three-level distinction in §15.7 determines what kind of revision each condition would require. A framework that cannot be replaced cannot be corrected — see §15.5.

What has not been done. No pilot has been run. The ratings in Table 4 are the author's, not a panel's. The framework's empirical claims are unconfirmed, and the framework should not be described as having passed T1. The pilot protocol that would provide evidence relevant to T1 is specified in Appendix A. The consolidated list of what the framework does not claim is in §15.6.

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with.
