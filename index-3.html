The Responsibility of Existence

A framework for answerability under asymmetric influence — grounded in a thin, defensible justificatory core, with stated failure conditions for its activation rule, and with one positive philosophical claim: that under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability is a necessary epistemic precondition for symbiosis. It handles domination; it does not solve it.

Abstract

In one sentence: when you exercise discretionary influence over the conditions under which others live, choose and develop, and when they cannot exit or contest that influence effectively, you owe them an account of it — and you cannot be the sole judge of whether that account is good enough.

What this essay is, and what it is not. It is a framework for deciding when influence creates a duty to answer, how demanding that duty is, who must discharge it, and how it can be discharged without turning into new domination. It is not a new foundational principle in the tradition of Pettit, Forst or Jonas. Its core principle (J) is a defensible synthesis of existing traditions, not a demonstration of necessity. Its contribution is partly operational — the activation rule, the duty ladder, the rating protocol, the demonstration of ECP's advantage over Young in a decisive case, the measurable indicators of symbiosis, and the conversion of the reviewer regress from diagnosis to design — and partly philosophical: the symbiosis-answerability thesis (§5.2), which holds that under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability is a necessary epistemic precondition for the more powerful party to know whether the relation is symbiotic.

1. Ground. J is a thin, disjunctive principle: arrangements that systematically constrain others' conditions must provide at least one of meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation. Which is required depends on the structure of the constraint — where exit and review work, justification need not be owed; where they fail, it is. J is primarily an epistemic principle: it protects against the actor confusing their own view with the truth. It is defensible, not necessary. The framework handles domination; it does not solve it.

2. Standing and symbiosis. Who is owed an account, including beings that cannot speak for themselves, handled through four tiers of standing under uncertainty with an explicit plausibility threshold (§4). Symbiosis is specified with observable indicators, so that the framework's ideal is as contestable as its procedure (§5.1). Collective contestation may represent silent parties, but it must not undermine individual contestation (§4.6). The symbiosis-answerability thesis (§5.2) is the framework's positive contribution: under conditions of asymmetric influence, the more powerful party's belief about whether S1–S3 hold is structurally shaped by the asymmetry itself, so that without a procedure through which the less powerful party can correct that belief, the more powerful party cannot know whether the relation is symbiotic.

3. Activation. One rule: a discretion gate plus three drivers (entrenchment, severity, contestation deficit) that determine one of three levels of duty, with anchors and a rating protocol that make the judgments visible and contestable (§6–9).

4. Structure. A floor of minimum conditions, the prohibition on being sole judge of one's own influence, the reviewer regress treated as a design problem, and accounts of conflict, future generations and learning (§10–13).

5. Application and testing. Worked cases including one that appears to refute the framework, five falsification conditions with independent measures and provisional thresholds for the activation rule, and modular conditions for replacement (§14–15).

Answerability is the procedure. Symbiosis is the ideal it serves. Relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation — that is what the framework aims at. The relation between procedure and ideal is stronger than this image suggests: §5.2 argues that under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability is not just the road to symbiosis, but the necessary epistemic condition for knowing whether symbiosis has been achieved.

The framework has not been empirically tested. Its activation rule is specified so that it can be (§15.2); its other parts are argued and offered for contestation.

Part I

Problem, Scope and Ground

1.The Problem, Scope and Terms

The capacity to shape other people's conditions has grown faster than the practices for answering for it.

Recommendation systems, credit scoring, clinical triage tools and AI assistants shape the conditions of millions. So do states writing policy and companies setting terms, and, at a different scale, clinicians, teachers, landlords and parents. Existing moral vocabulary covers parts of this territory: blame for wrongful acts, rights for protected interests, consent for voluntary exchanges, regulation for what law has already caught. What falls between them is influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act, and that nonetheless determines what others can do and become.

1.1 What the framework does and does not do

It answers four questions: when does influence create a duty to give an account; how demanding is that duty; who must discharge it; and how can review be organised so that it does not become a new instrument of domination. It does not tell anyone what to decide. It is not a decision theory, and it is not a substitute for law, rights or substantive standards of justice. It is meant to be used alongside them, as an account of what actors owe to those they affect, and to whom.

1.2 Terms

Influence: the exercise or maintenance of control over conditions under which others act, choose, develop or fare. Maintaining a structure one could alter, such as keeping a known-flawed system running, counts as influence. Mere ability to help does not (§3).

Actor: a person, organisation or institution, including those that design, deploy and maintain technical systems. Technical systems are treated as instruments, and answerability falls on the humans and organisations behind them (whether some systems may themselves be affected parties is taken up in §4).

Affected party: any being whose interests are materially affected by the influence.

Discretion: the actor has genuine alternatives at tolerable cost and a role in setting the influence, rather than merely executing a rule it had no part in making or contesting.

Answerability: a duty to give reasons for the conditions one helps create or maintain, to those affected or their representatives; to permit examination of those reasons; and to remain open to correction. It is distinct from blame, from liability and from authority. Under the symbiosis-answerability thesis (§5.2), it is also the necessary epistemic condition for the more powerful party to know whether symbiosis has been achieved.

1.3 Relation to existing work

The framework is a synthesis and an operationalisation, and its debts are large: Pettit and Lovett on domination, Forst on the right to justification, Scanlon on reasonable rejection, Young on structural responsibility, Bovens and Shoemaker on accountability and answerability, Tronto, Held and Goodin on care and vulnerability. Its contribution is comparative and partly philosophical, not foundational. What it offers is (i) a disjunctive activation rule, (ii) an allocation of duties by capacity, (iii) an explicit rating protocol, (iv) a demonstration of ECP's advantage over the social connection model (§3.3), (v) stated failure and replacement conditions (§2.2, §15), and (vi) the symbiosis-answerability thesis (§5.2), which is the framework's positive philosophical contribution. Whether the combination earns its keep is for readers to judge.

  • The framework answers four questions: when influence creates a duty to answer, how demanding the duty is, who must discharge it, and how review can be organised without becoming new domination.
  • It is meant to be used alongside law, rights and substantive standards of justice — not as a substitute for them.
  • Its contribution is comparative and partly philosophical: it offers a thesis — the symbiosis-answerability thesis — that none of Pettit, Forst, Young or care ethics alone can formulate.

2.The Ground and Its Support

The framework needs a thin, defensible principle, a stated set of failure conditions for its central instrument, and several directions from which the principle can be reached.

2.1 The core principle (J)

Any arrangement that systematically and non-trivially constrains the conditions under which others act, choose or develop must, for those affected, provide at least one of three things: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification that is genuinely open to their contestation. Where exit is available at tolerable cost and review is effective, justification need not be owed. Where they are not, justification is required, and the strength of the requirement scales with how thoroughly exit and review fail.

J is deliberately thin. It does not say what is good, which outcomes are required, or who prevails when interests conflict. It says what kind of relationship must exist between those who shape conditions and those who live in them: one in which the affected are not simply subject to unaccountable power, whether through exit, review, or justification. The three alternatives are not equivalent — exit is often cheaper than review, and review is often cheaper than justification — but they share the same function: to prevent the actor from being the sole judge of its own influence.

Why disjunctive, and not a single requirement. A monistic J (justification always) is vulnerable to the consequentialist, who can point to cases where exit and review do the same work at lower cost. A monistic J (exit always) is vulnerable to the republican, who can point to cases where exit is unavailable or costly and review is the only remedy. A monistic J (review always) is vulnerable to the relational, who can point to cases where review cannot reach the particular situation of the vulnerable and justification is required. The disjunctive formulation is the only one that survives all three.

J is primarily an epistemic principle. J is not a claim that justification makes actions right. It is a claim that actors who cannot be contested — whether by exit, review or justification — are more likely to be wrong, and that the practice of contestation is the best available protection against this. The epistemic framing is the old principle that no one should be judge in their own cause, applied to influence. It is compatible with moral realism and with consequentialism, because both can accept contestation as an epistemic safeguard without accepting it as a fundamental value.

J is defensible, not necessary. The framework does not claim that J is a necessary truth, an unavoidable implication of practical reason, or a transcendental precondition of agency. It claims that J is defensible: that a critic who rejects it must either accept the consequences of unaccountable power or engage in a performative contradiction. Neither of these is a refutation. Both are reasons to take J seriously. This is a smaller claim than the earlier formulations made, and it is the claim the framework can actually defend. Its positive philosophical contribution — the symbiosis-answerability thesis — is separate and is argued in §5.2.

2.2 A methodological constraint: stating failure conditions

A framework that cannot state the conditions under which its central instrument would fail is harder to correct. This framework states them in advance.

The essay does not claim to solve the problem of how normative frameworks are assessed. It makes a narrower move: it states, in advance and in operational form, five conditions under which its central instrument — the activation rule of §6–9 — should be revised or abandoned. These conditions are given in full in §15.2. Falsifiability in the technical sense is contested in the philosophy of science, and this essay takes no position on that dispute. What is claimed is smaller: that a framework claiming applicability to real decisions is easier to correct if it states its failure conditions before the test is run.

Two limitations follow. First, the thresholds are placeholders. Second, the conditions test the activation rule, not the whole framework. The ground (§2.1, §2.3), the standing tiers (§4), the floor (§10), the review conditions (§11) and the symbiosis-answerability thesis (§5.2) are assessed by argument and by contestation, not by the tests in §15.2.

  • The framework states, in advance, five conditions under which the activation rule should be revised or abandoned (§15.2).
  • This is a methodological constraint, not a contribution to the philosophy of method.
  • The conditions apply to the activation rule, not to every part of the framework.

2.3 Why J: convergence as evidence, not proof

J is supported by four independent routes. Their convergence is evidence for J, not a proof of it. A separate and stronger argument for J — the symbiosis-answerability thesis — is given in §5.2.

The framework does not rest on a single theory. It rests on the observation that J is supported from several independent starting points.

  • The justification route. If principles governing relations among agents must be justifiable to each of them (Scanlon 1998; Forst 2012), then constraint that cannot be justified to the constrained treats them as objects of management rather than as participants.
  • The republican route. The distinctive wrong of domination is the standing capacity to interfere arbitrarily, that is, without being forced to track the interests of those affected (Pettit 1997, 2012; Lovett 2010). Contestability is the remedy.
  • The epistemic route. Actors are fallible, partial about their own influence, and prone to self-serving judgment. Requiring them to explain and to expose their reasons to challenge improves decisions by almost any standard of what is good. This route is the primary ground of the revised J (§2.1).
  • The relational route. Obligations arise within dependence. Those with power over dependents carry special responsibilities, and justification must attend to the particular situation of the vulnerable (Tronto 1993; Held 2006; Goodin 1985).

What convergence shows, and what it does not. It does not prove that J is true in a correspondence sense. It is a reason to accept J, not a demonstration that J must be accepted. Four independent routes to J make J more defensible than any one route alone would, and they raise the cost of rejecting J: a critic must reject it on all four routes, not just one.

What convergence does not show. It does not show that the four routes are fully independent. They share a history, and a critic could argue that their convergence is an artefact of that history. That is a real objection. The reply is not that the objection is wrong but that it is an empirical claim about intellectual history, and it can in principle be tested. The essay does not claim to have settled this question. The symbiosis-answerability thesis (§5.2) is a different and stronger argument: it does not depend on the convergence of traditions but on an analysis of what symbiosis is.

  • J is supported by four independent routes: justificatory, republican, epistemic and relational. The epistemic route is primary.
  • Their convergence is evidence for J, not a proof of J.
  • The symbiosis-answerability thesis (§5.2) is a different and stronger argument, independent of the convergence of traditions.

2.4 Rivals, taken in their best forms

Consequentialism. Sophisticated consequentialists can accept J as an institutional implication, especially in its disjunctive form. The real disagreement is whether J is basic or derived, and it matters where aggregate welfare seems to favour overriding contestation. There the framework sides with contestation in severe cases, for epistemic reasons, while permitting temporary override in emergencies subject to sunset and review (§12.2).

Kantianism. It shares the core but grounds standing in rational agency, which creates problems for infants, the cognitively disabled and animals. §4 handles these through tiers.

Virtue ethics. It supports the humility the framework requires, but it is weak on structural questions: who reviews whom, and by what procedure.

Justice as fairness and capabilities. Rawls and the capabilities approach (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006) supply substantive accounts of what is at stake for people. The framework is procedural and can draw on these to calibrate severity (§9). The cost is stated in §2.6.

Rights. Rights are outputs, and the framework treats them as external constraints. What it adds is an account of when and how actors must justify influence in the space that rights and regulation leave open.

Realism. The realist who denies that power owes anything to the weak cannot be refuted, only outlasted. The framework's reply is that the realist who argues for realism already relies on the practice of argument, which presupposes something like J.

2.5 Traditions that start elsewhere

Ubuntu-inspired moral theory (Metz 2007), Confucian role ethics (Ames 2011) and many Indigenous relational ontologies (Watts 2013; de la Cadena 2015; Kimmerer 2013) begin from relational personhood and obligations to kin, community and land. They tend to arrive at related demands on those who hold power over dependents, by different grounds. The framework treats them as independent support for its operative core. They also press two challenges: that the affected party may be a community rather than an individual, so that contestation and representation may be collective (§4.6, §10); and that the boundary of who counts is not settled by any one tradition (§4).

2.6 Costs

J is thin, so it underdetermines outcomes. The framework presupposes a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected; it is therefore not neutral among all comprehensive doctrines. Its aim is breadth of acceptability rather than neutrality. And because the ground is a cluster of supports rather than a proof, it is open to the objection that the cluster is shared prejudice. The reply is the modular design of §15.3: the ground can be replaced without discarding the rest, provided the replacement does the same work.

  • The core principle (J) is deliberately thin and disjunctive.
  • J is supported by four independent routes, with the epistemic route as primary. Their convergence is offered as evidence, not as proof.
  • J is not necessary. It is defensible. Its costs are stated, not concealed, and it is modular by design.

3.The Exercised-Capacity Principle

Influence triggers the duty, stakes and contestation scale it, and capacity allocates it. The framework handles domination; it does not solve it.

An actor that exercises or maintains discretionary influence over the conditions of others' lives owes them an account of it when exit and review are unavailable or ineffective. How much it owes scales with the stakes and with the affected parties' difficulty in contesting the influence (§7). The burden of establishing the mechanisms through which the account is given falls on actors in proportion to their capacity to establish them (§8.2).

Trigger: exercised or maintained influence, and the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. This separates the principle from the demanding duty of rescue (Singer 1972) and from the overdemandingness that critics press against capacity-based views (Murphy 2000).

Scale: stakes and contestation deficit.

Allocation: capacity to discharge. A front-line worker can explain her decisions. She cannot establish an independent review board.

3.1 Why not the rivals

Causal responsibility is necessary for attribution but insufficient for structural influence (Thompson 1980). Fault-based responsibility concerns blame; answerability does not presuppose fault (Shoemaker 2011). Relational responsibility is real, and the framework retains it, but relational duties alone say nothing about anonymous influence. Consent is valuable where meaningful, but under asymmetry it can be manufactured or coerced. The social connection model (Young 2011) is the closest rival, and the framework is largely compatible with it; it adds a discretionary trigger and a specification of levels. §3.3 demonstrates the advantage in a decisive case.

3.2 The cost: answerability handles power; it does not solve it

A principle that makes the powerful responsible risks being used by them as a badge of legitimacy. The framework does not claim to eliminate this risk. Three constraints apply: responsibility is not authority (§8, §16), review must meet independence conditions (§11), and the risk is tested directly in §15.

Handling, not solving. The framework's aim is not to solve the problem of power — no framework can. Its aim is to convert the problem from an unstructured condition into a set of design constraints that can be observed, contested and revised. A framework that claimed to solve domination would be either naive or dangerous. A framework that handles domination accepts that the work is never done.

What the framework can do is make capture visible and costly. §11.4 specifies the mechanism: Level 3 authorisation requires publication of R1 indicators before authorisation, and an actor that refuses the publication cannot claim Level 3 legitimacy. This does not prevent capture. It degrades the legitimacy of a captured process, which is the most a procedural framework can do.

3.3 The exercised-capacity principle against the social connection model: a decisive case

A general claim about allocation is worth little without a case in which it does work that the closest rival cannot.

The case. A recommendation algorithm on a large platform amplifies content that increases political polarisation. Who bears the duty to answer for it?

Young's answer. Everyone who participates in the structure that produces the harm shares forward-looking responsibility. The strength of this answer is its fidelity to how structural harm is actually produced. Its weakness is that it does not tell any of these parties what they owe, to whom, or by when.

ECP's answer. The trigger is exercised discretionary influence, combined with the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. The users have no discretion over the ranking code, and no meaningful exit from the platform's network effects. The advertisers have discretion over whether to fund, not over how the ranking works. The engineers and executives have discretion over both, and the users cannot exit or review the ranking they produce. So the duty attaches, at Level 3, to the design team and the executives, and at Level 2 to the regulators who could have required audit but did not.

Why this is a decisive advantage. Young's model has been criticised for exactly this: if everyone is responsible, no one is. ECP keeps the structural insight and adds the allocation that the model lacks. The test is practical: put both frameworks in front of a regulator and ask which one tells her what to demand, of whom, and by when. Young's model says "facilitate collective action". ECP says "the design team owes Level 3; the regulator owes Level 2; here are the indicators". That difference is the framework's reason to exist.

What ECP owes Young. The framework does not claim to have refuted Young. If Young's model can be given an allocation that does the same work without a discretionary trigger, the framework's advantage disappears. That is a live question, and the framework invites it.

  • Young's social connection model identifies structural harm but does not allocate responsibility.
  • ECP retains the structural insight and adds allocation by exercised discretion combined with the unavailability of exit and review.
  • The advantage is demonstrated in a concrete case, not asserted in general terms.
  • ECP does not claim to have refuted Young; it claims to have entered a contest it can lose, on grounds specified in advance.

Part II

Who Is Owed an Account

4.Standing Under Uncertainty

Incomprehensibility is not evidence of unimportance, but neither is it evidence of importance. The framework needs a threshold.

4.1 Four tiers

  • Tier A: agents. Beings with normative agency (persons).
  • Tier B: welfare subjects. Beings with interests of their own: sentient animals, and humans who lack agency, such as infants.
  • Tier C: uncertain candidates. Entities for which there is a non-negligible, evidence-based probability of Tier B status (Birch 2024).
  • Tier D: non-subjects. Entities with no credible interests of their own: stones, rivers and ecosystems as such. The framework claims no direct standing for them. They matter through the interests of beings in Tiers A–C who depend on them, through future generations (§12), and through values that people hold non-instrumentally.

4.2 The plausibility threshold

The test for Tier C is whether there is a credible, evidence-based reason to think the entity has states that matter to it: behavioural and physiological indicators of valenced experience, evolutionary or functional continuity with known subjects, or features associated with serious theories of consciousness. Without such a threshold, precaution generates reasons everywhere and guides nowhere (Sunstein 2005).

4.3 The stone, restated

The stone marks the edge of the framework's claims. For beings that meet the threshold, our failure to understand their way of being is no reason to dismiss their interests. For the stone, which does not meet it, the framework claims no standing and says so.

4.4 Artificial systems

Answerability for the influence of AI systems falls on the humans and organisations that design, deploy and maintain them. Whether some artificial systems may themselves be Tier C candidates is contested, and the framework does not answer it.

4.5 Perspectival plurality

For beings in Tiers A–C, their way of experiencing the world may be difficult or impossible for us to reach. That a perspective is inaccessible is not evidence that it is absent (Fricker 2007). This is the content of the recognition condition in §10.

4.6 Representation

Some affected parties cannot take part directly: future generations, infants, animals, communities that lack institutional voice. Representation is legitimate only if (i) representatives are chosen through a process that can itself be contested, (ii) they state the basis on which they claim to know the interests concerned, (iii) where interests are contested there is more than one representative or perspective, (iv) they can be removed, (v) they are neither the actor nor dependent on the actor for appointment or funding, and (vi) they represent uncertainty rather than substituting invented certainty. Where the represented can express preferences, those take precedence over the representative's view of their best interests.

(vii) Collective contestation must not undermine individual contestation. Where a collective procedure is used to represent affected parties, the procedure must preserve the ability of individuals within the collective to contest the collective's decisions, and to seek independent review of them where the collective's own procedures fail. A collective that silences its members is not a substitute for individual standing. It is an additional layer of possible domination. The framework does not take a position on which traditions are legitimate. It requires that no individual's standing be extinguished by the fact that a collective has spoken.

  • Four tiers of standing: agents, welfare subjects, uncertain candidates, non-subjects.
  • Tier C requires a plausibility threshold.
  • Representation of silent parties is legitimate only under six conditions.
  • New: collective contestation must not undermine individual contestation.

5.Interdependence, Difference and Non-Domination

Existence is relational. Symbiosis is the ideal that relational existence can fail to meet — and it must be measurable if the framework is to be taken seriously. §5.2 argues that the relation between symbiosis and answerability is stronger than means and end.

Living beings, institutions, technologies and ecosystems exist through relations of dependence and influence. Existence is relational: no being exists except through relations of dependence, influence and constitution — a child through care, an ecosystem through nutrient cycles, a person through language and recognition. This is not a metaphysical claim about the nature of being; it is an empirical and phenomenological one.

Some of these relations are reciprocal and enabling, others unequal, extractive or harmful. The word "symbiosis" is used here with care. In biology it covers mutualism but also parasitism, so it cannot by itself carry a normative meaning. In this essay it names a normative ideal: relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§10).

Answerability (§3) is the procedure by which this ideal is pursued. Symbiosis is the horizon. Answerability is the road. But the relation between them is stronger than this image suggests: §5.2 argues that under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability is not just the road to symbiosis, but the necessary epistemic condition for knowing whether symbiosis has been achieved.

Harmony enforced by silencing, homogenising or sacrificing the vulnerable is not symbiosis. Difference is not a defect to be corrected. Different beings may perceive reality through entirely different capacities. The ability to explain something does not mean one has understood its full significance. This is why difference should be approached with curiosity rather than automatic correction. The asymmetry also runs both ways: the more capable may misunderstand the less capable, and the less capable cannot audit the more capable. That second half is why independent capacity for review (§11) matters.

5.1 Making symbiosis measurable (or admitting it is not)

If the framework states failure conditions for its activation rule, its own ideal cannot be exempt from assessment.

Symbiosis was defined above as a normative ideal: relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation. If the framework's stated failure conditions (§2.2) are taken seriously, symbiosis cannot remain a purely rhetorical horizon.

Three indicators, each observable.

  • Non-exploitation (S1). The dependent party's continued participation does not require it to accept terms that it would reject if it had exit at tolerable cost. Measured by: the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, adjusted for exit costs. A high gap indicates exploitation; a low gap indicates that the relation is not extractive, whatever else it is. Note that the indicator is defined by the dependent party's stated preferences, not by the more powerful party's assessment of what those preferences should be.
  • Non-assimilation (S2). The dependent party's difference is not treated as a defect to be corrected. Measured by: whether the actor's interventions aim at removing the difference or at removing the burden the difference carries. A programme that seeks to make deaf children hearing is assimilation; a programme that provides sign language and accessible environments is not. The indicator is observable in the design of interventions, not in the intentions of actors. It also requires that the dependent party can identify which of their burdens are being addressed — an assessment the more powerful party cannot make on their behalf.
  • Continued possibility (S3). The dependent party retains the conditions for continued existence, development and participation. Measured by: whether F1–F5 (§10) hold over time, not just at a single decision. A relation that satisfies F1–F5 at one moment and violates them at the next is not symbiotic; it is a relation that happened to be benign once. Because S3 is temporal, it requires that the dependent party can report on whether the conditions still hold — which the more powerful party cannot assess from a single observation.

What this does, and what it does not do. It does not make symbiosis a score. It makes it contestable in the same way the activation rule is: with anchors, counter-profiles, independent raters. If a relation scores low on S1–S3 across repeated assessments, the framework says it is not symbiotic — whatever the actors claim. If a relation scores high, the framework does not say it is symbiotic; it says the observable indicators are consistent with it.

Why this matters for the framework's integrity. The framework requires actors to answer for their influence. It would be incoherent for the framework's own ideal to be exempt from that requirement. A framework whose central ideal cannot be assessed is a framework with a hole in its centre. §5.1 closes the hole. §5.2 argues that the hole cannot be closed without answerability.

  • Existence is relational.
  • Symbiosis is a normative ideal, specified through three observable indicators: non-exploitation (S1), non-assimilation (S2), continued possibility (S3).
  • Each indicator requires the dependent party's own assessment — the more powerful party cannot specify what S1–S3 require on their behalf.
  • The framework applies to its own ideal the standard it applies to actors.

5.2 The symbiosis-answerability thesis: the framework's positive contribution

Under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability is not merely a means to symbiosis. It is the necessary epistemic precondition for the more powerful party to know whether the relation is symbiotic.

The previous section argued that symbiosis and answerability are connected: symbiosis is the ideal, answerability is the procedure by which it is pursued. This is a structural relation (means to end), and it is compatible with the possibility that other means — love, tradition, empathic attunement — could serve the same end. This section argues for a stronger claim: that under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability is not merely one means among others to symbiosis, but the necessary epistemic precondition for the more powerful party to know whether symbiosis has been achieved. Call this the symbiosis-answerability thesis.

Under conditions of asymmetric influence, the more powerful party's belief about whether the three indicators of symbiosis (S1–S3, §5.1) hold is systematically shaped by the asymmetry itself. Without a procedure through which the less powerful party can correct that belief against the more powerful party's own preferences, the more powerful party cannot have knowledge — as opposed to belief — that the relation is symbiotic. Answerability is therefore a necessary epistemic precondition for symbiosis under conditions of asymmetric influence — not a means among others, but the form that the more powerful party's knowledge of the relation must take.

The argument. Five premises, one conclusion.

Premise 1: Symbiosis requires that the less powerful party's perspective actually counts. S1 requires that the dependent party does not accept terms they would reject if they had exit at tolerable cost. S2 requires that interventions remove the burden the difference carries, not the difference itself. Both require that the less powerful party's own assessment — what they would reject, what burden they carry — actually informs the relation. A relation in which the more powerful party merely believes they have taken the other's perspective into account is not sufficient. The other's perspective must actually count.

Premise 2: Under asymmetric influence, the more powerful party's belief about whether the less powerful party's perspective counts is systematically shaped by the asymmetry itself. This is the decisive move, and it is what distinguishes the strengthened thesis from a weaker "reliability" claim. The more powerful party's categories, preferences, and habits of interpretation are formed in a context where their own perspective is the default and the less powerful party's is the deviation. This is not a contingent failure of empathy, love, or virtue in particular individuals. It is a structural feature of asymmetric knowledge: the categories through which the more powerful party understands the relation are themselves constituted by the asymmetry. The more powerful party may mistake their own projection for empathy, their own preferences for the other's interests, their own comfort for the other's flourishing. They cannot see the relation from the less powerful party's side, because the asymmetry is precisely what prevents them from doing so.

Premise 3: Therefore, no procedure that relies solely on the more powerful party's own assessment can establish that the less powerful party's perspective actually counts. This includes empathy, love, tradition, virtue, and even careful reflection. These procedures can establish that the more powerful party believes the perspective counts. They cannot establish that it actually does — because the belief is precisely what the asymmetry distorts. A more empathetic person may have a better belief than a less empathetic one, but no individual's belief is guaranteed to track the less powerful party's actual perspective, because the categories of empathy are themselves shaped by the asymmetry.

Premise 4: The only procedure that can establish that the less powerful party's perspective actually counts is one through which the less powerful party can correct the more powerful party's assessment against the more powerful party's own preferences. If P3 holds — that no procedure based solely on the more powerful party's assessment can work — then the procedure must incorporate input from a source that is not generated by the more powerful party's frame. The only such source is the less powerful party's corrective response: their "no, that is not what I would reject," their "no, that intervention removes my difference rather than its burden," their "no, the conditions no longer hold."

Premise 5: Such a procedure is answerability. Answerability, in the general sense the framework uses, is precisely the set of procedures through which the less powerful party can make their perspective count against the more powerful party's — through exit, review, or contestation (the three disjuncts of J, §2.1). The mechanism by which symbiosis-knowledge is possible is the same mechanism by which the framework's activation rule (§7) is triggered.

Conclusion: Under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability is a necessary epistemic precondition for symbiosis. The conclusion is not that answerability is sufficient for symbiosis. A relation can be answerable and still exploitative, still assimilating, still foreclosing. The conclusion is not that answerability is always necessary. In relations of symmetry, or where exit is trivial, the more powerful party may have other routes to symbiosis-knowledge. The conclusion is that under conditions of asymmetric influence — the conditions that characterise the cases the framework addresses — answerability is the only procedure through which the more powerful party can know, rather than merely believe, that S1–S3 hold.

What kind of necessity this is. The thesis claims conditional and epistemic necessity, not logical, metaphysical or moral necessity. It does not claim that it is impossible for a more powerful party to know about symbiosis without answerability — only that under actual conditions of asymmetric influence, this cannot be done reliably. It does not claim that the more powerful party is morally required to know — only that if the more powerful party wants knowledge (rather than mere belief) of whether the relation is symbiotic, answerability is the condition. The conditions (asymmetric influence, the desire to know) are not artificial. They are the conditions that the framework addresses, and the conditions under which most of the cases in §14 arise.

Why this is not the convergence argument. §2.3 argued that J is supported by four independent traditions. That argument is defensive: it shows that J is hard to dismiss. The symbiosis-answerability thesis is different. It does not depend on the convergence of traditions. It depends on an analysis of what symbiosis is, and on an epistemic claim about what power does to knowledge. The convergence argument supports J from the outside. The symbiosis-answerability thesis supports J from the inside — from the structure of the ideal that J is supposed to serve. This matters because the two arguments have different weaknesses. The convergence argument is vulnerable to the charge that the four routes are not independent. The symbiosis-answerability thesis is vulnerable to the charge that it is circular. If one fails, the other may stand.

Why this is a contribution, not a synthesis. The four traditions the framework draws on each have a gap that the symbiosis-answerability thesis addresses.

Pettit has a negative ideal — the absence of domination — but no positive account of what non-dominating relations should look like. The symbiosis-answerability thesis supplies one: relations in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (S1–S3). Pettit can tell us what to avoid. The thesis can tell us what to aim at.

Forst has a procedure — justification must be available to those affected — but no substantive theory of what good relations consist in. The symbiosis-answerability thesis supplies one: the procedure is not an end in itself, but the epistemic condition for relations of the kind S1–S3 describe. Forst can tell us what we owe each other. The thesis can tell us why.

Young has a diagnosis — harm is produced by structures — but no procedure for addressing it beyond collective action. The symbiosis-answerability thesis supplies one: the activation rule (§7), the duty ladder (§8), and the review conditions (§11) are procedures for identifying and addressing the structural production of harm. Young can tell us what is wrong. The thesis can tell us what to do.

Care ethics (Tronto, Held, Goodin) has an ideal — responsiveness to vulnerability — but a weak procedure and a vulnerability to paternalism. The symbiosis-answerability thesis supplies a procedure that prevents care from becoming paternalistic: the cared-for can always contest the carer's interpretation of their needs (S1, S2). Care ethics can tell us to care. The thesis can tell us how to care without dominating.

None of these traditions has the combination of positive ideal, substantive procedure and epistemic grounding that the thesis provides. The thesis is not a synthesis of them. It is a relation between them that none of them alone can formulate, and that each of them needs.

The risks, and how the thesis handles them.

Risk 1: Circularity. If symbiosis is defined by S1–S3, and answerability is what S1–S3 require, then the thesis is tautological.

The reply: S1–S3 are specified independently of answerability. S1 measures the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, adjusted for exit costs. This is observable without invoking any procedure of answerability. S2 measures whether interventions aim at removing the burden or the difference. This is observable in the design of interventions. S3 measures whether F1–F5 hold over time. This is observable. The thesis adds a substantive claim: these three conditions can only be reliably assessed — and therefore only reliably secured — through procedures of answerability. This is not tautological. It is a claim about the epistemic conditions under which S1–S3 can be known, and therefore acted upon.

Risk 2: Overreach. The thesis claims that answerability is the only way to secure symbiosis. But there are other ways — love, empathy, tradition, mutual attunement.

The reply: The thesis is limited to conditions of asymmetric influence, and it claims necessity, not sufficiency. Under conditions of symmetry, love and empathy may be sufficient. Under conditions of asymmetry, they may work sometimes, but they cannot be relied upon, because the more powerful party's empathy and attunement are themselves shaped by the asymmetry (Premise 2). Answerability is not a substitute for love and empathy; it is the epistemic condition that makes them trustworthy when the asymmetry would otherwise distort them. This is the claim the thesis actually makes.

Risk 3: Metaphysical overreach. If symbiosis is an objective ideal, the thesis is vulnerable to metaethical critique.

The reply: The thesis is proceduralist. Symbiosis is not a metaphysical entity; it is what S1–S3 measure. S1–S3 are observable indicators, not essences. The thesis claims that answerability is the epistemic condition for knowing whether S1–S3 are met, not that answerability constitutes symbiosis metaphysically. This is compatible with the epistemic J (§2.1), and it is the framing that survives metaethical realism.

What the thesis does not claim. It does not claim that answerability is sufficient for symbiosis. It does not claim that answerability is always necessary. It does not claim that the thesis refutes the rival traditions. It claims that each of them has a gap that the thesis addresses, and that the thesis is stronger than any of them alone. It does not claim that the thesis is proven. It claims that it is defensible, and that it is the strongest positive contribution the framework can make.

Why this matters for the framework's self-understanding. In the earlier formulations of this essay, symbiosis and answerability were connected as ideal and procedure, means and end. The symbiosis-answerability thesis makes the connection stronger and more interesting. Answerability is not just a way to pursue symbiosis. It is the epistemic form that symbiosis takes when influence is asymmetric. The framework's contribution is not only that it operationalises answerability (which it does) but that it shows why answerability is the right thing to operationalise (which the earlier formulations assumed rather than argued).

  • The symbiosis-answerability thesis: under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability is a necessary epistemic precondition for the more powerful party to know whether symbiosis has been achieved.
  • The argument has five premises: (P1) symbiosis requires that the less powerful party's perspective actually count; (P2) under asymmetric influence, the more powerful party's belief about whether it does is systematically shaped by the asymmetry; (P3) therefore no procedure based solely on the more powerful party's assessment can establish that it does; (P4) the only procedure that can is one through which the less powerful party can correct the more powerful party's assessment; (P5) such a procedure is answerability.
  • The necessity is conditional (given asymmetric influence, given the desire to know) and epistemic (concerns the conditions for knowledge, not for metaphysics). It is not logical, metaphysical or moral necessity.
  • The thesis addresses gaps in Pettit (no positive ideal), Forst (no substantive theory of good relations), Young (no procedure) and care ethics (weak procedure, paternalism risk) that none of them alone can fill.
  • Defended against three charges: circularity (S1–S3 are independently specified), overreach (limited to asymmetric influence, claims necessity not sufficiency), and metaphysical overreach (proceduralist, not metaphysical).
  • The thesis does not claim that answerability is sufficient for symbiosis, or that it is always necessary.

Part III

When, and How Much

6.The Influence Profile

Five components, each assessed against observable indicators.

Table 1. The five components of the influence profile
Component Role Guiding question
Discretion (D) Gate Does the actor have genuine alternatives and a role in setting this influence?
Entrenchment (E) Driver How long do the consequences last, and how hard are they to undo?
Severity (S) Driver How much is at stake for the affected?
Contestation deficit (C) Driver How hard is it for the affected to exit, object, or even find out what is done to them? This is where the disjunctive J (§2.1) enters the operationalisation.
Foreseeability (F) Attribution What could the actor reasonably have known?

Two clarifications. First, persistence refers to the consequences, not to the act. Second, foreseeability does not drive the level. It fixes what the actor must answer for, and generates a duty of inquiry proportional to the stakes.

The five components do not discard a richer profile of influence; they reorganise it. Magnitude and Scope are subsumed within Severity. Asymmetry is subsumed within Contestation Deficit. Control is subsumed within Discretion. Persistence is subsumed within Entrenchment. Nothing is lost; the profile is made simpler by being made derivable.

  • Five components: Discretion (gate), Entrenchment, Severity, Contestation deficit (drivers), Foreseeability (attribution).
  • Contestation deficit is where the disjunctive J enters the operationalisation.
  • Foreseeability does not drive the level; it fixes what the actor must answer for and generates a duty of inquiry.

7.The Activation Rule

Discretion opens the gate. Entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit set the level.

7.1 The rule

  • No discretion: the actor does not bear the duty for this influence. It shifts to whoever set the governing rule or could have. The actor still owes escalation (§8.2).
  • Discretion present: Level 1 always applies.
  • Level 2 applies if any one driver is High, or any two are Moderate.
  • Level 3 applies if S is High and at least one of E or C is High.

Severity is judged first at the individual level. For decisions about the design of a system that acts on many people, it is also judged at the aggregate level (§9.1). Where an actor lacks the evidence to exclude a credible severe outcome, it rates S on the credible worst case.

7.2 Why these conditions

Each element can be derived from J (§2.1).

  • Discretion. Justification is owed for what an agent chooses.
  • Entrenchment. What makes mistaken or unjustified influence tolerable is the possibility of correction afterwards. Entrenchment removes that channel.
  • Severity. The strength of a complaint scales with the burden it describes (§12.1).
  • Contestation deficit. Where the affected can exit, object effectively and understand what is done to them, they supply part of the accountability themselves (Hirschman 1970). The deficit measures how much must instead be supplied by the actor and by reviewers. It is the operational measure of how thoroughly exit and review fail.

7.3 What the rule is not

It is not a score and it applies no weights. It is not a verdict either. It yields a presumptive level that can be overridden only with stated reasons (§9.3). It is a decision aid with its joints exposed.

  • Discretion is the gate; E, S and C set the level: L1 always with discretion; L2 if any driver is High or two are Moderate; L3 if S is High and at least one of E or C is High.
  • Each condition is derivable from J, not stipulated.
  • The rule is not a score and not a verdict.

8.The Duty Ladder and Who Bears It

Three levels, each containing the one below. Duties are allocated by capacity, not heaped on whoever is nearest.

8.1 The levels

Level 1: Explain. The actor can state, on request and in proportion to the matter, the purpose of the action, the interests considered, the alternatives considered and the known uncertainties.

Level 2: Review and respond. Level 1, plus: an accessible channel for objection; access to the relevant information; a reasoned response within a reasonable time; revision where warranted; a record of decisions and reasons; a reviewer other than the original decider where feasible; and evidence that review is not symbolic.

Level 3: Preserve, examine, authorise. Level 2, plus: preserved records and version history; independent examination meeting the conditions of §11; a written assessment of less harmful alternatives; authorisation by a body outside the actor for severe or irreversible influence; staged implementation, reversibility by design or a sunset clause; remedy for those harmed; and post-implementation review. Level 3 authorisation requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation (§11.4).

8.2 Allocation by capacity

The level attaches to the influence, that is, to the arrangement. Duties are then allocated.

Those with the capacity to establish mechanisms (institutions, system owners, funders, regulators) bear the duty to establish Level 2 and Level 3 mechanisms. Others along the chain owe their own Level 1 explanation, honest participation, escalation of concerns and non-obstruction. No scapegoating: a front-line actor is not answerable for structural deficits she cannot cure, though she remains answerable for her own discretion.

8.3 Not authority

A higher level means stronger constraints on power, not greater authority. Level 3 does not make the most powerful actor the arbiter of its own case.

  • Three levels: L1 explain, L2 review and respond, L3 preserve, examine and authorise.
  • Level 3 requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation.
  • The level attaches to the arrangement; duties are allocated by capacity. No scapegoating.
  • Higher level means stronger constraints, not greater authority.

9.Rating Protocol: Anchors, Hygiene and Sensitivity

Judgment cannot be eliminated. It can be made visible, checkable and contestable.

9.1 Anchors

Table 2. Anchors for the three drivers
Driver Low Moderate High
Entrenchment Consequences brief, or easily undone at trivial cost; no lock-in Consequences last months to years, or undoing them takes substantial cost; some lock-in Effectively permanent or intergenerational; undoing is impossible or prohibitive
Severity Convenience, preference or easily substitutable goods Significant interests (income, opportunity, non-life-threatening health, important relationships), remediable Life, health, basic needs, basic agency, development
Contestation deficit Affected can exit at tolerable cost, know what is done, and have voice channels that lead to correction One of exit, information or effective voice meaningfully impaired Two or more impaired, or no exit and no effective voice

Severity is rated on the most burdened individual first. Breadth does not raise individual severity. For decisions about system design, aggregate severity is rated on population-level effects. Capabilities-style accounts of what is at stake (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006) can be used to calibrate these ratings.

9.2 Hygiene

  1. Rate before looking at the level the rule produces and before deciding what the actor wants to do.
  2. Use at least two raters, one independent of the actor where Level 2 or above is in question.
  3. Record disagreements instead of averaging them.
  4. Sensitivity test. Move each rating one step up and down. If the level changes, the case is borderline.
  5. Calibration. Compare the profile with the reference cases in §14.

9.3 Reasoned override

The presumptive level may be overridden up or down only with written reasons. Overrides are recorded and, where safe, published.

9.4 Counter-profiles

Any affected party or representative may file an alternative profile, and the actor must respond to the specific differences.

  • Anchors are observable indicators, not scores.
  • Hygiene: rate before knowing the outcome; two raters; record disagreements; run the sensitivity test; calibrate against reference cases.
  • Overrides require written reasons.
  • Counter-profiles keep the framework's own categories contestable.

Part IV

Structure: Floor, Review, Power

10.The Floor: Minimum Conditions in Relationships of Dependence

Continued existence is not enough. A being can stay alive or operational while losing any say over its own circumstances.

  • F1 — Exit or voice. Where possible, a meaningful opportunity to withdraw, refuse or communicate disagreement.
  • F2 — Contestation. Decisions are open to challenge regardless of the intelligence, authority or technical capability of the decider. Contestation may be collective, subject to §4.6 (vii).
  • F3 — Development. Opportunities for learning, adaptation and growth are preserved.
  • F4 — Non-foreclosure. The future possibilities of others are not unnecessarily closed.
  • F5 — Recognition. Interests and possible experiences are acknowledged rather than dismissed because they are hard to understand (Fricker 2007).

F1 and F2 are the mirror image of the contestation deficit in §7: where they fail, C rises.

  • Five minimum conditions (F1–F5): exit or voice, contestation, development, non-foreclosure, recognition.
  • The Floor applies independently of the activation rule.
  • Protection is not captivity.

11.No Sole Judge: Review, Power and the Regress

No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence. The reviewer regress is a design problem, bounded by finality without infallibility — and by a precondition that converts diagnosis into design.

11.1 The prohibition

An actor exercising substantial influence must remain answerable to forms of review that are meaningfully independent of its own interests and decisions.

This does not assume that the powerful are malicious. Actors are prone to self-serving judgment and are limited in what they can see (§2.3), and review that is not independent of the reviewed has little claim to legitimacy.

11.2 The regress

If every reviewer needs a reviewer, accountability looks like an endless hierarchy. The response has three parts.

  • Finality without infallibility. Working systems end the regress with decisions that are final for practical purposes but can be reopened on new evidence, on new standing, on demonstrated procedural failure, or at a sunset.
  • Contestation is not adjudication. The reviewed and the affected may challenge a reviewer's independence or competence before a differently constituted body.
  • Plurality and publicity instead of a top. Several reviewers with different standpoints, publishing their reasons, make decentralised scrutiny possible (cf. Ostrom 1990).

11.3 Design conditions for review

  • R1 Independent appointment, funding and removal.
  • R2 Plurality.
  • R3 Publicity.
  • R4 Contestable reviewers.
  • R5 Effectiveness.
  • R6 Contestable rules.

Capture indicators: reviewers appointed or funded by the reviewed; a record of never dissenting; staff moving between reviewer and reviewed; reviewers denied access; chronic delay.

11.4 Power over the framework itself: from diagnosis to design

The framework's earlier formulation treated the power regress as a limit it could not eliminate. That was too weak. It has an internal resource against capture, and the resource is T5.

Who defined the categories? Who selected the reviewers? Who can dismiss them? Whose interests are excluded even where formally represented? These four questions are asked in every application (§14, step 6). The deeper problem remains: powerful actors will shape how the framework is applied to them. No framework can eliminate this. But a framework can convert the problem from a concession into a design constraint.

The revised requirement. T5 must be a precondition, not a diagnosis. Level 3 authorisation (§8.1) requires, as a condition of validity, that the authorising body publishes its R1 indicators before authorisation: who appointed its members, who funds it, how members can be removed, and the rate at which its findings have been reversed or ignored. An authorisation issued by a body that cannot or will not publish these indicators is not a Level 3 authorisation. It is a Level 2 authorisation with a Level 3 label.

Why this changes the framework's status. It does not eliminate the power regress — no framework can. But it converts the regress from a concession into a design constraint. An actor that wants Level 3 legitimacy must accept the conditions that make Level 3 legitimacy meaningful. If it refuses, the framework does not collapse; it downgrades the actor's claim. That is a resource Young, Forst and Pettit do not have: their frameworks name the problem of power but cannot, from within, penalise its exercise. This framework can.

What this does not do. It does not solve the problem of who appoints the appointer. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. The framework cannot prevent this. What it can do is make the capture visible. Visibility is not prevention, but it is the precondition of contestation. This is the sense in which the framework handles domination rather than solves it.

  • No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence.
  • The regress is treated as a design problem: finality without infallibility, contestation not adjudication, plurality and publicity instead of a top.
  • Review must meet six conditions (R1–R6). Capture indicators are named.
  • T5 is a precondition of Level 3 authorisation.
  • The framework handles domination; it does not solve it.

12.Conflicts, Ecology and Future Generations

Decisions must be justified, not derived.

12.1 Weighing conflicting interests

Neither majority interest nor individual preference automatically prevails. The method is individual comparison: compare the burdens that each alternative places on each affected party, party by party, and favour the alternative that avoids the most serious individual burden. Claims of comparable weight may be aggregated as a tie-breaker (Scanlon 1998). Among the acceptable alternatives, choose the one with minimum necessary harm.

12.2 Emergencies

Where an immediate, serious threat makes the full procedure impossible, an actor may act first, provided it acts only as far as necessary, records its reasons at the time, is bound by an automatic sunset, and submits to review afterwards. Emergencies create no standing exemptions.

12.3 Ecological integrity

Ecosystems cannot be assessed through the preferences of individual participants. Protecting them may require restricting activities, and the justification runs through dependent beings in Tiers A–C, through future generations, and through the non-instrumental values that people hold (§4.1).

12.4 No automatic hierarchy

Intelligence, productivity, population size and technical sophistication do not by themselves determine moral priority. Weight follows the nature and strength of the interests at stake, not a being's rank on an imagined scale of worth.

12.5 Future generations

The duty is condition-regarding: to preserve the conditions under which future people can exercise agency and meet basic needs, and a reasonably wide space of valuable options, rather than to fix the choices they will make. Because it concerns conditions and not particular individuals, it does not depend on who will exist (cf. the non-identity problem, Parfit 1984). Option preservation is not absolute. It operates as a weight, strongest where actions are irreversible (cf. Jonas 1984).

  • Individual comparison first; aggregation only as tie-breaker.
  • Emergencies create no standing exemptions: sunset, record, review.
  • Ecological integrity is protected through dependent beings, future generations and held values.
  • Future generations: preserve conditions and options, not particular choices.

13.Learning, Correction and Reversibility

No actor, institution or intelligence should assume its understanding is complete.

The duty to learn. Where capacity exists and consequences are significant, actors monitor outcomes, investigate unexpected results, listen to criticism and update.

Reversibility by design. Consequential decisions should be built to be revisable: staged implementation, monitoring, independent evaluation, defined conditions for reconsideration, and sunset clauses.

Self-application. The framework is itself an influence on those who adopt it and on those affected by their adoption. It is subject to its own principles. Versions are dated and public, criticism is invited, counter-profiles apply to its own application, and §15 states how it can be tested and replaced.

  • The duty to learn is a duty where capacity exists and consequences are significant.
  • Reversibility by design reduces the risk that incomplete understanding becomes permanent.
  • The framework is subject to its own principles.

Part V

Application and Testing

14.Application: A Nine-Step Procedure and Worked Cases

The same procedure yields very different obligations for different cases. A framework that gave every case the same answer would be useless.

14.1 The procedure

  1. Identify the relationship. Who is affected, in which tier (§4), and what dependencies exist?
  2. Identify actors and discretion.
  3. Rate E, S and C against the anchors (§9.1), and F for attribution. Use two raters and record reasons.
  4. Apply the activation rule (§7) and run the sensitivity test.
  5. Check the Floor (F1–F5, §10).
  6. Analyse power (§11.4).
  7. Examine alternatives and unrepresented interests (§12.1).
  8. Allocate duties by capacity (§8.2) and set up review that meets R1–R6.
  9. Reassess on stated triggers, and invite counter-profiles.

14.2 Case: AI triage in intensive care

A hospital deploys an AI system to prioritise ICU admissions during a respiratory pandemic. Entrenchment is High, severity is High, and contestation deficit is High. Level 3.

What Level 3 requires: preserved model versions, descriptions of training data, individual scores and override records; an independent ethics board with lay and patient representation; a written comparison with clinician-only triage; authorisation from the public-health authority; a sunset clause; and a remedy for those wrongly denied.

Power analysis: the categories were set by the vendor and clinical leadership, and the ethics committee is appointed by the hospital board, which fails R1 unless changed. A hybrid in which the AI recommends and a human decides, with the decision documented, is the default preference because it keeps contestation alive.

The framework does not decide the case. It tells the hospital what it owes an answer to, and to whom.

14.2b Case: the emergency physician (a counterintuitive result)

Every other case in this essay produces the answer a careful reader would predict. This one does not.

A physician in an emergency department must decide, in under ninety seconds, whether to intubate a patient. The activation rule gives Level 3.

The counterintuitive result. Level 3 requires independent authorisation, preserved records and a written assessment of alternatives — before the act. A reader's first reaction is that this is absurd: the physician cannot convene an ethics board in ninety seconds.

The reply. The Level 3 duty does not fall on the physician in the moment. It falls on the arrangement (§8.2): on the hospital, the protocols, the audit trail, the retrospective review, and the training. The physician's own duty is Level 1 in the moment and Level 2 afterwards. The ninety-second decision is answerable, not authorised in advance. It is authorised by the structure that made it possible, and that structure is the duty-bearer of Level 3.

Why this case is included. It looks like a reductio of the framework. The reply is that the reader has misidentified the duty-bearer, not that the framework is wrong. A framework that survives its own apparent reductio is stronger than one that only survives confirmation.

  • The emergency physician case appears to refute the framework.
  • The reply: the Level 3 duty falls on the arrangement, not on the physician in the moment.
  • A framework that survives its own apparent reductio is stronger than one that only survives confirmation.

14.3 Case: a platform's recommendation system

For individual recommendations: Level 2. For the design of the system: Level 3, with preserved version history, independent audit, published aggregate metrics, and a sunset for any optimisation that cannot show population-level reversibility. "Engagement" is defined by the platform, audits are typically controlled by it, and non-users affected through the public sphere are excluded.

14.4 Case: a community health worker

She has little institutional power. The rule says the arrangement requires Level 3. But she has no capacity to build independent review. Under §8.2, the duty to establish mechanisms falls on the programme, the ministry and the funders. Her personal duties are to explain her decisions, respond to objections within her role, and escalate when protocols fail. The level belongs to the arrangement; the duties are allocated by capacity.

14.5 Four short cases

  • One-off organism release. Level 3, although the act happens once.
  • Dating platform age penalty. Level 2.
  • Café menu change. Level 1 only, and arguably not even that.
  • Physician following a protocol. No discretion over the protocol's content; the duty to justify the content moves to its designers.

15.Limits, Testing and Replacement

This section gives the operational detail behind the failure conditions stated in §2.2, and states the limits that the stress-test of J and the symbiosis-answerability thesis have exposed.

15.1 Limitations

Operational limits.

  • Ratings need judgment and the anchors will be contested.
  • Representation risks projection and paternalism (mitigated in §4.6, not removed).
  • Review can be captured (§11).
  • Small actors may be over-burdened in practice.
  • All claims about its effects are hypotheses. No test has yet been run.

Limits of the ground.

  • J underdetermines outcomes.
  • The ground is a convergence argument and not a proof (§2.3).
  • The framework was developed mainly within one intellectual tradition. §2.5 engages others, but wider critique is needed.

Limits exposed by the stress-test of J.

  • J cannot be shown to be necessary. It is a defensible position, not an unavoidable one.
  • The framework handles domination; it does not solve it.
  • J is primarily epistemic, not moral.
  • The framework's originality is comparative, not foundational.

Limits of the symbiosis-answerability thesis.

  • The thesis is limited to conditions of asymmetric influence. In relations of symmetry, or where exit is trivial, the thesis may not apply. It claims a conditional necessity, not an unconditional one.
  • The thesis is proceduralist, not metaphysical. Symbiosis is not a metaphysical entity; it is what S1–S3 measure.
  • The thesis is defensible, not proven. It may be circular if S1–S3 are not independently specified. §5.2 argues that they are, but the argument can be challenged. The thesis may also be accused of addressing gaps in Pettit, Forst, Young and care ethics that do not exist, or of addressing them less effectively than their own proponents would. These are live objections, and the essay invites them.
  • The thesis does not claim that answerability is sufficient for symbiosis, or that it is always necessary. It claims that it is a necessary epistemic precondition under conditions of asymmetric influence, for the more powerful party to know (rather than merely believe) that S1–S3 hold.

15.2 Five falsification conditions: full specification

The tests use independent measures where possible, are specified in advance, and state provisional thresholds. The thresholds are placeholders. What matters is the structure: independent measures, matched baselines, pre-specified thresholds.

  • T1 Reliability. Trained raters independently rate a calibration set of at least 30 diverse cases. If agreement on level (Krippendorff's alpha) is below 0.60, the anchors are revised. If it remains below 0.40 after two revisions, the profile is abandoned as a decision tool.
  • T2 Discriminative validity. The levels fail to track the safeguards that independent expert panels, blind to the framework's output, judge necessary (weighted kappa below 0.50 over at least 30 cases).
  • T3 Effectiveness. In matched or staggered-adoption comparisons over at least two years, adopting institutions fail to improve, relative to controls, on at least two of three independent indicators: time to correct erroneous decisions; share of challenges that lead to change; and affected parties' reported fairness and trust in an independent survey.
  • T4 Harm. Adopters show worse outcomes for the most vulnerable groups than matched controls on independently measured harm indicators. The framework is then suspended and revised.
  • T5 Capture and legitimation. Within three years, a majority of adopters' review bodies fail R1 on documented appointment, funding and removal, or adopters' rate of successful external challenges falls without improvement on the T3 indicators. As a precondition (§11.4), T5 also requires that Level 3 authorisation be preceded by publication of R1 indicators; an authorisation without such publication is not a Level 3 authorisation.

These tests face confounding and selection problems like any institutional comparison. They are better than none, and they make the framework's claims capable of being wrong.

15.3 Replacement

The framework is modular. Its ground (Part I), standing (Part II), activation (Part III) and structure (Part IV) can be replaced separately. The symbiosis-answerability thesis (§5.2) is a separate module: if a stronger positive thesis is developed, it can replace the symbiosis-answerability thesis without discarding the framework's operational parts. A framework or module F′ should replace the current one if:

  1. F′ improves on at least one independent indicator in §15.2 or resolves a limitation in §15.1.
  2. F′ causes no unacknowledged regression elsewhere.
  3. F′ is operationally applicable and produces more defensible decisions on a substantial set of cases.
  4. F′ is corrigible: it states its own falsification and replacement conditions.
  5. The transition is trialled, with a sunset or a route back.
  6. The incumbent is not privileged.

If these are met, F′ should be adopted, because §13 requires it.

  • Limitations are stated in four groups: operational, ground, stress-test-exposed, and thesis-specific.
  • The thesis-specific limits: limited to asymmetric influence; proceduralist not metaphysical; defensible not proven; does not claim sufficiency.
  • Five falsification conditions (T1–T5) with full operational specification and provisional thresholds.
  • Six replacement conditions — the framework is modular and supersedable by design.

16.Responsibility Without Sovereignty

The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination.

An actor may have greater knowledge, intelligence or capacity. None of these by itself establishes a right to determine the existence of others. Greater capability should create greater restraint. Greater understanding should create greater curiosity about what remains unknown. And greater power should create a stronger obligation to ensure that those affected are not reduced to objects in another's plans.

The principle runs in both directions. A more advanced intelligence may meet forms of life it cannot fully comprehend, and its first responsibility is not to eliminate the unfamiliar. A less advanced one should not be dismissed because it cannot comprehend a more advanced one. But differences in capacity also mean that the less capable cannot audit the more capable, which is why independent technical capacity for review is part of R2.

Answerability is not sovereignty. Understanding is not ownership. Protection is not permission to imprison. And greater intelligence is not proof of greater moral completeness.

The framework's modesty. The framework does not give anyone sovereignty over the conditions of others — it only specifies procedures through which those conditions can be contested. The symbiosis-answerability thesis (§5.2) makes the point sharper: the more powerful party cannot know whether the relation is symbiotic without the less powerful party's contestation. Answerability is not a concession the powerful make to the weak. It is the epistemic condition of the powerful knowing what they are doing. The framework handles domination. It does not solve it. Its modesty is its strength.

Conclusion

17. An Open Commitment to Existence

Be responsible for the influence you possess. Be humble before the existence you cannot fully comprehend.

We are part of something larger than ourselves. Our understanding is incomplete. Our influence is unequal. Our relationships shape conditions that extend beyond our immediate existence. Every being holds a point in reality that cannot be exchanged for anyone else's — the stone as much as the person, though the stone's point is harder for us to reach.

We cannot know everything that deserves consideration. But we can decline to treat the limits of our understanding as proof that nothing lies beyond them, and we can ask for evidence where the question is open. This does not require abandoning reason. It requires allowing reason to recognise its limits. It does not require abandoning responsibility. It requires understanding that responsibility without care can become cold control, while care without accountability can become arbitrary intervention. Both are necessary.

The stone understands nothing of us. We barely understand the stone. But between us lies a relationship that neither of us can fully encompass — and that relationship is not less real for it. The framework claims nothing on the stone's behalf. It marks the edge of what we can say about who matters, and it reminds us to hold that edge with humility.

The responsibility of existence is not the responsibility to rule existence. It is the responsibility to recognise that our actions become part of the conditions under which others must live, grow and discover their own possibilities. The more we are capable of changing those conditions, the more carefully we must consider what our influence means. The more we understand, the more honestly we must acknowledge what we do not. And the more power we possess, the more important it becomes to remember that existence is not ours alone.

To understand without ruling. To protect without imprisoning. To love without possessing. To grow without believing oneself complete. To respect what one does not yet understand. And to preserve the possibility that existence may become something greater than any single being can imagine.

In practice: when your influence shapes the conditions of others, say why, let someone else check, and keep a way back. Answerability is the procedure. Symbiosis is the horizon. Under conditions of asymmetric influence, the two are not just connected as means and end — they are constitutively linked. The more powerful party cannot know whether the relation is symbiotic without the less powerful party's contestation. The responsibility of existence is to keep both in view.

  • Responsibility grows with the capacity to shape conditions, but it must never become a justification for domination.
  • The framework does not promise harmony. It demands answerability, in the service of symbiosis.
  • Under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability and symbiosis are constitutively linked.
  • The framework handles domination. It does not solve it. This is its modesty, and its strength.
  • Understand without ruling, protect without imprisoning, grow without believing oneself complete.

Common objections

Eleven objections the framework has to survive. Each is a real objection; none is fatal — but the tenth is the hardest, and the eleventh exposes what the framework's positive thesis must defend.

1."This is a values framework dressed up as a procedure."

Partly, and the essay says so (§2.6). J carries a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected. The claim is breadth of acceptability, not neutrality. A fair test is whether people with different comprehensive views can use it to reach different answers in the same case. They can: it does not decide the ICU case.

2."Why should contestation override better outcomes?"

It does not always. Where exit and review are available and effective, justification need not be owed (§2.1). The revised J is disjunctive, not monistic. It privileges justification only where exit and review fail. The remaining disagreement is genuine, and the framework sides with contestation in severe cases for epistemic reasons, while permitting temporary override in emergencies subject to sunset and review (§12.2).

3."The capacity principle legitimises power."

Answerability is not authority (§3.2, §8.3). Higher levels mean stronger constraints. T5 looks for exactly this misuse. The risk remains and is not argued away; §11.4 converts it from a concession into a design constraint. The framework handles this risk; it does not eliminate it.

4."Who reviews the reviewers?"

The regress is bounded, not eliminated (§11): finality without infallibility, review conditions R1–R6, plural and public reasoning, and counter-profiles. It also needs a political precondition, a balance of power, that no framework can supply by itself.

5."Precaution under uncertainty is either paralysing or too weak to matter."

The credibility threshold in §4.2 answers the first: reasons arise only where there is evidence or a plausible mechanism. Against the second: in Tier C the principle does real work, requiring investigation and proportionate safeguards. The stone does not meet the threshold and is not claimed to.

6."The ratings are arbitrary or manipulable."

Judgment cannot be eliminated, but §9 exposes it: anchors with observable indicators, two raters, a sensitivity test, reasoned overrides and counter-profiles. Tests T1 and T2 examine consistency.

7."This is Western liberal philosophy in disguise."

Its development lies mostly in that tradition, and §15.1 says so. It does not claim universality for J. It claims convergence among several routes (§2.3) and engages other traditions (§2.5). That engagement shaped the text: contestation and representation may be collective, ecosystem standing is left open, and the boundary of who counts is not treated as settled.

8."It will burden the already overburdened."

The level attaches to the arrangement and duties are allocated by capacity (§8.2). The health worker case (§14.4) and the emergency physician case (§14.2b) show the mechanism. Whether burdens migrate to front-line staff is part of what tests T3 and T4 would reveal.

9."Why not just rely on rights and regulation?"

Rights and regulation are necessary and are not replaced. They leave gaps: influence by actors the law does not reach, structural harms not yet recognised as rights violations, and cases where the law lags. The framework helps decide what to demand of actors where law is silent.

10."Why must the powerful justify themselves to the affected? What if they simply refuse?"

This is the hardest objection, and the framework does not pretend to refute it. The reply has four parts. First, J cannot be shown to be necessary. A determined realist cannot be argued out of his position, because argument is the practice he rejects. This is a limit stated in §15.1. Second, the realist who argues against J is already engaged in the practice J describes. This is a performative presupposition, not a refutation. Third, the framework makes realist capture visible and costly. Fourth, and most importantly, the symbiosis-answerability thesis (§5.2) makes the point sharper: the powerful party cannot know whether the relation is symbiotic without the affected party's contestation. The refusal to justify is not just a moral failing; it is an epistemic one. The realist who refuses answerability does not gain knowledge of the relation. He loses it.

11."The symbiosis-answerability thesis is circular. It smuggles answerability into the definition of symbiosis."

This is the most serious objection to the framework's positive contribution, and the reply is in §5.2. S1–S3 are specified independently of answerability. S1 measures the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, adjusted for exit costs. S2 measures whether interventions aim at removing the burden or the difference. S3 measures whether F1–F5 hold over time. These are observable without invoking any procedure of answerability. The thesis adds a substantive claim: these three conditions can only be reliably assessed — and therefore only reliably secured — through procedures of answerability. This is not tautological. It is a claim about the epistemic conditions under which S1–S3 can be known. The thesis may be wrong — the argument can be challenged — but it is not circular. If the reader finds the reply unconvincing, that is itself a useful test of the framework.

Glossary

Answerability
The duty to give reasons for conditions one helps create or maintain, to permit examination, and to remain open to correction. Under the symbiosis-answerability thesis, the necessary epistemic condition for the more powerful party to know whether symbiosis has been achieved under conditions of asymmetric influence (§5.2).
Collective contestation
Contestation by a community or people in their own right. Legitimate only if it does not undermine individual contestation within the collective (§4.6 vii).
Contestation deficit (C)
How hard it is for affected parties to exit, object effectively or find out what is done to them. The operational measure of how thoroughly exit and review fail, and therefore of how strongly justification is required (§7).
Convergence
The support J receives from four independent traditions (§2.3). Offered as evidence for J, not as proof. Distinct from the symbiosis-answerability thesis.
Counter-profile
An alternative influence profile filed by an affected party, to which the actor must respond (§9.4).
Credible standing
Evidence-based reason to think an entity has interests of its own (§4.2).
Discretion (D)
Genuine alternatives and a role in setting the influence. The gate of the activation rule.
Entrenchment (E)
How long consequences last and how hard they are to undo.
Exercised-Capacity Principle (ECP)
Exercised influence triggers the duty when exit and review are unavailable or ineffective; stakes and contestation deficit scale it; capacity allocates it (§3). Demonstrated against Young's social connection model in §3.3.
Failure conditions (T1–T5)
Five conditions under which the activation rule should be revised or abandoned (§2.2, §15.2). A methodological constraint, not a claim to have solved the philosophy of method.
Finality without infallibility
Decisions final for practical purposes but reopenable on defined grounds (§11.2).
The Floor (F1–F5)
Minimum conditions in relationships of dependence: exit or voice, contestation, development, non-foreclosure, recognition (§10).
J
The core principle: systematic constraint of others' conditions requires at least one of meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation (§2.1). Primarily epistemic; defensible, not necessary.
Levels 1–3
Explain; review and respond; preserve, examine and authorise (§8).
Presumptive level
The level the activation rule yields, overridable only with stated reasons (§7.3, §9.3).
Relational existence
The empirical and phenomenological claim that no being exists except through relations of dependence, influence and constitution (§5).
Severity (S)
How much is at stake for the affected, rated at individual and, for system design, aggregate level.
Symbiosis
A normative ideal: relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§5). Made measurable through S1–S3 (§5.1). Under the symbiosis-answerability thesis, answerability is its necessary epistemic condition under asymmetric influence (§5.2).
Symbiosis indicators (S1–S3)
Non-exploitation, non-assimilation, continued possibility (§5.1). Specified independently of answerability.
Symbiosis-answerability thesis
The claim that under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability is a necessary epistemic precondition for the more powerful party to know whether symbiosis has been achieved (§5.2). Defended against charges of circularity, overreach and metaphysical overreach. Limited to conditions of asymmetric influence; proceduralist rather than metaphysical.
Tiers A–D
Agents; welfare subjects; uncertain candidates; non-subjects (§4.1).

  5. Making symbiosis measurable (§5.1) — are S1–S3 the right indicators?
  6. The symbiosis-answerability thesis (§5.2) — is the argument circular? Are S1–S3 really independently specified? Is the necessity claim — that answerability is a necessary epistemic precondition — defensible, or does it overreach? Is premise P2 — that power systematically distorts the powerful party's belief about whether it has taken the other's perspective into account — defensible, or does it overstate what power does to knowledge? Does the thesis really address gaps in Pettit, Forst, Young and care ethics, or does it merely restate them?
  7. The counterintuitive case (§14.2b) — is the reply convincing, or does it merely relocate the demand to a place where it cannot be tested?

Substantive objections that are not addressed will be listed in a future revision. The framework is offered as a tool, and its positive contribution is offered as a thesis that can be defeated. Its success will be measured by whether it is used, tested and replaced — and whether the symbiosis-answerability thesis survives the objections it invites.

About this text

By Jesper Blom.

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability. Its contribution is comparative and partly philosophical.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see Bibliography). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Six features of the framework deserve comment, in order of the stress-test that produced them.

First, J is disjunctive (§2.1). Second, J is primarily epistemic (§2.1). Third, J is defensible, not necessary (§2.1). Fourth, collective contestation may represent silent parties, but it must not undermine individual contestation (§4.6). Fifth, the framework handles domination; it does not solve it (§3.2, §16).

Sixth, and most importantly, the framework's positive contribution is the symbiosis-answerability thesis (§5.2). The thesis argues that under conditions of asymmetric influence, the more powerful party's belief about whether S1–S3 hold is systematically shaped by the asymmetry itself, so that without a procedure through which the less powerful party can correct that belief, the more powerful party cannot know — as opposed to merely believe — that the relation is symbiotic. Answerability is therefore a necessary epistemic precondition for symbiosis under conditions of asymmetric influence. The thesis is defended against three charges — circularity, overreach, and metaphysical overreach — and it is shown to address gaps in Pettit, Forst, Young and care ethics that none of them alone can fill. This is the framework's strongest philosophical claim, and it is what the rest of the essay is built to support.

The framework's methodological constraint (§2.2, §15.2) remains: it states, in advance, five conditions under which its activation rule should be revised or abandoned. But the constraint applies to the activation rule, not to the whole framework. This is a smaller claim than the earlier formulation made, and it is the claim the framework can actually defend.

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with. A framework that cannot be replaced cannot be corrected — see §15.3.
