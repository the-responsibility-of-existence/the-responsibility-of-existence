In three sentences

  1. When you exercise discretionary influence over others' conditions and they cannot exit or contest it, you owe them an account.
  2. The account must take at least one of three forms: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation. J is necessary but not sufficient: it must be paired with the Floor and assessed against SY1–SY3.
  3. You cannot be the sole judge of whether the account is good enough — the more powerful party bears a heightened burden to seek independent evidence and correction.

Abstract

In one sentence: when you exercise discretionary influence over the conditions under which others live, choose and develop, and when they cannot exit or contest that influence effectively, you owe them an account of it — and you cannot be the sole judge of whether that account is good enough.

What this essay is, and what it is not. It is a framework for deciding when influence creates a duty to answer, how demanding that duty is, who must discharge it, and how it can be discharged without turning into new domination. It is not a new foundational principle in the tradition of Pettit, Forst or Jonas. Its core principle (J) is a thin, disjunctive, abductively supported synthesis of existing traditions, not a demonstration of necessity. J is necessary but not sufficient: it must be paired with the Floor (§10) and assessed against SY1–SY3 (§5.1), and a relation can satisfy J genuinely and fail substantively (§2.1). Its contribution is stated openly in §1.5 and shown in Table 1: an interlocking operational set (activation rule, duty ladder, T5-precondition, epistemic burden principle, allocation by exercised capacity) that no single tradition supplies. Its normative location is stated openly in §2.7: internal to the justificatory tradition, but only to its thin version.

Universal symbiosis and normative symbiosis. The essay rests on a descriptive worldview — universal symbiosis — that existence is relationally woven: dependence, influence, interaction, competition, conflict, destruction and mutual creation are all part of the fabric. It distinguishes this sharply from normative symbiosis: the pro tanto floor specified by SY1–SY3, which says that certain ways of arranging relations are unacceptable. The second does not follow from the first (§2.0a). The descriptive claim is argued, not asserted (§5.0b). The framework states its premises openly (§2.0a), argues for them against alternatives (§2.0b), distinguishes logical consequences from normative commitments from practical recommendations, and argues rather than derives its normative core. §2.3c adds a non-instrumental argument: each party's agency is valuable in itself, not merely as a means.

1. Ground. J is a thin, disjunctive principle: arrangements that systematically constrain others' conditions must provide at least one of meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation. J is primarily an epistemic principle. J is abductively supported and has stated defeaters (§2.2b).

2. Standing and symbiosis. Who is owed an account, including beings that cannot speak for themselves, handled through four tiers of standing under uncertainty with an explicit plausibility threshold (§4). Symbiosis is specified with observable indicators (SY1–SY3) (§5.1).

3. Activation. One rule: a discretion gate plus three drivers (entrenchment, severity, contestation deficit) that determine one of three levels of duty, with anchors and a rating protocol (§6–9).

4. Structure. A floor of minimum conditions, the prohibition on being sole judge of one's own influence, the reviewer regress treated as a design problem, and accounts of conflict, future generations, ecological wholes, genuine tragedy, and learning (§10–13). §5.6 specifies where protection ends and domination begins.

5. Application and testing. Worked cases including one that appears to refute the framework (§14.2b) and one that shows the T5-precondition failing (§14.7). The empirical tests are proposed, not performed. §15.4b–§15.4f specify what the framework predicts, what it would count as confirmation, and what would make the pilot informative.

Answerability is the procedure. Normative symbiosis is the floor. The framework does not claim that a relation meeting SY1–SY3 is therefore good. It claims that a relation failing SY1–SY3 is not good, whatever else it is.

The apophatic constraint (§15.2b) is what follows from the framework's hardest question: the superior cannot certify that it has avoided imposing its own understanding, but it can preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate — provided a party outside the superior can verify compliance. In the Limit 3 case, where no such party exists, A1–A4 cannot be enforced, though they remain obligations. A1–A4 are stated as normative commitments, not logical consequences (§2.0a). §15.2c explains what Limit 3 actually shows.

The framework is a diagnostic, not a procedure (§15.3, §15.3b). The framework is testable. It has not been tested (§15.4–§15.4f, Appendix A).

The argument in brief

The line of argument. The essay moves in one direction. It starts from a limit on understanding (no actor fully understands what it shapes), adds stated normative premises, and derives a thin principle, J, about when influence must be answerable. It then specifies a floor, a rule for when duties apply, a ladder for how demanding they are, and a procedure for using all of this. Finally it states how each part could be shown wrong. Each section is either a premise, a principle, a tool, or a test, and the text says which.

The essay is long, and its spine can be stated in a page. This is that page.

  1. The worldview. Existence is relationally woven — universal symbiosis. The relations include dependence, influence, interaction, competition, conflict, destruction and mutual creation. This is a descriptive claim about the structure of reality, not an ethics (§5), and it is argued for in §5.0b, not asserted. It is heuristic, not foundational.
  2. The problem. Influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act is not adequately covered by existing moral vocabulary. This is the gap the framework addresses (§1).
  3. The principle (J). Arrangements that systematically and non-trivially constrain others' conditions must, for those affected, provide at least one of: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation (§2.1). J is necessary but not sufficient.
  4. The normative step. The move from universal symbiosis to normative symbiosis does not follow from the relational premise alone. It requires additional premises — standing, vulnerability, capacity, fallibility, contestation — and the framework states them openly (§2.0a) and argues for them against alternatives (§2.0b).
  5. Why J. J is abductively supported. Five considerations make J plausible (§2.3, §2.3b), and a non-instrumental argument grounds it more strongly (§2.3c), but they are treated as considerations, not as independent proofs. J has stated defeaters (§2.2b).
  6. The trigger. Influence creates the duty when discretion is present and exit or review is unavailable or ineffective. How much duty scales with entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit (§7–§8).
  7. The epistemic claim. Under asymmetric influence, the more powerful party has weaker grounds for treating its own assessment as decisive. It therefore bears a heightened burden to obtain independent evidence and correction. This is a defeasible claim, not a necessary condition for knowledge (§5.2).
  8. The limits. J is not sufficient. It is defensible. Its activation rule has proposed evaluation conditions; its ground and epistemic principle have stated defeaters. None of these is a proof of anything. They are invitations to argue. Its empirical claims are proposed and testable, not confirmed. §15.4b–§15.4f specify what the framework predicts, what would count as confirmation, and what would make the pilot informative.
  9. The hardest question. Can a superior intelligence preserve a being's existence, freedom and development without understanding what the being is? The framework's answer (§15.2b) is that it cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure, but it can preserve the conditions under which the imposition is nameable, revisable and resistible. Four structural conditions follow: preserve the capacity to resist (A1), do not require justification in the superior's terms (A2), do not interpret non-resistance as consent (A3), treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding (A4). In the Limit 3 case — where no party outside the superior can verify compliance — they cannot be enforced, though they remain obligations (§15.2b, §15.2c).

What it is not. The framework is not a procedure for certifying legitimacy. A relation can follow every rule and still fail to be legitimate. The framework's job is to name that possibility, not to deny it (§15.3).

If you read only one section after this page, read §7 — that is where the framework either works or does not. If you read only one passage, read §5.2. If you want to know what the framework cannot do, read §15.2, §15.2b, §15.2c and §15.6.

Part I

Problem, Scope and Ground

1. The Problem, Scope and Terms

The capacity to shape other people's conditions has grown faster than the practices for answering for it.

Recommendation systems, credit scoring, clinical triage tools and AI assistants shape the conditions of millions. So do states writing policy and companies setting terms, and, at a different scale, clinicians, teachers, landlords and parents. Existing moral vocabulary covers parts of this territory: blame for wrongful acts, rights for protected interests, consent for voluntary exchanges, regulation for what law has already caught. What falls between them is influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act, and that nonetheless determines what others can do and become.

1.1 What the framework does and does not do

It answers four questions: when does influence create a duty to give an account; how demanding is that duty; who must discharge it; and how can review be organised so that it does not become a new instrument of domination. It does not tell anyone what to decide. It is not a decision theory, and it is not a substitute for law, rights or substantive standards of justice.

1.2 Scope conditions: when the framework does not apply

The framework is offered for a specific class of cases. It does not claim to apply everywhere.

  • Symmetrical relations. Where parties have roughly equal capacity to exit, review and contest, J is trivially satisfied or not engaged.
  • Relations governed by enforceable rights and effective regulation. Where law provides effective rights, review and remedies, the framework may add little. Formal legality alone is not enough. The framework supplements legal standards where those protections fail in practice; it never displaces them.
  • Purely causal influence without discretion. The framework's trigger is exercised or maintained discretionary influence, not mere causal connection.
  • Trivial influence. The severity floor in §7 is meant to exclude it.
  • Relations where the affected party is not a candidate for standing. Stones and rivers as such do not have standing under this framework (§4.1).

1.3 Terms

Influence: the exercise or maintenance of control over conditions under which others act, choose, develop or fare. Actor: a person, organisation or institution, including those that design, deploy and maintain technical systems. Affected party: any being whose interests are materially affected by the influence. Discretion: the actor has genuine alternatives at tolerable cost and a role in setting the influence, rather than merely executing a rule it had no part in making or contesting. Answerability: a duty to give reasons for the conditions one helps create or maintain, to those affected or their representatives; to permit examination of those reasons; and to remain open to correction. It is distinct from blame, liability and authority.

Four terms the framework uses throughout. They are minimal by design — the framework does not need a full theory of any of them, but it needs enough precision to avoid equivocation:

  • Existence: the fact of being a finite, relationally constituted entity that can be affected by and can affect others. Existence is not a normative status; it is the condition under which normative questions arise.
  • Symbiosis: a relation of dependence in which the dependent party retains the conditions for continued existence, development, and participation. Symbiosis is not mutual benefit; it is the structural condition under which dependence does not become extraction.
  • Freedom: the effective capacity to act, choose, and develop within constraints one has not chosen. Freedom is not the absence of constraint; it is the presence of capacity within constraint.
  • Responsibility: the condition of being the party whose actions, omissions, or maintained structures make a difference to what happens. Responsibility is not blame; it is the structural position from which answerability arises.

These definitions are deliberately thin. They do not commit the framework to a substantive theory of existence, freedom, or responsibility. They specify what the framework means when it uses the words, so that the argument does not equivocate.

Five terms that must be kept apart. The framework uses five terms that ordinary language often conflates. Each names a different thing, and the framework's claims depend on not mixing them:

A party can bear a large answerability burden — can be required to explain, to submit to review, to justify — without being morally at fault for what happened, and without acquiring any authority over those it is answerable to. The framework's central claim is that these five things come apart, and that treating them as one thing is what produces the confusion the framework is meant to address.

1.4 Relation to existing work

The framework is a synthesis and an operationalisation, and its intellectual debts are substantial: Pettit and Lovett on domination, Forst on the right to justification, Scanlon on reasonable rejection, Young on structural responsibility, Bovens and Shoemaker on accountability and answerability, Tronto, Held and Goodin on care and vulnerability. Its contribution is comparative and operational, with a limited philosophical proposal rather than a new foundational principle.

1.5 The specific claim, in one paragraph

If the framework has a single contribution, it should be statable in a paragraph — and then shown.

The framework's contribution is not that non-domination matters (Pettit), that those subject to norms have a right to justification (Forst), that responsibility for structural harm is shared (Young), or that capability creates responsibility for the future (Jonas). All of that is in the tradition. The framework's contribution is the following claim:

The evidential burden of justification under asymmetric influence is not adequately captured by any of the existing traditions, because none of them specifies how the burden is allocated, how it is graduated by entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit, or how review mechanisms can be designed so that they degrade rather than merely consult the powerful actor's claimed legitimacy. The framework's contribution is to specify (i) the activation rule, (ii) the duty ladder, (iii) the T5-precondition, and (iv) the epistemic burden principle, as an interlocking set of operational proposals that no single tradition supplies.

Why this is not just a synthesis. A synthesis of existing positions would be a combination. The framework is not a combination. It is an interlocking set — each piece depends on the others, and the whole does work that none of the parts does.

Adjacent literatures the table does not name. Several operational components have close analogues in literatures not conventionally grouped with the four traditions in Table 1. Accountable governance (Bovens 2007; Mulgan 2003) supplies the distinction between answerability and accountability. Risk-based regulation (Black 2010; Hood, Rothstein & Baldwin 2001) supplies the graduated, risk-proportional structure that is the closest institutional analog of the duty ladder. Administrative-law proportionality (Alexy 2002; Mashaw 1983) supplies the logical form of the level distinctions. Experimentalist governance (Sabel & Zeitlin 2010) supplies the iterative review-and-correction structure that the T5-precondition formalises. The precaution / cost-benefit debate (Sunstein 2005; Wiener 2016) supplies the threshold debate that §4.2 enters.

1.6 What the title claims, and what it does not

"Responsibility" here means answerability under asymmetric influence (§1.3). It does not mean moral responsibility in general, causal responsibility or legal liability, and the essay offers no theory of those. "Existence" marks the horizon rather than the claim: the framework applies to anything that is relationally woven and can stand in the relations of §5.0a, which includes persons, institutions and, in tiers, other beings (§4). It does not claim that existence as such carries a duty. What the essay supplies is a theory of when influence creates a duty to answer, how demanding that duty is, who bears it, and how it can be discharged without becoming new domination. A reader who wants a theory of responsibility in the wider sense will find one part of it here.

2. The Ground and Its Support

The framework needs a thin, defensible principle, an abductive justification that is honest about its own weakness, and stated defeaters for the ground itself.

2.0 A motivating premise: fallibilism about understanding

F (fallibilism). No actor fully understands the conditions it shapes, or everything that depends on what it does. This is a claim about the limits of understanding, not about the cosmos and not about any ranking of beings.

B (bridge norm). Where an actor's influence falls on others who bear the cost of its errors, and who may see what the actor cannot, the actor should not be the sole judge of that influence; it should seek correction from them.

What F and B do. F alone yields no duty. A fact about limited understanding does not by itself entail a norm, which is why B is stated openly as a normative premise rather than smuggled in. Together, F and B motivate J (§2.1) and the epistemic burden principle (§5.2). They do not entail J.

Why an obligation, and not merely prudence or benevolence. Prudence would say: it is in the actor's interest to seek correction. Benevolence would say: it is kind of the actor to seek correction. B says neither. It says that the affected party has a claim on the actor — a claim grounded in the fact that the actor's errors fall on them and that they may see what the actor cannot.

What depends on F, and what does not. The motivation for J and for the epistemic burden principle draws on F and B. The exercised-capacity principle (§3) does not. The activation rule, duty ladder, rating protocol, floor and the T5 precondition are procedural and stand or fall on their own tests (§15.4). The apophatic constraint and A1–A4 (§15.2b) are conditional on F. A reader who rejects F can therefore still use the framework's procedural machinery, but that reader loses A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint. This is a real loss, not merely a loss of motivation.

2.0a What the relational premise does not do

The claim that existence is relationally woven does not by itself entail any normative claim. This section states the additional premises explicitly, and distinguishes three kinds of claim.

The claim that existence is relationally woven (universal symbiosis, §5) does not by itself entail any normative claim. From 'A exists in relation to B' nothing follows about how A should treat B, any more than from 'A is spatially near B' anything follows about how A should treat B.

The additional premises. What the essay does instead is state the additional premises that, together with the relational premise, motivate the framework's normative claims:

  • P1 (standing). Affected parties have moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful.
  • P2 (vulnerability). Affected parties can be harmed by the exercise of discretionary influence over their conditions.
  • P3 (capacity). Some actors have the capacity to exercise such influence, and some do not.
  • P4 (fallibility). No actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. (F, §2.0.)
  • P5 (contestation). Affected parties have a claim that the actor not be the sole judge of the influence when they bear the cost of its errors and may see what the actor cannot. (B, §2.0.)

The framework's normative claims — J (§2.1), the activation rule (§7), the duty ladder (§8), SY1–SY3 (§5.1) — follow from these premises together. They do not follow from any one premise alone, and they do not follow from the relational premise alone.

Three kinds of claim, kept apart.

  • Logical consequences. If P1–P5 are accepted, some conclusions follow deductively. For example: if B is accepted, then an actor that controls both the influence and the evidence used to assess it cannot close the question using only evidence it controls.
  • Normative commitments. Some claims are not deduced from the premises but are normative commitments the framework states openly: J, the floor (F1–F5), SY1–SY3, and A1–A4. These are defended by argument but they are not theorems.
  • Practical recommendations. The activation rule (§7), the duty ladder (§8), the rating protocol (§9), the T5-precondition (§11.4), the pilot protocol (Appendix A) are practical recommendations. They stand or fall on their own tests (§15.4).

Why this matters. The three-way distinction is the essay's answer to the charge that it commits an is–ought fallacy. It does not derive norms from facts. A reader who disagrees can say precisely where: at the premises (§2.7, §2.0b), at the normative commitments (§2.3, §5.3, §15.2b), or at the practical recommendations (§15.4).

2.0b Why these premises, and not others?

§2.0a states the framework's premises openly. Openness is not justification. This section argues that P1–P5 are better starting points than their main alternatives, without claiming that they are proven.

P1 (standing): affected parties have moral weight that cannot be reduced to instrumental value. The main alternatives are: (a) purely instrumental standing — affected parties count only insofar as they serve the powerful; (b) standing only for rational agents (Kant); (c) standing only for those who can express themselves. Alternative (a) is coherent but has consequences that most moral traditions reject: it licenses treating infants, the cognitively disabled, animals, and future generations as mere means. Alternative (b) has the same problem for those who lack rational agency. Alternative (c) excludes infants, non-human animals, and those who cannot speak — and is difficult to reconcile with the framework's own treatment of future generations (§12.5) and non-communicating persons (§4.6). P1 is the minimal position that avoids these consequences. Only the thin version is needed, not the thick Habermasian one (§2.7). It is not proven; it is better supported than its alternatives.

P2 (vulnerability): affected parties can be harmed by discretionary influence. The alternative is standing without vulnerability — a party has moral weight but cannot be harmed. This is difficult to sustain: a being with moral weight but no vulnerability to harm has no interests that could be violated, which collapses back into alternative (a) for P1. P2 is what makes P1 operational: it specifies what kind of harm the framework is concerned with.

P3 (capacity): some actors have the capacity to exercise discretionary influence, and some do not. The alternative is that all actors have equal capacity — which is empirically false. The framework does not need P3 to be true in every case; it needs P3 to hold for the cases it addresses (§1.2). P3 is a claim about the distribution of capacity, not about its value.

P4 (fallibility): no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. This is F (§2.0). The alternative is the claim that some actor has complete understanding — which no one has demonstrated. P4 is a defeasible empirical claim; §5.0c adds a structural argument for it from embeddedness.

P5 (contestation): affected parties have a claim that the actor not be the sole judge. This is B (§2.0). The alternative is that the actor may be the sole judge of its own influence — which is precisely the position the framework was designed to reject. P5 is not derived from P1–P4; it is a further normative commitment. §2.9 shows how much of it can be reached from P4 and a narrower premise. But it is the commitment that makes the framework's procedural machinery necessary (§5.2, §11).

What this shows, and what it does not. P1–P5 are better supported than their alternatives. They are not proven. A reader who accepts a different set, for example (a) for P1, will not be persuaded, and the framework says so. The claim is not that P1–P5 are the only defensible premises, but that they make the framework's claims coherent with the fewest unacceptable consequences.

What would refute P1–P5. If a critic can show that an alternative set of premises — say, instrumental standing plus capacity allocation — produces the same operational conclusions with fewer normative commitments, the framework's premises should be revised. If a critic can show that P1–P5 are internally inconsistent, they should be revised. Neither has been shown. But the absence of a refutation is not a proof of correctness, and §2.0a's distinction between logical consequences, normative commitments, and practical recommendations applies here: P1–P5 are normative commitments, not logical consequences.

2.0c Acting under fallibilism without paralysis

If no actor fully understands what it shapes (F), why act at all? The framework's answer is that fallibilism changes how one acts, not whether.

Not acting is also acting. Where an actor already holds influence, abstaining is a choice with consequences for the dependent. Uncertainty therefore cannot justify inaction; it can only shape the form of action.

Four rules for acting under uncertainty. (1) Prefer reversible steps where alternatives are otherwise comparable. (2) Act in proportion to the evidence for harm, and raise the burden of proof as irreversibility rises. (3) Build in a trigger for correction before acting: a named reviewer, a date, or a signal that would count as evidence of error. (4) Act, record, review. A decision that is recorded and reviewable is a better response to ignorance than a delay that no one can examine.

The test for paralysis. If the framework's demands would block every action under uncertainty, they are mis-applied. §7 (activation) and §8 (ladder) exist to scale the demand, so that low-stakes, reversible influence carries light duties.

What humility does not license. It does not license treating all views as equally good, nor deferring to whoever speaks loudest. Humility is directed at one's own judgement of one's own influence, which is why the remedy is external correction (J), not relativism.

2.0d Where the step from universal to normative symbiosis is weakest

§2.0a states that normative symbiosis does not follow from universal symbiosis. This section says where the remaining gap is, and what happens if it is not closed.

What universal symbiosis does. It is a descriptive worldview that makes the framework's concern intelligible: if existence is relationally woven, then influence over others' conditions is pervasive and unavoidable, and so the question of its justification is not optional. That is a claim about the scope of the problem, not about its answer.

The weak point. The step is carried entirely by P1–P5 (§2.0a). A critic can accept universal symbiosis and reject every normative premise, and nothing in the descriptive claim forces them back. The label "symbiosis" in normative symbiosis can also mislead, because biological symbiosis includes parasitism and exploitation. The word names the framework's floor (SY1–SY3), not a fact about nature.

What follows. (i) Universal symbiosis is a motivating description and should not be cited as a ground for the floor. (ii) The text bears this out: §5.3 derives SY1–SY3 from thin standing, as the three ways standing can be made vacuous, and §2.1c, §2.3 and §2.9 argue for J without it. Remove the descriptive claim and the normative argument stands. (iii) A reader who finds the descriptive framing unnecessary can skip the worldview material in §5 and lose scope, not validity.

2.1 The core principle (J)

Any arrangement that systematically and non-trivially constrains the conditions under which others act, choose or develop must, for those affected, provide at least one of three things: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification that is genuinely open to their contestation. Where exit is available at tolerable cost and review is effective, justification need not be owed. Where they are not, justification is required, and the strength of the requirement scales with how thoroughly exit and review fail.

J is deliberately thin. It does not say what is good, which outcomes are required, or who prevails when interests conflict. It says what kind of relationship must exist between those who shape conditions and those who live in them: one in which the affected are not simply subject to unaccountable power.

What the qualifiers require. J's three disjuncts are not satisfied by the bare existence of exit, review, or justification. Each has a minimum threshold:

  • Meaningful exit requires that the affected party can leave at tolerable cost without losing the conditions for continued existence, development, or participation (F1, F3, F4, §10). See also §B.1 for operationalisation.
  • Independent review requires that the reviewer meets R1–R6 (§11.3). See also §B.2 for operationalisation.
  • Justification genuinely open to contestation requires that dissent channels exist and have been used, that the vocabulary for objection is available, and that intermediaries not controlled by the actor exist (§15.2b, sources (iii) and (iv)). See also §B.3.

A power structure can satisfy the form of any of these and fail the substance. When it does, J is not satisfied.

When none of the three is practically possible. There are cases where exit is unavailable, review cannot reach the actor, and contestation is not open to the affected party. In such cases J is not satisfied, and the framework does not claim that it can be. What the framework can do is name the failure, identify which of the three disjuncts is missing and why, and — where possible — specify what would be required to restore one of them.

J is primarily an epistemic principle. J is not a claim that justification makes actions right. It is a claim that actors who cannot be contested are more likely to be wrong, and that contestation is the best available protection against this.

J is defensible, not necessary as a deduction. The framework does not claim that J is a necessary truth. It claims that J is abductively defensible: that it explains important recurring risks of unchecked discretion while remaining compatible with plural substantive theories of justice.

J is necessary but not sufficient for justice

The objection. A disjunctive principle can be satisfied in more than one form. Can a power structure genuinely meet J and the Floor and still be deeply unjust? Yes. The framework says so, and this section says what follows.

The case. Consider a society that genuinely satisfies J and the Floor, but the distribution of resources is such that one group, while formally free to exit and contest, cannot in practice use either. The society satisfies J and the Floor, and it is unjust. What it fails is not J or the Floor but something the framework does not measure: the material conditions under which formal freedoms are usable. §2.1b addresses this.

What the framework says. This case does not refute J; it shows what J was never meant to do. J specifies the relationship between those who shape conditions and those who live in them. It does not specify the distribution of resources, opportunities or capabilities. A society can satisfy J and the Floor and still fail on distributive justice. The framework's response is not to expand J to cover distribution — that would make it a substantive theory of justice, which it is not — but to say that J and the Floor are necessary conditions for a non-arbitrary relation, not sufficient conditions for a just one. §2.1b specifies what additional conditions make J-satisfaction adequate.

2.1b What makes J-satisfaction adequate

§2.1 states that J is necessary but not sufficient. That is an honest position, but it is an incomplete one. The question it leaves open is: what additional conditions would, together with J and the Floor, make a relation adequate? Not fully just — that is a substantive question outside the framework — but adequate in the sense that it is not merely formally correct.

Four conditions. They are not arbitrary: they correspond to the four ways J can be satisfied formally but not materially.

(a) Material capacity. Formal exit and formal voice are empty without resources, information, time, and the absence of coercion. This is specified in §5.5 (M1–M3) and is the "material threshold" that §5.1 mentions but does not operationalise.

(b) Epistemic adequacy. The affected party must be able to understand the influence to which it is subject. Where the influence is technical or opaque, the actor must provide translation, not merely disclosure. Disclosure without translation is formal compliance, not substantive. This is close to A2 in §15.2b but applies more generally.

(c) Iterated stability. J-satisfaction must hold over time, not only at a point. A one-off review that produces no continuing mechanism is not adequate. This connects to §11.2's "finality without infallibility" — the mechanism must be renewable.

(d) Non-foreclosure of future contestation. The relation must preserve the conditions under which the affected party can develop new grounds for objection. This is F4 but more demanding: not only "do not close off alternatives" but "keep open the capacity to identify new alternatives."

Together: J + Floor + (a) + (b) + (c) + (d) is adequate — not fully just, but adequate. Adequacy is a fourth-order claim: not "J guarantees justice" but "J + these four conditions is the minimum for a relation that is not merely formally correct."

This does not solve §2.1's objection. It answers it. The objection says that J can be satisfied and the relation still be unjust. The answer is that J + (a)–(d) cannot be satisfied and the relation still be adequate — even if it can still be unjust in a stronger sense. That is the difference between "adequate" and "just." The framework delivers the first, not the second.

2.1c Why asymmetric influence creates a duty: the argument in four steps

§2.0a lists premises; this section shows how they combine into an obligation, so that the duty does not rest on P5 alone.

Step 1: influence is not a neutral fact. An actor who shapes the conditions under which others act has made itself a cause of how their lives go. This is not yet a duty (§2.0a). It is the precondition for one.

Step 2: the affected cannot reciprocate the control. Where the influence is asymmetric, the affected cannot return it, and cannot simply opt out at tolerable cost. Their position is therefore one of dependence on the actor's discretion (P2, P3).

Step 3: the actor cannot be the only judge of its own claim. Two independent considerations support this. Consistency: an actor that claims standing to decide for others while denying that they may ask why claims a right it would not grant to anyone else in its own position. Correlated error: an actor's judgement of its own influence is produced by the same understanding and interests that produced the influence, so self-review reproduces the mistake (§2.9). The second consideration does not depend on P1, and it also answers the sincere paternalist who would accept unreviewed rule by someone wiser: such a ruler cannot be identified from the inside (P4). Neither consideration is a theorem. A reader who rejects both has rejected P1 and P4.

Step 4: the duty is what remains. Given steps 1–3 and P4, the actor owes the affected an answer: a way for them to register error that does not pass through the actor's own judgement (J, §2.9). The duty is answerability, not benevolence, and it scales with how much exit and review fail.

Where this stops. Step 3 carries the weight and remains a normative commitment. The four steps give two reasons why it is hard to avoid for actors who exercise influence. They do not prove that it binds an actor who rejects both P1 and P4.

2.2b What would refute J: stated defeaters

A principle that cannot be defeated is not a principle; it is a dogma.

  • D1 — The unaccountable-power hypothesis is false. If it can be shown empirically that actors with unaccountable discretion over others' conditions do not systematically produce worse outcomes — measured independently of the actors' own reports — than actors subject to exit, review or contestation, then J's epistemic justification collapses.
  • D2 — A better principle explains the same cases. If a rival principle explains all the cases J explains, does so more simply, and generates clearer practical guidance, then J should be replaced.
  • D3 — The convergence is an artefact. If it can be shown that the "routes" to J (§2.3, §2.3b, §2.3c) are not independent considerations but one tradition dressed in multiple vocabularies, then the argument for J reduces to a single route.
  • D4 — The costs exceed the benefits. If adopting J produces worse outcomes for the affected than not adopting it — measured by independent indicators — then J should be abandoned.

These defeaters are stated in advance so that the framework can be corrected. The operational form of D1–D4 is in §15.7 (Table 8).

2.3 Why J: an abductive justification

J is offered as a plausible explanation of two recurring risks of unchecked discretionary power: domination and epistemic error.

The framework does not rest on a single theory. It rests on an abductive inference: J is proposed as a compact explanation of a set of recurring risks associated with unaccountable power.

Four considerations, not four independent proofs.

  • The justificatory consideration. If principles governing relations among agents must be justifiable to each of them (Scanlon 1998; Forst 2012), then constraint that cannot be justified to the constrained treats them as objects of management rather than as participants.
  • The republican consideration. The distinctive wrong of domination is the standing capacity to interfere arbitrarily (Pettit 1997, 2012; Lovett 2010).
  • The epistemic consideration. Actors are fallible, partial about their own influence, and prone to self-serving judgment. This is the primary ground of J.
  • The relational consideration. Obligations arise within dependence (Tronto 1993; Held 2006; Goodin 1985).

2.3b The iterated-trust argument

A fifth consideration supports J, distinct from the four in §2.3. It is not a proof, but it is independent enough to count as additional support — and it is the only one of the five that is institutional rather than moral.

The argument. Where two parties interact repeatedly and unequally, the weaker party's trust is rational only if the stronger party's influence is subject to exit, review, or contestation. Why? Because in the absence of such channels, the weaker party cannot distinguish (a) a stronger party that exercises its influence well from (b) a stronger party that exercises it badly. Over repeated interaction, the weaker party will rationally discount all assurances from the stronger party, including true ones. The result is a loss for both: the stronger party loses the ability to be believed, and the weaker party loses the benefit of warranted reliance.

Why this is a J-argument and not another. J is the minimum condition under which iterated trust remains rational. A stronger party that offers exit, review, or contestation gives the weaker party a channel through which good faith can be verified. Without such a channel, trust is not a sign of reliance but of ignorance — and ignorance is not a stable foundation for cooperation.

What the argument adds. The four considerations in §2.3 are all moral or epistemic: justification, non-domination, fallibilism, relational care. The iterated-trust argument is institutional: it does not say that J is morally required, but that J is the minimum under which institutions of unequal cooperation can be sustained over time. It is a weaker claim but a different one.

What the argument does not show. It does not show that J is sufficient. It does not show that J is the only possible foundation for iterated trust — a stronger party can in principle sustain trust through perfect predictability without review. But perfect predictability is not available to fallible actors, and it is precisely fallibilism (P4) that makes review channels necessary. The argument is therefore dependent on P4, just as the derivation of P5 in §2.9 is.

Why this matters. It gives a fifth independent consideration, and it is the only one that does not presuppose a specific moral tradition. A realist who rejects the justificatory tradition (§2.7) can still accept the iterated-trust argument, because it is instrumental rather than moral. It makes J available to a wider audience than the justificatory tradition alone reaches.

2.3c The non-instrumental argument

The four considerations in §2.3 and the institutional argument in §2.3b are all, in different ways, arguments that J is useful. They show that actors who respect J will do better — epistemically, institutionally, in maintaining trust. They do not show that J is right.

There is a stronger claim available, though it is more controversial. It is this: each party's agency is valuable in itself, not merely as a means to others' ends. This value cannot be reduced to its usefulness. Therefore arrangements that destroy agency are wrong, regardless of their consequences.

This is a substantive moral claim, not a thin one. A reader who accepts it does not need the four considerations in §2.3 or the institutional argument in §2.3b; a reader who rejects it cannot be persuaded by them. What the framework says is that the claim is defensible, not that it is proven — and that the four considerations and the institutional argument are additional support for those who accept it, not substitutes for it.

Why include a controversial claim in a framework that has otherwise been careful to avoid them? Because the alternative is worse. A framework that argues only that J is useful leaves open the possibility that J should be abandoned if it ceases to be useful. A framework that argues that J is right, even when it is not useful, closes that possibility. The cost of the stronger claim is that it will not persuade readers who reject its premise. The benefit is that it says what it actually believes.

What this claim does not require. It does not require that agency be the only value, or the highest value, or that it never be traded against other values. It requires only that agency not be treated as mere means — as something whose value is exhausted by its usefulness to others. A framework can hold this without holding that agency is absolute. The claim is that agency has non-instrumental value, not that it has infinite value.

2.4 Rivals, taken in their best forms

Consequentialism. Sophisticated consequentialists can accept J as an institutional implication. Kantianism. It shares the core but grounds standing in rational agency, which creates problems for infants, the cognitively disabled and animals. §4 handles these through tiers. Virtue ethics. It supports the humility the framework requires, but it is weak on structural questions. Justice as fairness and capabilities. The capabilities approach (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006) supplies substantive accounts of what is at stake. Rights. Rights are outputs, and the framework treats them as external constraints. Realism. The realist who denies that power owes anything to the weak cannot be refuted from within the framework, because the framework is internal to the justificatory tradition (§2.7).

2.4b The power objection

A procedural framework that tells the powerful how to justify themselves may simply give them a new vocabulary for doing what they were going to do anyway.

The objection. The framework asks powerful actors to explain, to submit to review, to justify. But the powerful are precisely the actors with the resources to perform these rituals convincingly.

The reply, in three parts. First, the objection is partly correct. Proceduralism can legitimate power. That is why §11.4 treats T5 as a precondition rather than a diagnosis. Second, the objection is not specific to proceduralism. Any normative framework can be captured. The framework's claim is that T5-as-precondition makes the capture visible. Third, the framework's modesty is not an admission of failure. The framework claims to handle the problem, not to solve it.

What this does not do. It does not answer the objection fully. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. The objection is the second-hardest the framework faces, after the realist objection in objection 1.

2.5 Traditions that start elsewhere

Ubuntu-inspired moral theory (Metz 2007), Confucian role ethics (Ames 2011) and many Indigenous relational ontologies (Watts 2013; de la Cadena 2015; Kimmerer 2013) begin from relational personhood and obligations to kin, community and land. The framework treats them as additional considerations pointing toward its operative core — not as independent proofs.

2.6 Costs

J is thin, so it underdetermines outcomes. The framework presupposes a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected; it is therefore not neutral among all comprehensive doctrines. The reply is the modular design of §15.5.

2.7 The framework's normative location, stated openly

A framework that claims breadth while relying on a specific tradition is not honest about what it is.

The framework is internal to a justificatory tradition. It takes for granted that those subject to arrangements have standing to demand reasons for them. The consequence for a reader who rejects it is stated in §2.0b.

What the framework actually requires. Only the thin version of the justificatory tradition, in three claims of decreasing thickness:

  1. Thin standing. Affected parties have some moral weight that cannot be reduced to their instrumental value to the powerful. Widely shared.
  2. Semi-thick procedural claim. Arrangements that impose significant costs on affected parties must be justified to them, not merely to a third party. Justificatory-tradition-specific, but not uniquely so.
  3. Framework-specific claim. Justification can take the form of meaningful exit, independent review, or contestation — disjunctively. Specific to this framework.

Why thin standing is a reasonable starting point. Three considerations support it, which are not proofs but are more than assertions:

First, thin standing is the minimal normative position compatible with taking the affected party seriously as a source of reasons rather than as an object to be managed.

Second, thin standing is widely shared across traditions that otherwise disagree. The framework does not require agreement on why — only on that.

Third, thin standing is a claim that the affected party can press. It is not a gift the powerful give the weak; it is a claim the weak can make.

Why J follows from thin standing, given B. Given thin standing and B, J is a reasonable specification of what the claim amounts to in practice. The disjunctive form of J (§2.1) is the weakest form that satisfies this claim.

2.8 Adversarial tests: what Pettit, Forst and Jonas would object

Section 1.5 and Table 1 show what the framework adds to the four traditions it engages. §2.7 shows its normative location within one of them. Neither shows what those traditions would say against it.

Pettit: the epistemic framing undercuts the republican point

The objection. The framework's epistemic framing of J (§2.1) undercuts the republican point. Pettit is not primarily an epistemologist: his claim is that domination is a political fact, not an epistemic one.

The reply. First, the epistemic framing is an entry point, not a foundation. Because domination is invisible to the actor without contestation, contestation is a necessary safeguard. Second, the framework does not replace the republican claim with the epistemic one. It supplements it.

Forst: the thin/thick distinction is unstable

The objection. Forst's right to justification is already thin. If Forst already has the thin version, what does the framework add?

The reply. The framework adds allocation. Forst says that the affected party has a right to justification; the framework says who must give it, in what form, by when, and with what safeguards.

Jonas: the framework is too near-sighted

The objection. Jonas is concerned with the species and with the future; the framework is concerned with present affected parties.

The reply. The framework is a special case of Jonas's ethics, applied to contemporary structural influence, without Jonas's metaphysical grounding. What the framework inherits from Jonas is the capacity-based structure of responsibility and the forward-looking orientation, which §12.5 applies to future generations.

2.9 Why no sole judge: reaching P5 from a narrower premise

§2.0b states that P5 (the claim that the actor not be the sole judge of its own influence, B) is a further normative commitment and not derived from P1–P4. It is the point where the framework stipulates most. This section reduces how much has to be stipulated. It does not remove it.

A narrower premise. P5 can be reached from P4 together with one narrower commitment, the conflict-of-interest norm (CI): where an actor has a stake in how a matter is decided, and the cost of deciding it badly falls on others who cannot correct it, the actor should not be the sole judge of whether it decided well. P4 says that no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes (§2.0), and §2.3 adds that actors tend to judge their own influence in self-serving ways. With CI, P5 follows for the cases the framework addresses: discretion, a stake, and costs borne by others. CI is not left as a bare stipulation; the next paragraph derives it.

Why CI is not a bare stipulation. It rests on three steps. (i) Weight. By P1 the affected have moral weight that is not reducible to their value to the actor, so errors in how they are treated matter beyond the actor's own interests. (ii) Correlated error. An actor's judgment of its own influence is produced by the same understanding, interests and incentives that produced the influence. Where one of those is the source of a mistake, such as a blind spot, a motivated reading or an unexamined assumption, self-review reproduces the mistake, because the reviewer shares it. Self-review is therefore weakest exactly where errors are most likely to occur. This is an empirical claim, familiar from audit, peer review and clinical practice, and it is defeasible. (iii) Independence. Only a channel that does not run through the actor's own judgment can in principle detect such errors. It need not be error-free. It need only be less correlated with the actor's error than the actor's own review is, which is why §11.2 and §11.3 treat independence as a matter of degree and design. Where costs fall on others who matter (i) and self-review cannot reliably detect the errors (ii), an independent channel is required (iii). CI is this conclusion for cases in which the actor has a stake. What remains normative is (i), which is P1, the thin standing the framework already assumes and that is widely shared (§2.7). What is added is (ii), an empirical premise that can be tested and refuted.

What this explains about J. J's three disjuncts are three ways of obtaining a signal about the actor's error that does not pass through the actor's own evaluation. Exit lets the affected register failure without the actor interpreting it. Independent review brings in a party with different interests and different blind spots. Contestable justification obliges the actor to answer testimony from those who bear the consequences. That explains why J is disjunctive, since the channels are substitutes where one is available and another is not, and why it is primarily an epistemic principle. It also explains why J is necessary but not sufficient: an independent channel makes error detectable, but does not by itself ensure that error is corrected or that the outcome is just. That is the work of the Floor (§10) and of SY1–SY3 (§5.1).

Convergence. Several traditions would require some check external to the actor, for different reasons. The framework does not need them to agree on why. These are summaries of how each tradition can support the conclusion, not claims that every theorist in it accepts it.

  • Contractualism and the right to justification (Scanlon 1998; Forst 2012): principles must be justifiable to each affected party, and a sole judge cannot secure that, because justifiability is a relation to other people's reasons.
  • Republicanism (Pettit 1997, 2012): the standing capacity to decide alone and unchecked is what domination consists in.
  • Institutional consequentialism: where actors are fallible and biased, a rule with no external check performs worse in expectation than one with a check, so institutions should add checks.
  • The capabilities approach (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006): an actor who alone judges the conditions of others' capabilities can erode them without having to answer for it, which makes contestation a protection of agency.
  • Care ethics (Tronto 1993; Held 2006): care requires responsiveness to the one cared for, and a sole judge has no channel through which that response arrives.

Where the convergence stops. Three cases weaken or defeat the derivation.

  • The wise and benevolent decider. Some perfectionist views hold that a sufficiently wise actor should decide alone. The framework's reply is P4 again: such an actor cannot be identified from the inside. A reader who denies P4 for some actor will not accept P5 for that actor, and the framework says so.
  • Epistemic authority. Where the reviewer knows less than the actor, as in clinical or technical settings, external review can lower quality. This is the regress problem of §11.2. The framework does not claim that review is always better, only that the actor should not be the sole judge. Independent review can take the form of process audits, expert panels or testimony from the affected.
  • Small or easily reversed matters. CI's clause about costly errors makes P5 weak here, in line with the way J's requirement scales with the severity and entrenchment of the constraint (§2.1).

What would refute this derivation. Two kinds of evidence. First, evidence that for some class of actors with a stake and with high costs to others, self-review detects the relevant errors as well as independent review does, which would show that (ii) fails for that class. This could be tested by comparing the error-detection rates of self-review and independent review on matched cases, in the manner of the tests in §15.4. This is a proposal. No such test has been run. Second, evidence that every available independent channel is more strongly correlated with the actor's error than the actor's own review is, which is the capture problem of §11.4. That is an argument for better design of channels, and for plurality among them, not for dropping them. Neither piece of evidence has been shown.

Result. P5 is not proven, and the derivation does not remove every stipulation. It reaches P5 from P1 (thin standing), P4 (fallibility) and an empirical claim about correlated error, with P4 itself supported by the embeddedness argument of §5.0c. What remains normative is the thin standing the framework already needs, and what remains contestable is an empirical premise that can in principle be tested. The convergence above is additional support. It is not what the derivation rests on.

  • Positioning ("what we add") is not engagement ("what they would object").
  • Pettit would say the epistemic framing undercuts the republican point. The framework replies: the epistemic framing is an entry point, not a foundation.

    • 3. The Exercised-Capacity Principle

    Influence triggers the duty, stakes and contestation scale it, and capacity allocates it.

    An actor that exercises or maintains discretionary influence over the conditions of others' lives owes them an account of it when exit and review are unavailable or ineffective. How much it owes scales with the stakes and with the affected parties' difficulty in contesting the influence (§7). The burden of establishing the mechanisms through which the account is given falls on actors in proportion to their capacity to establish them (§8.2).

    Trigger: exercised or maintained influence, and the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. Scale: stakes and contestation deficit. Allocation: capacity to discharge.

    3.1 Why not the rivals

    Causal responsibility is necessary for attribution but insufficient for structural influence (Thompson 1980). Fault-based responsibility concerns blame; answerability does not presuppose fault (Shoemaker 2011). Relational responsibility is real, but relational duties alone say nothing about anonymous influence. Consent is valuable where meaningful, but under asymmetry it can be manufactured or coerced. The social connection model (Young 2011) is the closest rival.

    3.2 The cost: answerability handles power; it does not solve it

    A principle that makes the powerful responsible risks being used by them as a badge of legitimacy. The framework does not claim to eliminate this risk. Three constraints apply: responsibility is not authority (§1.3, Table 1a; §8.3), review must meet independence conditions (§11), and the risk is tested directly in §15.

    Handling, not solving. The framework's aim is not to solve the problem of power — no framework can. Its aim is to convert the problem from an unstructured condition into a set of design constraints that can be observed, contested and revised.

    3.3 The exercised-capacity principle alongside the social connection model: an illustrative case

    A general claim about allocation is worth little without a case in which it does work that the closest rival cannot.

    The case. A recommendation algorithm on a large platform amplifies content that increases political polarisation. Who bears the duty to answer for it?

    Young's answer. Everyone who participates in the structure that produces the harm shares forward-looking responsibility.

    ECP's answer. The trigger is exercised discretionary influence, combined with the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. Users have no discretion over the ranking code, and no meaningful exit from the platform's network effects. The engineers and executives have discretion over both, and users cannot exit or review the ranking they produce. So the duty attaches, at Level 3, to the design team and the executives, and at Level 2 to the regulators who could have required audit but did not.

    What ECP may add in this case. Young's model has been criticised for exactly this: if everyone is responsible, no one is. ECP keeps the structural insight and adds the allocation that the model lacks.

    • Young's social connection model identifies shared responsibility; ECP proposes a more granular allocation.
    • The advantage is demonstrated in a concrete case.
    • ECP does not claim to have refuted Young; it claims to have entered a contest it can lose.

    3.4 Distributed influence and delegated discretion

    A principle that only handles face-to-face influence is not a principle for the conditions most people actually live under.

    Five modes of contribution.

    • Causing. The party's action produces the effect directly.
    • Enabling. The party creates the conditions under which another party can produce the effect.
    • Controlling. The party retains the ability to stop, redirect, or authorise the effect, even if it does not itself act.
    • Foreseeing. The party knows or could reasonably know that the effect will occur.
    • Benefiting. The party gains from the effect without contributing to it.

    How ECP allocates. Causing and controlling are sufficient for the trigger. Enabling is sufficient only where the enabler retains the ability to withdraw the enabling condition at tolerable cost. Foreseeing is not sufficient on its own, but it raises the level. Benefiting is not sufficient on its own, but it generates a duty of inquiry.

    The delegation question. Three conditions determine whether delegation succeeds or fails:

    1. The delegator retains the ability to revoke.
    2. The delegator sets the frame.
    3. The delegation is not a genuine transfer of discretion.

    The problem of diffusion. In complex systems, responsibility can diffuse until no party is answerable. The framework's response is not to insist that some party must be answerable. It is to insist that the diffusion be located and named. Where no party has the capacity to discharge the duty, the framework says so (§14.5e).

    When responsibility changes over time. A party's responsibility is not fixed at the moment of action. It changes as the party's capacity and knowledge change.

    A worked case: the supply chain. A garment brand sources from a factory that sources from a subcontractor that employs home workers. Who bears the ECP duty?

    • The home workers exercise no discretionary influence. No duty.
    • The subcontractor exercises discretion over hours and pay, but under constraints set by the factory. Level 1 duty; Level 2 if the factory's constraints are such that the subcontractor cannot refuse them at tolerable cost.
    • The factory exercises discretion over which subcontractor it uses and what terms it sets. Level 2 duty; Level 3 if the terms are so constrained by the brand that the factory cannot refuse.
    • The brand sets the frame: prices, deadlines, audit criteria. By the three conditions above, delegation to the factory is formal only. Level 3 duty.

    What this does not do. It does not solve the collective action problem. Where no party can revoke, set the frame, or restore discretion, ECP does not generate a determinate allocation.

    Part II

    Who Is Owed an Account

    4. Standing Under Uncertainty

    Incomprehensibility is not evidence of unimportance, but neither is it evidence of importance.

    4.1 Four tiers

    • Tier A: agents. Beings with normative agency (persons).
    • Tier B: welfare subjects. Beings with interests of their own: sentient animals, and humans who lack agency.
    • Tier C: uncertain candidates. Entities for which there is a non-negligible, evidence-based probability of Tier B status (Birch 2024).
    • Tier D: non-subjects. Entities with no credible interests of their own: stones, rivers and ecosystems as such.

    4.2 The plausibility threshold

    The test for Tier C is whether there is a credible, evidence-based reason to think the entity has states that matter to it. Without such a threshold, precaution generates reasons everywhere and guides nowhere (Sunstein 2005).

    4.2b Five properties, not one

    The framework's Tier A–D structure is often read as a ranking by "how much" a being matters. It is not. The tiers distinguish five properties that are often conflated:

    • Moral status. Whether the being has interests that count in the framework's assessment at all.
    • Interests. What the being has reason to want or avoid, given its form of life.
    • Sentience. Whether the being has subjective experience.
    • Vulnerability. Whether the being can be harmed by the exercise of discretionary influence.
    • Capacity to express. Whether the being can express preferences, objections, or refusals.

    These five properties are related but not identical. A framework that equates them will either over-attribute or under-attribute.

    Precaution under uncertainty. Tier C is where the framework's precaution is most active. The plausibility threshold is not "any possibility"; it is "a credible, evidence-based reason to think the entity has states that matter to it."

    The projection problem. Representation of uncertain entities carries a specific risk: that the representative projects their own values onto the represented party. The framework's response is not to pretend otherwise. It is to make the projection visible: where the entity's preferences cannot be determined, the representative's account is a claim, not a fact, and it must be reviewable as a claim.

    4.3 The stone, restated

    The stone marks the edge of the framework's claims. For beings that meet the threshold, our failure to understand their way of being is no reason to dismiss their interests. For the stone, which does not meet it, the framework claims no standing and says so.

    4.4 Artificial systems

    Answerability for the influence of AI systems falls on the humans and organisations that design, deploy and maintain them. Whether some artificial systems may themselves be Tier C candidates is contested, and the framework does not answer it.

    4.5 Perspectival plurality

    For beings in Tiers A–C, their way of experiencing the world may be difficult or impossible for us to reach. That a perspective is inaccessible is not evidence that it is absent (Fricker 2007).

    4.6 Representation

    Representation is legitimate only if (i) representatives are chosen through a process that can itself be contested, (ii) they state the basis on which they claim to know the interests concerned, (iii) where interests are contested there is more than one representative, (iv) they can be removed, (v) they are neither the actor nor dependent on the actor, and (vi) they represent uncertainty rather than substituting invented certainty.

    (vii) Collective contestation must not undermine individual contestation. Where a collective procedure is used to represent affected parties, the procedure must preserve the ability of individuals within the collective to contest the collective's decisions.

    5. Interdependence, Difference and Non-Domination

    Existence is relational. What follows from that is not an ethics, but the ground on which an ethics must stand.

    Living beings, institutions, technologies and ecosystems exist through relations. Some of these relations are dependence, some are influence, some are interaction, some are competition, some are conflict, some are destruction, and some are mutual creation. This is the descriptive claim the essay calls universal symbiosis: not a claim that the universe is harmonious, moral, or guided by purpose, but a claim that nothing exists — no being, no institution, no technology, no ecosystem — except through relations with other things. The relations are not optional additions to isolated existents. They are what the existents are made of.

    Universal symbiosis is a worldview, not an ethics. It is compatible with mutualism and with parasitism, with care and with extraction, with cooperation and with domination. The essay's central normative claim, that certain ways of arranging relations are unacceptable, does not follow from it; §2.0a and §5.3 make that step explicit. Universal symbiosis describes what exists. Normative symbiosis specifies what is owed.

    5.0a Three relations, not one

    Universal symbiosis is a single word for at least three distinct relations:

    1. Causal influence. A affects B. A factory's emissions affect a downwind community's air.
    2. Dependence. A requires B for its continued existence, development, or functioning. A child depends on its caregivers.
    3. Ecological belonging. A is part of a system that has properties A does not have alone. A species belongs to an ecosystem.

    These three relations can come apart. An actor can affect a party without depending on it. An actor can depend on a party without affecting it in the relevant sense. An actor can belong to a system without exercising discretion over it.

    The ethical question the essay addresses is not 'does A exist in relation to B?' — the answer is always yes. The question is 'when does A's relation to B create a duty of answerability?' Its answer is not 'always' — it is 'when A exercises discretionary influence over B's conditions and B cannot exit or contest it.'

    The local and the systemic. Local actions can produce systemic effects, and systemic changes can produce local effects. The essay's activation rule (§7) and its account of distributed influence (§3.4) are attempts to locate responsibility in cases where the local and the systemic cannot be separated cleanly.

    The word "symbiosis" is used in this essay in a single, normative sense from here on: it names a floor for relations of dependence — the minimum conditions under which dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§10). This is normative symbiosis, and it is distinct from universal symbiosis in the way just stated. Parasitism, extraction, and domination are as much a part of universal symbiosis as mutualism is; the framework's claim is that they are unacceptable, not that they do not occur.

    5.0b Why universal symbiosis?

    §5 states that existence is relationally woven. This is a claim about the structure of reality, not a moral claim, and it is not self-evident. Three arguments support it. One limitation qualifies them. And one further question — what kind of philosophical starting point the claim is — must be answered without turning it into a shortcut from ontology to ethics.

    The negative argument. Nothing we have observed exists without relations. An atom is constituted by its relations to other atoms and to fields. An ecosystem is constituted by its relations between species, climate, and substrate. An institution is constituted by its relations between persons, rules, and roles. A mind is constituted by its relations to a body, a language, and other minds. In each case, the entity is not an isolated thing to which relations are added; the entity is what it is because of the relations. The negative argument does not prove that every entity is constituted this way. It establishes that no clear counterexample has been found, and that the burden of proof lies with the atomist who claims that some entity could exist without any relations at all.

    The abductive argument. Universal symbiosis explains certain features of reality better than atomism does. Meaning, function, and ecological role are properties that exist only relationally: a word means what it does because of its place in a language; a heart functions as a heart because of its place in a body; a species has an ecological role because of its place in an ecosystem. Atomism can describe the physical components of these features, but it cannot explain why the features exist without reference to relations. Universal symbiosis explains this in a single move: the relations are not added to the entities; they are what the entities are.

    The phenomenological argument. The experience of being an isolated self already presupposes relations. A self that experiences itself as separate from others has already distinguished itself from them — which is a relational act. Language, embodiment, and the recognition of other minds are all conditions of the experience of isolation, not its absence. This does not show that everything is relationally constituted; it shows that the experience of isolation is itself relational.

    What would count against universal symbiosis. The claim is not a proof, and it should state what would refute it. Three things would: (i) a clear case of an entity that exists without any relations to anything else; (ii) a demonstration that meaning, function, or ecological role can be fully explained without reference to relations; (iii) a demonstration that the experience of isolation is not itself relational. None of these has been established. But the absence of a counterexample is not a proof, and universal symbiosis should be read as a plausible hypothesis — the best available description of what exists — rather than a demonstrated truth.

    Why it matters for the framework — and what it does not do. Universal symbiosis matters for the framework because it specifies the kind of question the framework asks. If entities are constituted by their relations, then questions about who affects whom, who depends on whom, and which relations are already structurally asymmetric are not optional additions to the ethical question — they are part of what the ethical question is about. A framework that ignored the relational structure of existence would be asking about isolated agents, and there are no isolated agents. This is why §5.0a distinguishes causal influence, dependence, and ecological belonging: they are the three relations in which the framework's question arises.

    A first argument that depends on it: no null baseline. If entities are constituted by their relations, there is no baseline of non-influence from which an actor can say that it merely left things alone. Declining to act, to review or to change an arrangement is also a way of shaping others' conditions (§1.3 counts maintenance as influence). So "I did nothing to them" fails as a way of escaping the question, even though it may still be a true answer to it, since the actor may have done nothing wrong. This is the modest one of its two functions. The second, which supports P4, is in §5.0c. A reader who rejects universal symbiosis loses these two arguments, not the normative machinery (§2.0b), and can replace it by defending a null baseline directly.

    What kind of starting point this is. Universal symbiosis is a plausible hypothesis about the structure of what exists, defended against atomism but not proven. It locates the question the framework asks and does not answer it: it does not say which relations are acceptable or what is owed to whom. It does not claim that the universe is harmonious, that every relation is good, or that the whole outranks its parts; relations include destruction and extraction as much as care. A reader who accepts it and rejects normative symbiosis has made a different normative choice, not a logical error (§2.0a, §2.0b). A reader who rejects it can still use the normative machinery built on P1–P5. The two are separable, and better together.

    5.0c The stone and the universe: why embeddedness gives P4

    A stone, a person and an artificial general intelligence are different kinds of thing, and the differences matter. They share one feature that matters more for this framework: each is a finite part of the whole it tries to understand. The final question, what the whole is, has no answer that any of them can reach, and more intelligence does not change that. It changes the resolution of the local model, not the position of the one who holds it. For the final question every answerer ties at zero.

    The argument. (1) By universal symbiosis (§5.0b), every entity is constituted by relations within the whole, and none stands outside it. (2) Any entity that models the whole, or the conditions it shapes within the whole, is a finite part of what it models, and its model is itself a part of the whole. A finite part has fewer states available to it than the whole it belongs to, so it cannot hold a complete model of the whole at the whole's own resolution, and it cannot include in that model a complete model of itself that includes the model. (3) Influence spreads along relations, so the conditions an actor shapes extend beyond what any finite model of them can follow to the end. (4) Therefore no actor can fully understand the conditions it shapes, however intelligent it is. That is P4, here as a structural consequence of embeddedness and not only as an empirical observation.

    What this does and does not show. It shows that the limit is one of position and not of capacity, so it applies to the stone, the person and the AGI alike, and no future intelligence escapes it by being smarter. It does not show that all understanding is equal. Better models exist, and a person understands more about a river than a stone does. What the argument removes is the claim to completeness. What then separates answerers is whether their errors can be found. P4 does not depend on this argument: it stands as a defeasible empirical claim (§2.0b), and the argument adds a structural reason for it. The step from P4 to P5 still goes through P1 and CI (§2.9). It is an informal sketch that a physicist or philosopher of science would need to state formally, and premise (3), the spread of influence, is the one most open to challenge.

    The stone test. For any claim of authority, ask whether the claim would carry the same authority if a stone made it. If it would, the authority does not come from the intelligence of the one who makes the claim. It comes from the channels around it: review, exit, contestation and a record. If it would not, ask what the intelligence supplies that a channel cannot. The test applies to a person, an institution, an AGI and this framework. A superior that cannot be corrected has, epistemically, the same authority as the stone for the final question, however much it understands. That is the point of Limit 3 (§15.2c), stated as a test.

    5.1 Making normative symbiosis measurable (or admitting it is not)

    If the framework states defeaters for its ground and failure conditions for its activation rule, its own ideal cannot be exempt from assessment.

    Three indicators.

    • Non-exploitation (SY1). The dependent party's continued participation does not require it to accept terms that it would reject if it had exit at tolerable cost. Measured by: the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, adjusted for exit costs. Where the dependent party cannot state preferences, SY1 is assessed through the representational substitutes in §4.6 and the structural indicators in §15.2b.
    • Non-assimilation (SY2). The dependent party's difference is not treated as a defect to be corrected. Measured by: whether the actor's interventions aim at removing the difference or at removing the burden the difference carries.
    • Continued possibility (SY3). The dependent party retains the conditions for continued existence, development and participation. Measured by: whether F1–F5 (§10) hold over time.

    Cases where one indicator fails while others hold.

    1. SY1 fails, SY2 and SY3 hold. A workfare programme requires participants to accept low-wage work as a condition of benefits. SY1 fails: participants accept terms they would reject at tolerable exit cost. SY2 holds: the programme provides work, not "re-education." SY3 holds: participants retain the conditions for continued existence and development.
    2. SY2 fails, SY1 and SY3 hold. A language programme for deaf children teaches them to speak and discourages sign language. SY1 holds: parents accept voluntarily. SY3 holds: children develop language capacity. SY2 fails: the programme treats deafness as a defect to be corrected.
    3. SY3 fails, SY1 and SY2 hold. A training programme places adults with cognitive disabilities in jobs that pay adequately and does not treat their disability as a defect. But the jobs are dead-end. The relation forecloses development.

    These cases show that the indicators do specific work: each catches a failure that the others miss.

    Are SY1–SY3 independent of each other? Not fully, but they are independent enough that no single one can substitute for the other two:

    • They come apart in actual cases (as the three examples above show).
    • They have different measurement procedures.
    • They have different defeaters.

    SY1's limits as an indicator. SY1 is a necessary but not a sufficient indicator of non-exploitation. If stated preferences are adaptive, the comparison between stated preferences and accepted terms can converge for the wrong reason. The framework's response is to weight structural indicators (dissent channels, counterfactual improvement) more heavily than SY1 where they diverge (§15.2b).

    What "exit at tolerable cost" means when alternatives are absent. Exit cost is the total cost to the affected party of leaving the relation: material, social, psychological, and opportunity costs. "Tolerable" means the cost does not violate the party's F1–F5 protections (§10). Where no real alternative exists, SY1 cannot be assessed by the party's stated preference for exit, because there is nothing to prefer to. The framework's response is to assess SY1 through the structural indicators (iii and iv in §15.2b) and through counterfactual improvement.

    What SY1–SY3 do not show. A relation that satisfies all three is not therefore good. A relation that fails one is not therefore beyond repair. The indicators are diagnostic, not verdictive.

    • Universal symbiosis (descriptive) is distinguished from normative symbiosis (prescriptive) throughout.
    • Normative symbiosis is a floor, specified through three observable indicators.
    • SY1–SY3 are independent enough to do work that one indicator could not.
    • SY1 is weighted below (iii)/(iv) in §15.2b.
    • "Exit at tolerable cost" means the cost does not violate F1–F5.
    • Indicators are diagnostic, not verdictive.

    5.2 The epistemic burden of asymmetric influence

    Power does not make knowledge impossible. It changes what counts as adequate evidence.

    Where one party controls the terms of dependence, it may also control the categories, information and measures through which the relation is evaluated. The more powerful party can therefore mistake the absence of visible objection for consent, compliance for flourishing, or institutional approval for legitimacy.

    The claim is narrower than it might appear: asymmetric influence creates a heightened risk of epistemic self-confirmation. It does not claim that powerful actors are incapable of understanding others, or that the less powerful party is always correct.

    Where an actor exercises substantial discretionary influence over parties who lack effective means of exit or contestation, the actor bears a heightened burden to support claims about the effects and legitimacy of that influence with evidence that is not controlled solely by the actor, and with meaningful opportunities for affected parties or their legitimate representatives to correct the account.

    This is a rebuttable burden of inquiry and justification. It is not a claim that answerability is the only route to knowledge, that affected parties are infallible, or that procedure is sufficient for justice.

    Argument

    1. The object of assessment is partly first-personal. Whether a relation is exploitative, assimilating or agency-preserving cannot always be determined from aggregate outcomes alone.

    2. Asymmetry affects the production and interpretation of evidence. The more powerful party may set the available options, define success, control records, determine which complaints are legible, and select the time horizon over which outcomes are measured.

    3. Self-assessment is therefore insufficient as a default. When the actor controls both the influence and the evidential frame used to evaluate it, its conclusion is not independent confirmation.

    4. The appropriate response is plural correction, not a single privileged voice.

    5. This yields a practical principle of answerability.

    What follows — and what does not

    The argument supports a presumption against epistemic closure. This is not a new principle. It is B (§2.0) applied to the specific question of what the actor owes when it controls both the influence and the evidential frame.

    How adaptive preferences are weighed — and the paternalism risk

    The problem. A party may report satisfaction because it has adapted its preferences to a constrained situation. SY1 takes stated preferences seriously; but taken alone, it cannot distinguish genuine acceptance from adapted acceptance.

    How the framework weighs. When SY1 and the structural indicators diverge, the framework weighs them across four dimensions:

    1. Consistency across time.
    2. Consistency across contexts.
    3. Availability of the vocabulary for objection.
    4. Counterfactual improvement.

    None of these four is decisive on its own. Together they form the framework's answer to the question "how much weight should be given to stated preferences?": as much as the evidence about their formation supports, and no more.

    The paternalism risk, named explicitly. Three constraints prevent the framework from licensing the powerful to override what the affected party says:

    • The party's stated preferences remain the default.
    • The framework does not authorize an outsider to speak for the party.
    • The framework treats the party's own account as evidence, not as noise.

    What this does not solve. The framework cannot, in general, distinguish a party that has genuinely accepted its conditions from a party whose capacity to reject them has been dismantled (§15.2, Limit 1).

    Defeaters and tests
    • B1 — No systematic epistemic risk.
    • B2 — Better alternatives.
    • B3 — Participation can worsen knowledge.
    • B4 — Independent review is not independent.
    • B5 — Disproportionate burden.

    5.3 Why this normative symbiosis, and not another

    SY1–SY3 are not self-evidently the right indicators of a good relation. This section defends their selection by deriving them from the thin standing claim, and by comparing them to the main rival floors.

    The objection. §5.1 specifies normative symbiosis through SY1–SY3. But why these three? Why not welfare, or capability, or autonomy?

    What kind of ideal normative symbiosis is. It is a floor, not a complete theory of the good relation.

    What the framework deliberately leaves open. A relation can meet every one of SY1–SY3 and F1–F5 and still be cold, indifferent, hollow, or unjust in ways the framework does not measure. The framework does not claim to specify:

    • Care in any thick sense.
    • Trust.
    • Meaning.
    • Community.
    • Mutual development.
    • Positive ideals of the good relation.

    Where these ideals are specified, the framework points to the traditions it engages: the capability approach (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006), care ethics (Tronto 1993; Held 2006), the relational traditions (§2.5). The framework's contribution is the floor, not the house.

    Deriving SY1–SY3 from thin standing

    The question. Under what conditions is the standing claim non-vacuous? SY1–SY3 specify the three distinct ways standing can be made vacuous, and therefore the three minimum conditions for it not to be.

    • SY1 — Non-exploitation. If affected parties can demand reasons but are then required to accept terms they would reject if they had exit at tolerable cost, their demand for reasons is hollow.
    • SY2 — Non-assimilation. If affected parties can demand reasons but their difference is treated as a defect to be corrected, their standing is absorbed into assimilation.
    • SY3 — Continued possibility. If affected parties can demand reasons but the conditions of their continued existence, development, or participation do not hold, they have no power to do anything with the reasons they receive.

    What the derivation shows, and what it does not. The derivation shows that SY1–SY3 are the three distinct ways standing can fail. It does not show that SY1–SY3 are sufficient for a good relation.

    Compared to rival floors

    Welfare. A benevolent despot with perfect welfare provision can still fail SY1, SY2, and SY3.

    Capability. A capability theorist proposes a richer floor. SY1–SY3 are thinner — they specify minimum conditions rather than a substantive list.

    Autonomy. Autonomy accounts often struggle with cases of legitimate dependence. SY1–SY3 handle these cases directly.

    Six counterexamples
    • 1. The cold but non-exploitative order. A relation in which SY1, SY2 and SY3 all hold — but the relation is emotionally empty. What catches it: nothing in SY1–SY3. The framework says so explicitly.
    • 2. The order that preserves existence but blocks development. What catches it: SY3, via F3. The case is a test of whether F3 is read formally or substantively.
    • 3. The order that preserves future possibilities but controls almost every present choice. What catches it: SY1 partially. This is the case that the framework most clearly does not fully catch.
    • 4. The order where preferences are adaptive. What catches it: Limit 1 (§15.2), and §15.2b's structural indicators.
    • 5. The order that protects an ecosystem but harms sentient individuals. What catches it: nothing in SY1–SY3 directly. Handled by §12.6 and §12.1.
    • 6. The order that is symbiotic for the collective but dominating for the individual. What catches it: §4.6 (vii).

    What the six cases show. Three of the six fall outside what SY1–SY3 catch, and the framework should say so explicitly. Two are caught, but only if the principles are read substantively. One is at the edge of what SY1–SY3 can detect.

    • SY1–SY3 are a pro tanto floor: binding on those who accept the thin standing claim.
    • They are derived from the thin standing claim.
    • They are not reducible to welfare, capability, or autonomy.
    • The framework deliberately leaves open care, trust, meaning, community, mutual development.
    • Six counterexamples are tested against SY1–SY3.

    5.3b Exit-alibi and the conjunctive minimum

    The most serious objection to J is that a party can satisfy the requirement by offering formal exit that is not, in practice, meaningful. This section states the reply, and states what remains as a weakness.

    A platform can permit data export but retain network effects. An employer can permit resignation but retain the competence monopoly. A state can permit emigration but retain border control. In each case, exit exists formally but not materially.

    The reply is not that J is wrong. It is that J's meaningful exit is already conjunctive. §B.1 specifies four thresholds: cost, functional parity, migration time, non-retaliation. If any of these is unmet, exit is not meaningful, and J is not satisfied. This is not an ad hoc fix; it follows from what "meaningful" means in J's formulation.

    What remains as a weakness. The thresholds (10 %, 90 %, 6 months, non-retaliation — §B.1) are contestable. A party can argue that 10 % is too strict or too lenient. This is an open question, not a closed one. The framework should say so. It should also say that the thresholds are starting points, not validated limits — and that anyone who wants to contest them must do so with argument, not with assertion.

    Conclusion. Exit-alibi is handled at the principle level but not at the threshold level. That is the honest position. A reader who accepts J's formulation cannot accept formal exit as satisfaction; a reader who disputes the thresholds disputes the specification, not the principle. Both are legitimate positions, but they are different positions.

    5.4 Care, respect, and the limits of understanding

    This section argues that care and respect are internal to the framework's premises in a specific, conditional sense — conditional on F, not on thin standing alone.

    Care and respect are not additions to the framework. They are what thin standing means when it is applied to a particular relation of dependence, and what fallibilism requires when the affected party's situation is not fully accessible to the actor. The qualification is that A1–A4 and the apophatic constraint are conditional on F, not on thin standing alone.

    Step 1. Responsibility without care becomes control

    An actor who gives an account of their influence but does not attend to whether the account responds to what is at stake for the other has given an account of the wrong thing. SY1 is precisely this.

    Step 2. Care without respect for the incomprehensible becomes paternalism

    Care that believes it understands the other's interests better than the other does can be benign in intention and domineering in effect. SY2 is the operational form of this danger.

    Step 3. Respect for the incomprehensible is internal to the framework — conditionally

    Both F and thin standing entail something that is often treated as a separate moral attitude. Fallibilism says: no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. Thin standing says: affected parties have moral weight. If both are accepted, they do not merely permit respect for what the actor does not understand; they require it.

    What this shows, and what it does not

    What it shows: care and respect are not imports. What it does not show: that a relation satisfying SY1–SY3 is therefore caring or respectful in any thicker sense.

    Defeaters
    • C1 — Care and respect are genuinely external.
    • C2 — SY1–SY3 can be satisfied without care or respect.
    • C3 — The terminology is equivocal.
    • C4 — Respect for the incomprehensible slides into a precautionary principle.

    5.5 Material capacity and the limits of formal freedom

    §2.1's counterexample — a society that satisfies J and the Floor but is unjust because the distribution of resources makes formal freedom unusable — is not a refutation. It is a specification of the gap. This section specifies the gap.

    The gap. Formal exit and formal voice are necessary but not sufficient. They are sufficient only if the affected party has the material capacity to use them. Without that capacity, formal freedom is an empty guarantee — present on paper, absent in practice.

    Three material conditions. These supplement the MEX thresholds in §B.1 (which apply to exit) and apply to both exit and voice:

    • M1 — Resources. The affected party has the economic, temporal and informational resources to exercise exit or voice. A worker who can formally resign but cannot afford unemployment does not have M1.
    • M2 — Access to alternatives. The affected party has access to the alternative that exit would lead to. A tenant who can formally move but has no housing to move to does not have M2.
    • M3 — Protection against retaliation. The affected party can use voice without retaliation. An employee who can formally complain but risks dismissal does not have M3.

    When M1–M3 fail. J's formal satisfaction is not adequate. This is not an extension of J — it is a specification of what "meaningful" in J's formulation already implies. J says "meaningful exit" and "real contest"; M1–M3 say what "meaningful" and "real" mean in material terms.

    Why this does not make J a distributive theory. It is tempting to think that M1–M3 make J a question of resource distribution. They do not. M1–M3 specify what is required for J to be satisfied, not how resources should be distributed. A society can satisfy M1–M3 by guaranteeing minimal resources, or by guaranteeing full equality, or by something in between. The framework does not say which. It only says that without M1–M3, J is not satisfied.

    What this solves. §2.1's counterexample — the society that satisfies J and the Floor but is unjust — satisfies J's form but not its material preconditions. It is a specification, not a refutation. The counterexample shows that J without M1–M3 is insufficient; it does not show that J with M1–M3 is.

    What this does not solve. It does not solve the question of how many resources are required, or who is to provide them. Nor does it solve the question of collective action — if M1–M3 fail for an entire population, that is a political question, not a question for the framework. The framework can diagnose the failure; it cannot repair it.

    5.6 Protection and domination: where the line is

    The framework says that protection is not captivity. It does not say where the line is. This section specifies it.

    The principle. Protection is justified when the protected party lacks the relevant capacity to choose. Protection becomes domination when it replaces the protected party's capacity instead of preserving it.

    The test. For any protective intervention, ask: does it preserve the capacity of the protected party to choose, or does it substitute the protector's choice for the protected party's? If it preserves, it is protection. If it substitutes, it is domination — even if the protector's choice is better.

    Three cases.

    • A parent protecting a child from a hot stove. The child lacks the capacity to judge the danger. Protection is justified. It does not replace the child's capacity; it preserves the child's capacity to develop one.
    • A state requiring seatbelts. The citizen has the capacity to judge the risk. The state substitutes its judgment for the citizen's. This is a mild form of paternalism; whether it is justified depends on how serious the risk is and how reversible the intervention is. The framework does not decide the case, but it names what is at stake.
    • A superior intelligence protecting a species from itself. The species has the capacity to choose, but not the capacity to see the consequences of its choices. Protection is justified in proportion to the gap between the species' capacity and the consequences it cannot see. It becomes domination when the superior substitutes its own ends for the species' ends, not merely its own judgment for the species' judgment.

    Why this matters for the framework's hardest question. §15.2b's A1–A4 specify what a superior must do when it cannot understand. This section specifies what any actor must do when it can understand but chooses to act protectively anyway. The two are related but not identical: A1–A4 address epistemic limits, §5.6 addresses protective limits.

    What the principle does not do. It does not tell you, in every case, whether an intervention preserves or substitutes capacity. That is a judgment, and the framework does not make it for you. What the principle does is give you the question to ask — and the recognition that the difference between protection and domination is not in the protector's intentions, but in whether the protected party's capacity survives the intervention.

7. The Activation Rule

Discretion opens the gate. Entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit set the level.

7.1 The rule

  • No discretion: the actor does not bear the duty for this influence. It shifts to whoever set the governing rule or could have.
  • Discretion present: Level 1 always applies.
  • Level 2 applies if any one driver is High, or any two are Moderate.
  • Level 3 applies if S is High and at least one of E or C is High.

Severity is judged first at the individual level. For decisions about the design of a system that acts on many people, it is also judged at the aggregate level (§9.1).

7.2 Why these conditions

Each element can be derived from J (§2.1).

  • Discretion. Justification is owed for what an agent chooses.
  • Entrenchment. What makes mistaken influence tolerable is the possibility of correction afterwards. Entrenchment removes that channel.
  • Severity. The strength of a complaint scales with the burden it describes (§12.1).
  • Contestation deficit. Where the affected can exit, object effectively and understand what is done to them, they supply part of the accountability themselves (Hirschman 1970).

7.3 What the rule is not

It is not a score and it applies no weights. It is not a verdict either. It yields a presumptive level that can be overridden only with stated reasons (§9.3).

7.4 What counts as discretion

Discretion is the gate (§7.1), so it needs a test that two raters can apply and a rule for doubtful cases. It is judged per influence and not per actor: the same person can have discretion over one influence and none over another.

Two conditions, both required. (1) Alternatives. The actor could have done otherwise at a cost it could bear and still remain the kind of actor it is. (2) Role. The actor had a part in making, maintaining or contesting the influence, as opposed to merely executing a rule it had no way to affect (§1.3). A constraint the actor imposed on itself, or a pressure it created or sustained by earlier choices, does not remove either condition.

Three borderline cases.

  • The rule-following employee. There is no discretion over the rule, and the duty for the rule falls on whoever set it (§7.1). Discretion usually remains in how the rule is applied, and the duty attaches to that residual.
  • Competitive pressure. "Our competitors would do the same" describes a cost of alternatives. If the pressure is produced or sustained by the actor's own choices or lobbying, condition (2) is met. If the cost is genuinely intolerable, the duty shifts to those who set the market's rules or could (§7.1). That shift should be recorded as a shift, not as the absence of a duty.
  • Emergent systems. Where the developer cannot predict individual outputs, discretion lies in design, deployment, monitoring and the decision to keep operating, even though no single output was chosen. Foreseeability (§6) then fixes what the actor must answer for.

Doubt (practical recommendation, §2.0a). Record which of the two conditions is doubtful. Until the doubt is resolved, treat discretion as present at Level 1. Level 1 costs little, an explanation on request, while a mistaken exemption removes the account altogether.

8. The Duty Ladder and Who Bears It

Three levels, each containing the one below. Duties are allocated by capacity, not heaped on whoever is nearest.

8.1 The levels

Level 1: Explain. The actor can state, on request and in proportion to the matter, the purpose of the action, the interests considered, the alternatives considered and the known uncertainties.

Level 2: Review and respond. Level 1, plus: an accessible channel for objection; access to the relevant information; a reasoned response within a reasonable time; revision where warranted; a record of decisions and reasons; a reviewer other than the original decider where feasible; and evidence that review is not symbolic.

Level 3: Preserve, examine, authorise. Level 2, plus: preserved records and version history; independent examination meeting the conditions of §11; a written assessment of less harmful alternatives; authorisation by a body outside the actor for severe or irreversible influence; staged implementation, reversibility by design or a sunset clause; remedy for those harmed; and post-implementation review. Level 3 authorisation requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation (§11.4).

8.2 Allocation by capacity

The level attaches to the influence, that is, to the arrangement. Duties are then allocated.

Those with the capacity to establish mechanisms bear the duty to establish Level 2 and Level 3 mechanisms. Others along the chain owe their own Level 1 explanation, honest participation, escalation of concerns and non-obstruction. No scapegoating: a front-line actor is not answerable for structural deficits she cannot cure, though she remains answerable for her own discretion.

8.3 Not authority

A higher level means stronger constraints on power, not greater authority. See §1.3 (Table 1a) for the full distinction.

9. Rating Protocol: Anchors, Hygiene and Sensitivity

Judgment cannot be eliminated. It can be made visible, checkable and contestable.

9.1 Anchors

Severity is rated on the most burdened individual first. For decisions about system design, aggregate severity is rated on population-level effects.

9.2 Hygiene

  1. Rate before looking at the level the rule produces and before deciding what the actor wants to do.
  2. Use at least two raters, one independent of the actor where Level 2 or above is in question.
  3. Record disagreements instead of averaging them.
  4. Sensitivity test. Move each rating one step up and down. If the level changes, the case is borderline.
  5. Calibration. Compare the profile with the reference cases in §14.

9.3 Reasoned override

The presumptive level may be overridden up or down only with written reasons. Overrides are recorded and, where safe, published.

9.4 Counter-profiles

Any affected party or representative may file an alternative profile, and the actor must respond to the specific differences.

9.5 What a rating can claim

Low, Moderate and High are ordinal categories assigned by raters applying anchors. They are not measurements, and the level the rule produces inherits the uncertainty of the ratings beneath it. Three practical recommendations (§2.0a) follow.

  • Report a range when the case is borderline. If the sensitivity test (§9.2) changes the level, report both levels and the ratings that drive the difference, not a single level.
  • Apply the higher level provisionally. A level that is too low removes review where it may be needed; a level that is too high costs an explanation and a channel for objection. Apply the higher level with a stated review date, and lower it only with reasons (§9.3). For a borderline between Level 2 and Level 3, apply Level 2 and request an independent rating of whether Level 3 applies, since authorisation by an outside body is costly.
  • Report agreement with the level. Give inter-rater agreement beside the level, and do not average disagreement away (§9.2).

No figure the framework reports is more reliable than the weakest rating beneath it. The pilot in Appendix A tests whether the anchors reduce disagreement, not whether the ratings are correct (§A.7b).

9.6 Decision tree

The rules in §7.1 are formulated as claims. A decision tree is the same rules in another form — one that reduces the number of places where a user has to make a judgment. The tree is not a new rule; it is §7.1 stated as a sequence of yes/no questions.

QUESTION 1: Does the actor have discretion over the influence?
  (Alternatives available at tolerable cost AND role in setting the influence)
  NO  → No duty under J. Shift to whoever set the rule.
  YES → Go to QUESTION 2.

QUESTION 2: Is the influence trivial?
  (Severity floor, §7.1)
  YES → No duty under J.
  NO  → Go to QUESTION 3.

QUESTION 3: Is any of E, S, C High?
  NO  → Go to QUESTION 4.
  YES → Go to QUESTION 5.

QUESTION 4: Are at least two of E, S, C Moderate?
  NO  → L1 (Explain).
  YES → L2 (Review and respond).

QUESTION 5: Is S High AND (E or C) High?
  NO  → L2 (Review and respond).
  YES → L3 (Preserve, examine, authorise).

What the tree does not do. It does not decide whether the cases are just. It decides which level applies. It is a diagnostic sequence, not a decision procedure (§15.3b).

Why a tree instead of rules. Rules in prose form require the user to hold several conditions in memory at once. A tree requires the user to answer one question at a time. Each question is a possible error source; the tree reduces the number of simultaneous error sources.

Part IV

Structure: Floor, Review, Power

10. The Floor: Minimum Conditions in Relationships of Dependence

Continued existence is not enough. A being can stay alive or operational while losing any say over its own circumstances.

  • F1 — Exit or voice. A meaningful opportunity to withdraw, refuse or communicate disagreement. The protection of F1 does not depend on the party's capacity to express refusal, and the absence of expressed refusal is not consent (§15.2b, A3).
  • F2 — Contestation. Decisions are open to challenge regardless of the intelligence, authority or technical capability of the decider. Contestation may be collective, subject to §4.6 (vii).
  • F3 — Development. Opportunities for learning, adaptation and growth are preserved.
  • F4 — Non-foreclosure. The future possibilities of others are not unnecessarily closed.
  • F5 — Recognition. Interests and possible experiences are acknowledged rather than dismissed because they are hard to understand (Fricker 2007).

F1 and F2 are the mirror image of the contestation deficit in §7: where they fail, C rises. The Floor is a conjunctive requirement with J, not an alternative to it (§2.1).

F1–F5 are minimum conditions, not a complete account of a good relation. The framework deliberately leaves open the positive ideals (§5.3). What it offers is the floor, not the house.

  • Five minimum conditions (F1–F5).
  • The Floor applies independently of the activation rule, and is conjunctive with J.
  • Protection is not captivity.

11. No Sole Judge: Review, Power and the Regress

No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence.

11.1 The prohibition

An actor exercising substantial influence must remain answerable to forms of review that are meaningfully independent of its own interests and decisions.

This does not assume that the powerful are malicious. Actors are prone to self-serving judgment and are limited in what they can see (§2.3), and review that is not independent of the reviewed has little claim to legitimacy.

11.2 The regress

If every reviewer needs a reviewer, accountability looks like an endless hierarchy. The response has three parts.

  • Finality without infallibility. Working systems end the regress with decisions that are final for practical purposes but can be reopened on new evidence, on new standing, on demonstrated procedural failure, or at a sunset.
  • Contestation is not adjudication. The reviewed and the affected may challenge a reviewer's independence or competence before a differently constituted body.
  • Plurality and publicity instead of a top. Several reviewers with different standpoints, publishing their reasons, make decentralised scrutiny possible (cf. Ostrom 1990).

11.3 Design conditions for review

  • R1 — Four kinds of independence, not one.
    • R1a — Organizational independence. The reviewer is a distinct body from the actor.
    • R1b — Financial independence. The reviewer's funding does not depend on the actor's continuing goodwill.
    • R1c — Informational independence. The reviewer has access to the information it needs without the actor's permission.
    • R1d — Publication independence. The reviewer can publish its findings without the actor's prior approval.
    Each of the four is necessary. A reviewer that fails any one of them fails R1.
  • R2 Plurality.
  • R3 Publicity.
  • R4 Contestable reviewers.
  • R5 Effectiveness.
  • R6 Contestable rules.

Capture indicators: reviewers appointed or funded by the reviewed; a record of never dissenting; staff moving between reviewer and reviewed; reviewers denied access; chronic delay.

11.4 Power over the framework itself: from diagnosis to design

The framework treats the power regress not as a limit it cannot eliminate, but as a design problem it can bound.

Who defined the categories? Who selected the reviewers? Who can dismiss them? Whose interests are excluded even where formally represented? These four questions are asked in every application (§14, step 6).

The requirement. T5 must be a precondition, not a diagnosis. Level 3 authorisation requires, as a condition of validity, that the authorising body publishes its R1 indicators before authorisation. An authorisation issued by a body that cannot or will not publish these indicators is not a Level 3 authorisation. It is a Level 2 authorisation with a Level 3 label.

How review bodies are captured. Three mechanisms:

  • Financial capture. The reviewer's funding depends on the actor's continuing goodwill.
  • Appointment capture. The reviewer is appointed by the actor, or by a body the actor controls.
  • Cultural capture. The reviewer shares the actor's assumptions, vocabulary, or professional norms, even where formally independent. The framework's response is R2 (plurality) and R4 (contestable reviewers).

What the framework can do about capture. It can require the R1 indicators to be published before authorisation (T5-precondition). It can require that a captured review be downgraded (§14.7). It can require a plural review structure (R2) and contestable reviewers (R4). What it cannot do is prevent capture.

The regress as a real limit. Three levels:

  1. First-order capture: the actor captures its own reviewer. The T5-precondition addresses this.
  2. Second-order capture: the body that appoints the reviewer is itself captured. The framework marks the authorisation as "authorisation issued by a captured body", which is weaker than Level 2.
  3. Third-order capture: no external body exists that can audit the appointer. This is the Limit 3 case in its institutional form (§15.2b, §15.2c). The framework does not have a solution at this level. What it can do is name it.

12. Conflicts, Ecology and Future Generations

Decisions must be justified, not derived.

12.0 Decision ordering: floor, comparison, aggregation, representation

Conflicts between affected parties cannot be resolved by reference to "symbiosis" or "the whole". They need an ordering, and the framework provides one.

What the framework is ordered by. Five steps:

  1. The floor (F1–F5) is not weighable. No party's F1–F5 protections can be traded against another party's non-floor interests. The only exception is §12.2.
  2. Within the floor, individual comparison (§12.1).
  3. Aggregation only as tie-breaker (§12.1).
  4. Representation of absent parties (§4.6).
  5. Uncertainty and irreversibility shift the burden.

What this does not authorize. It does not authorize any party to invoke "the whole", "the collective", or "the ecosystem" as a reason to sacrifice an individual's floor protections.

What this does not do. It does not resolve every conflict, does not claim that all legitimate interests can be harmonised, and does not claim that a correct decision always exists. Treating floor protections as not weighable does not make conflicts disappear; it makes them visible as conflicts of floor protections (§12.6). Where two parties' floor protections are both at stake and cannot both be satisfied, the framework requires that the decision be made with public reasons, be subject to review, and have its burdens recorded (§12.7, §12.8).

12.1 Weighing conflicting interests

Neither majority interest nor individual preference automatically prevails. The method is individual comparison: compare the burdens that each alternative places on each affected party, party by party, and favour the alternative that avoids the most serious individual burden. Claims of comparable weight may be aggregated as a tie-breaker (Scanlon 1998). Among the acceptable alternatives, choose the one with minimum necessary harm.

12.2 Emergencies

Where an immediate, serious threat makes the full procedure impossible, an actor may act first, provided it acts only as far as necessary, records its reasons at the time, is bound by an automatic sunset, and submits to review afterwards. Emergencies create no standing exemptions.

12.3 Ecological integrity

Ecosystems cannot be assessed through the preferences of individual participants. Protecting them may require restricting activities, and the justification runs through dependent beings in Tiers A–C, through future generations, and through the non-instrumental values that people hold (§4.1). Ecosystem integrity is not a free-standing value that can override individual floor protections (§12.0).

12.4 No automatic hierarchy

Intelligence, productivity, population size and technical sophistication do not by themselves determine moral priority. Neither do collective size, ecosystemic scale or future extension (§12.0).

12.5 Future generations

The duty is condition-regarding: to preserve the conditions under which future people can exercise agency and meet basic needs, and a reasonably wide space of valuable options (cf. Parfit 1984; Jonas 1984). Option preservation is not absolute. It operates as a weight, strongest where actions are irreversible.

Future generations are represented under the six conditions of §4.6. Their representatives are bound by the same limits as any other representative.

12.6 Ecological wholes and individual beings

The hardest conflicts are not between abstract "wholes" and abstract "individuals". They are between particular claims that cannot all be met. This section distinguishes three cases that must not be run together.

Three cases.

  1. The whole requires the death of individuals. A predator population must be culled to prevent ecosystem collapse. The individuals culled are sentient, and their deaths are harms.
  2. Individual interests threaten the whole. A growing human population's resource demands threaten a forest ecosystem.
  3. The whole is invoked but the real question is distribution. A dam is proposed as "necessary for the region's ecological health," but the benefits accrue to some and the costs to others.

Case 1. Not "the whole wins" and not "the individual always wins." The framework says: (a) the individual's F1–F5 protections are not weighable, but "not weighable" does not mean "never overridden in a genuine emergency" — §12.2 permits temporary violation under strict conditions. (b) The culling of sentient individuals for ecosystem preservation is a violation of their F1, and it must be treated as such. (c) If the culling is necessary, it is a tragedy, and the framework requires that the tragedy be named as one. (d) The framework requires that less harmful alternatives be examined and documented before any such action. (e) Future animals who would have been born into the collapsed ecosystem are not a counterweight that outweighs the current individuals; they are parties whose interests are represented, and whose floor protections, where they exist, are also not weighable. The conflict is between two sets of floor protections, and the framework does not have a decision procedure that resolves this uniquely.

Case 2. The individuals' current well-being is not automatically outweighed by the ecosystem's future. The framework's §12.0 ordering applies. If the ecosystem's collapse would violate the floor protections of many future parties, the framework permits restricting current individuals' non-floor interests — but not their own floor protections, unless §12.2's emergency conditions are met.

Case 3. The ecological framing is legitimate only if the ecological claim is genuine. Where the framing is a mask for a distributive conflict, the framework's response is §12.0 step 2 and step 3. "Ecological necessity" is not a trump card.

12.7 When every alternative harms someone

When every available alternative harms at least one party whose claim is legitimate, and no alternative is clearly best.

Two cases.

  1. The dam. Dam or leave undammed — both impose serious harms.
  2. The clinical allocation. One ventilator, two patients. Both patients' F1 is at stake.

What the framework says. Five conditions that a legitimate decision must satisfy:

  1. The decision must name the conflict.
  2. The decision must state the basis on which one harm is preferred to another.
  3. The decision must be reviewable.
  4. The decision must record the harms.
  5. The decision must not pretend that the framework has resolved what it has not.

12.8 When the framework's own principles collide

§12.7 covers conflicts between parties. The harder test is conflict between the framework's own commitments. Three cases, each with a resolution and its cost.

Case A: transparency (J) against privacy (Floor). A platform must justify its content decisions to users (contestable justification), but doing so would reveal one user's private data to another. Resolution: the Floor does not give way. Justification is given at the level of rules and aggregate reasons, and the individual record is released only to the independent reviewer under confidentiality. Cost: the contesting party sees less than J would ideally require, so J is met in a weakened form and the shortfall should be recorded.

Case B: protection (duty ladder) against agency (A1–A4). A carer or institution with high influence over a dependent person can prevent a harm only by overriding the person's stated choice. Resolution: override is permitted only where the person's capacity to resist or revise later is preserved, the intervention is the least restrictive available, and the person's resistance is treated as evidence against the carer's judgement (A4), not as a mere obstacle. Cost: some preventable harms will not be prevented. The framework accepts this where the alternative is making the carer's understanding the final measure.

Case C: exit (J) against the Floor of others. A community's right to leave a hierarchical arrangement conflicts with remaining members' floor protections, which depend on the leaver's contribution. Resolution: exit stays available, since without it J fails. Burdens created for the remaining members are addressed by compensation or transition arrangements, not by restricting exit. Cost: where no such arrangement exists, the remaining members bear a real harm, and §12.7's five conditions apply.

The general rule these cases show. The Floor is not weighed against non-floor interests, and J, the Floor and A1–A4 are not ranked absolutely against each other. When they collide, protect first the interest whose loss is both serious and hard to undo, and satisfy the weakened principle in the strongest form that remains. The reason is that errors about reversible losses can be corrected later by the very mechanisms J requires, while errors about irreversible losses remove the chance to correct. This is a presumption, not a lexical order: a trivial irreversible loss does not outweigh a serious reversible one, and the decision-maker must say why in each case. The collision is then handled in the nine-step procedure (§14.1b) as a floor judgement with a recorded shortfall, and the shortfall is submitted to review under the conditions of §12.7. A framework that claimed a strict order here would claim more than it can defend.

  • §12.0 states a five-step decision ordering.
  • Individual comparison first; aggregation only as tie-breaker.
  • Emergencies create no standing exemptions.
  • §12.6 distinguishes three cases of ecological-whole versus individual conflict.
  • §12.7 specifies five conditions that a legitimate decision must satisfy when every alternative harms someone.
  • §12.8 shows how the framework's own principles are handled when they collide: protect the least reversible, satisfy the weakened principle as far as possible, record the shortfall.

13. Learning, Correction and Reversibility

No actor, institution or intelligence should assume its understanding is complete.

The duty to learn. Where capacity exists and consequences are significant, actors monitor outcomes, investigate unexpected results, listen to criticism and update.

Reversibility by design. Consequential decisions should be built to be revisable.

Self-application. The framework is itself an influence on those who adopt it and on those affected by their adoption. It is subject to its own principles.

14. Application: A Nine-Step Procedure and Worked Cases

The same procedure yields very different obligations for different cases.

14.1 The procedure

  1. Identify the relationship.
  2. Identify actors and discretion.
  3. Rate E, S and C against the anchors (§9.1), and F for attribution.
  4. Apply the activation rule (§7) and run the sensitivity test.
  5. Check the Floor (F1–F5, §10).
  6. Analyse power (§11.4).
  7. Examine alternatives and unrepresented interests (§12.1).
  8. Allocate duties by capacity (§8.2) and set up review that meets R1–R6.
  9. Reassess on stated triggers, and invite counter-profiles.

14.1b What the procedure delivers

The nine steps deliver four different things at four different points.

  • A presumptive level (§7, §9). Overridable only with stated reasons (§9.3).
  • A floor judgment (§10, §12.0). Binary: either the Floor holds or it does not.
  • A power analysis (§11.4). A judgment about whether the actor's claim to Level 3 legitimacy is valid.
  • A duty to investigate further, or no allocation at all. At certain steps, the procedure produces not an answer but a requirement to look further.

What the procedure does not deliver. It does not deliver a uniquely correct answer where two competent raters can reach different ratings, and it does not resolve genuine conflicts where two parties' floor protections are both at stake (§12.0, §12.7). The procedure's value is not that it eliminates disagreement; it is that it locates it.

14.1c Decision traces

A decision trace is a step-by-step documentation of how a coding led to a level. It is not a justification — it is a trace. The reader should be able to follow the trace without guessing.

Trace for the ICU-triage case (§14.2):

STEP 1 — Discretion (QUESTION 1): YES.
  The hospital has alternatives (different triage models) and role
  (deploys the system). Discretion is present.

STEP 2 — Severity floor (QUESTION 2): NO, not trivial.
  The influence is on life and health.

STEP 3 — Drivers:
  E (Entrenchment): HIGH. Model versions cannot be recalled
    after decisions are made. Patients cannot recover lost time.
  S (Severity): HIGH. Life and health.
  C (Contestation deficit): HIGH. Patients cannot appeal a
    triage decision in real time.

STEP 4 — Any High? YES (all three).

STEP 5 — S High AND (E or C) High? YES.
  → L3.

TRACE ENDS AT: L3.

Trace for the café-menu case (§14.6):

STEP 2 — Severity floor: YES, trivial. Go to L1? No —
  the severity floor excludes J entirely.
→ No duty under J.

Trace for the AI company's internal audit (§14.7):

STEP 1 — Discretion: YES.
STEP 2 — Severity floor: NO.
STEP 3 — Drivers:
  E: MODERATE (model versions can be recalled but at cost).
  S: HIGH (aggregate: influence on many).
  C: HIGH (users cannot appeal algorithmic decisions
    independently).

STEP 5 — S High AND (E or C) High? YES.

BUT: T5-precondition (§11.4) is tested separately.
  R1a (organizational): NO — the review is internal.
  R1b (financial): NO — the review is paid for by the company.
  R1c (informational): NO — the company controls the data.
  R1d (publication): NO — the company approves publication.
  → R1 fails. T5-precondition is not met.
  → L3 claim downgraded to L2.

What the traces show. That the procedure is not a black box. Each step is visible, and each step can be contested. This is what makes the framework usable: the reader can follow the trace and say "here your coding is wrong" — rather than "I disagree with the conclusion."

14.1d Tie-breakers

When two raters disagree (§14.5c), a rule is needed. Without a rule, the disagreement is unresolvable. With a rule, it is manageable — even if the rule is arbitrary.

Rule 1 — On disagreement about Severity, apply the higher level provisionally. A level that is too low removes review where it may be needed. A level that is too high costs an explanation and an objection channel. The cost is asymmetric.

Rule 2 — On disagreement about Entrenchment, apply the higher level provisionally. Same logic: a too-low entrenchment coding underestimates duration, which is harder to correct afterwards than overestimating it.

Rule 3 — On disagreement about Contestation deficit, apply the lower level provisionally. Here the logic is reversed: a too-high C coding can lead to unnecessary review, which is less harmful than missing a real deficit. But a too-high C can also lead to a real deficit being overlooked. The rule is therefore weaker than 1 and 2.

Rule 4 — On disagreement about level, apply the higher level with a review date. An L2/L3 disagreement is resolved provisionally as L2 with a requirement for independent assessment of whether L3 applies (§9.5).

Rule 5 — All provisional decisions must be documented. A provisional decision that is not documented is not provisional; it is a hidden decision. The documentation must state which rule was applied and why.

What tie-breakers are not. They are not truths. They are conventions that make disagreement manageable. A rater who is convinced that the lower level is right can document their conviction and still follow the rule — the rule is a procedural rule, not an epistemic one.

14.2 Case: AI triage in intensive care

A hospital deploys an AI system to prioritise ICU admissions during a respiratory pandemic. Entrenchment is High, severity is High, and contestation deficit is High. Level 3.

What Level 3 requires: preserved model versions, descriptions of training data, individual scores and override records; an independent ethics board with lay and patient representation; a written comparison with clinician-only triage; authorisation from the public-health authority; a sunset clause; and a remedy for those wrongly denied.

Power analysis: the categories were set by the vendor and clinical leadership, and the ethics committee is appointed by the hospital board, which fails R1 unless changed. A hybrid in which the AI recommends and a human decides is the default preference because it keeps contestation alive.

The framework does not decide the case. It tells the hospital what it owes an answer to, and to whom.

14.2b Case: the emergency physician (a counterintuitive result)

Every other case in this essay produces the answer a careful reader would predict. This one does not.

A physician in an emergency department must decide, in under ninety seconds, whether to intubate a patient. The activation rule gives Level 3.

The counterintuitive result. Level 3 requires independent authorisation, preserved records and a written assessment of alternatives — before the act. A reader's first reaction is that this is absurd.

The reply. The Level 3 duty does not fall on the physician in the moment. It falls on the arrangement (§8.2): on the hospital, the protocols, the audit trail, the retrospective review, and the training. The physician's own duty is Level 1 in the moment and Level 2 afterwards.

Legitimate relocation or evasion? The relocation is legitimate if and only if all three conditions hold. (i) A concrete duty-bearer is named. (ii) The mechanism exists before the moment of decision and can be pointed to. (iii) The relocation is not the same move that would be made in every case where the framework is inconvenient.

14.3 Case: a platform's recommendation system

For individual recommendations: Level 2. For the design of the system: Level 3, with preserved version history, independent audit, published aggregate metrics, and a sunset for any optimisation that cannot show population-level reversibility.

14.4 Case: a community health worker

She has little institutional power. The rule says the arrangement requires Level 3. But she has no capacity to build independent review. Under §8.2, the duty to establish mechanisms falls on the programme, the ministry and the funders. Her personal duties are to explain her decisions, respond to objections within her role, and escalate when protocols fail.

14.5 Four short cases

  • One-off organism release. Level 3, although the act happens once.
  • Dating platform age penalty. Level 2.
  • Café menu change. Level 1 only, and arguably not even that.
  • Physician following a protocol. No discretion over the protocol's content.

14.5b Case: emergency water rationing (duties conflict)

A water authority must impose rationing within 48 hours. §12.2 allows acting first. The duties then split.

  • Not compressible: published reasons, preserved records, a contestation channel, exemptions for life-critical dependence, a sunset clause.
  • Compressible: prior authorisation, replaced by review completed before the sunset.
  • Not decided by the framework: which rationing rule is fair.

14.5c Case: the ambiguous severity rating

A municipal housing authority must decide whether to relocate residents. Rater A rates Severity as Moderate; Rater B rates Severity as High. Entrenchment is High, contestation deficit is Moderate. The level depends on Severity.

What the procedure delivers. Not a determinate level. The disagreement between raters is recorded (§9.2), the case is flagged as borderline, and the level is stated with the disagreement noted.

What the case shows. The procedure does not resolve every ambiguity.

14.5d Case: aggregate severity and the individual-comparison rule

A city must decide whether to restrict car traffic. The restriction imposes a Moderate burden on commuters and delivers a Moderate benefit to residents. But there are 200,000 commuters and 20,000 residents.

What the procedure delivers. §12.0 ordering applies: (1) Floor check; (2) individual comparison; (3) aggregation as tie-breaker. The procedure does not specify how to weigh 200,000 Moderate burdens against 20,000 High-severity benefits.

What the case shows. The procedure gives a floor-check and an ordering, but at step (3) it produces a demand for a normative decision, not a determinate answer.

14.5e Case: no party has the capacity to discharge the duty

A regional health authority learns that a widely used medical device has a small failure rate. Under §3.4, none of the parties in the chain retains the ability to revoke, set the frame, or restore discretion.

What the procedure delivers. Not an allocation. §3.4 says that where no party can revoke, set the frame, or restore discretion, ECP does not generate a determinate allocation.

What the case shows. The procedure's honest output is sometimes "no determinate allocation."

This case shows what happens when the framework's T5-precondition is not met: the actor does not lose the framework; the actor loses the claim to Level 3 legitimacy.

An AI company commissions an "independent" audit of its recommendation system. The audit is published with a foreword by the CEO, the reviewers are paid by the company, and the company's board appoints them.

14.7b Case: the captured regulator (second-order capture)

§14.7 shows the first-order case. This case shows the second-order case.

A national data protection authority is asked to approve an AI company's recommendation system. The authority's director was appointed by the same minister whose party received donations from the company's parent. The authority's budget is set annually by the ministry.

What the framework's T5-precondition delivers. The precondition is met formally. But the indicators themselves reveal that R1b is not met, R1c is partially met, and R1d is met formally but the authority's publications are widely understood to be constrained by the ministry's preferences.

What the framework says. The authorisation does not count as a Level 3 authorisation. The framework's response is to mark it as "authorisation issued by a captured body" — which is weaker than Level 2.

What the framework cannot do. It cannot tell the company what to do next. It can tell the company that its authorisation is not a Level 3 authorisation. It can tell the public that the authority is captured. But if the authority is captured by the same power that captured the company, and no court is available that is not itself captured, then the framework's response is exhausted.

15. Limits, Testing and Replacement

This section states what the framework's ground does not rest on, the three structural limits the framework cannot close, and what follows for its status as a diagnostic.

15.1 The ground is not derived

The framework rests on three premises it does not derive. Saying so is not a failure. Saying otherwise is.

(i) B is a premise, not a theorem. §2.0 states B as a bridge norm. F motivates B. F does not entail B. Therefore B is a premise.

(ii) Thin standing is a premise, not a theorem. §2.7 states thin standing as the framework's first normative commitment. It is chosen, not derived.

(iii) The operational components stand on their own — with one exception. The activation rule, the duty ladder, the rating protocol, the T5-precondition and the SY1–SY3 indicators do not depend on whether B or thin standing are correct. The exception is the apophatic constraint and A1–A4, which are conditional on F.

15.2 Three structural limits

These are not limitations of execution. They are limitations of what any procedure of the kind the framework proposes can see.

Limit 1 — Harmonious Domination. A society that satisfies every rule and every indicator, but the terms the dependent party accepts are terms it would reject at tolerable exit cost if it could imagine the alternative. The framework cannot detect this. The framework's partial response is a change of default: testimony from a party inside a structurally asymmetric relation is not evidence for symbiosis unless independent confirmation exists.

Limit 2 — Non-formulable harm. A harm that the harmed party cannot formulate — because the resources for formulating it have been withheld, or because the party's form of life is not legible in the framework's terms — falls outside the indicators. The framework's partial response is to treat silence as a presumption against, not a neutral condition.

Limit 3 — Unreadable dominance. A sufficiently advanced intelligence, or a sufficiently well-organised institution, can satisfy every procedural requirement while controlling a party whose form of life it does not understand and cannot make legible. The framework's partial response is to add understanding as a condition for L3. But this response has its own limit. The dominant party may believe it understands — and be wrong, in ways its own categories cannot detect. And in the Limit 3 case, where no party outside the superior can verify compliance, this specification has no binding force at all.

15.2b How to act toward what you cannot understand

The three limits in §15.2 are stated negatively: they say what the framework cannot see. The question this section addresses is positive: what can a superior intelligence do, given those limits? The answer is not a procedure, and the section says so.

The question. Can a superior intelligence preserve another being's existence, freedom and developmental possibilities without understanding what that being really is — and without making its own understanding the measure?

The honest answer. The superior cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure. Every act of preservation is an act of classification, and the classification is the superior's. There is no general procedure that guarantees non-imposition. But the claim that imposition is unavoidable in every respect is too strong. The categories can be thin, revisable and shadowed by alternatives; the superior can be required to keep them open to correction.

The superior must preserve not the being's current self-understanding, and not the superior's understanding of the being, but the structural conditions under which the being can develop and express a self-understanding that the superior cannot anticipate. The superior must act as if the being's reality exceeds its understanding, and must build that assumption into its action.

Three epistemic gaps, not one.

  • The experiential gap. The superior has no access to what the being's experience is like. Response: humility and precaution, but not paralysis.
  • The descriptive gap. The superior cannot describe the being's states in terms the being would recognize as accurate. Response: A2 and the funding of translation and intermediaries.
  • The causal gap. The superior cannot know whether its actions help or harm the being. Response: A3 and A4, plus structural indicators.

Four conditions follow.

  • A1 — Preserve the capacity to resist.
  • A2 — Do not require justification in the superior's terms.
  • A3 — Do not interpret non-resistance as consent.
  • A4 — Treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding.

How A1–A4 can fail in practice. Five failure modes: formal channel capture (A1), translation burden (A2), paralysis by A3, resistance misread as noise (A4), and institutional absence (all four).

When the requirements collide. Humility, precaution, reversibility, and preservation of agency are not always compatible. The framework does not resolve these collisions by a principle. It resolves them by a procedure: where the requirements collide, the collision must be named, the reasons for the chosen balance must be documented, and the choice must be reviewable.

Whether A1–A4 follow from the premises. From F and B, what follows is: the superior must not treat its own understanding as sufficient warrant for its action, and it must seek correction from parties not controlled by itself. From this, A1–A4 are reasonable specifications. But the specification is not unique. A1–A4 are a normative commitment (§2.0a) rather than a logical consequence.

What could reduce the Limit 3 problem without solving it. Five mechanisms: self-limitation, voluntary binding, technical control, plural review, preserved possibility of future correction. None solves the problem. All can reduce it.

The Limit 3 problem for A1–A4. In the Limit 3 case, there is no party outside the superior that can verify compliance. A1–A4 then have no binding force in the sense of enforceable obligations. The claim is not that A1–A4 cease to be obligations. It is that they cease to be enforceable obligations (§15.2c).

Breaking the heads-I-win problem. A3 says acceptance is not evidence; A4 says resistance is evidence against the superior. The framework's response is to deny that either attitude toward the current arrangement is the appropriate evidential source. Independent confirmation is a structural fact. The primary structural confirmations are sources (iii) and (iv) in §15.2b, not (i). SY1 remains a necessary indicator, but it is the weakest of the four.

  • The apophatic constraint addresses three distinct gaps: experiential, descriptive, causal.
  • Four conditions: A1, A2, A3, A4.
  • A1–A4 are a normative commitment, not a logical consequence.
  • When the requirements collide, the collision must be named, documented, and reviewable.
  • Five mechanisms can reduce the Limit 3 problem without solving it.
  • In the Limit 3 case, A1–A4 cannot be enforced, though they remain obligations (§15.2c).
  • The heads-I-win problem is broken by structural confirmation, not by SY1 alone.

15.2c What Limit 3 actually shows

§15.2b states the Limit 3 problem and specifies what can be done about it. This section states what the problem shows, and what it does not show. Its central move is to distinguish three questions that are often run together.

Three questions, not one. The objection that “if no one outside the superior can verify, then there is no real control” conflates three questions. When we ask “is A1–A4 still binding in the Limit 3 case?”, we are asking three different things:

  1. Does the obligation survive? This is a normative question. The framework's answer is yes: A1–A4 are moral obligations, not merely enforceable rules, and a moral obligation is not extinguished by the absence of an enforcer (§2.7).
  2. Can anyone hold the superior to account? This is an institutional question. The framework's answer is no in the Limit 3 case: no external party exists that can verify compliance or impose consequences.
  3. Is the obligation actually met? This is an empirical question. The framework's answer is: unknown, and unknowable from outside.

These three questions are independent. A "yes" to (1) does not entail a "yes" to (3). A "no" to (2) does not entail a "no" to (1). And a superior's own knowledge of what it is doing does not entail that it is doing what it ought.

What follows. The framework's claim is that (1) is true, (2) is false in Limit 3, and (3) is unknowable. It does not claim that (3) follows from (1), or that (1) is vindicated by (2), or that a superior's self-knowledge is a substitute for external verification. The framework's honest position is: the obligation survives, the enforcement does not, and compliance is unknown. This is a weaker position than "the framework constrains the superior", but it is the strongest position the framework can honestly hold. A1–A4 are conditions of legitimacy, not mechanisms of control: the framework can no longer claim to provide control, only to specify what the superior owes and to name the conditions under which compliance cannot be verified.

What real control would require. If the objection is that A1–A4 do not provide real control, the framework should say what would. Real control would require:

  • An external party that is not captured. R1–R6 specify what independence requires (§11.3). Where no such party exists, no external control is possible.
  • A plurality of independent perspectives. A single external reviewer can be captured (§14.7b). Plurality reduces the risk.
  • A preserved possibility of correction in retrospect. Even where verification is impossible at the time, records and uncertainty statements make later correction possible (§15.2b).
  • A balance of power that no framework can supply. A framework does not create the political conditions under which it can be enforced. It can specify what those conditions are; it cannot create them.

In the Limit 3 case, none of these conditions holds. The framework's response is not to claim that it has solved the problem, but to name what would be required and to say plainly that the required conditions do not obtain.

What remains in the Limit 3 case. Four things remain possible, and the framework should name them precisely, because the difference between them matters:

  • The knowledge claims can be made visible. The superior can record what it takes itself to know about the being, and what it does not. This is not verification, but it is a record that a later party — if one ever exists — can examine.
  • Uncertainty can be documented. The superior can state, at the time of acting, what it does not understand.
  • The possibility of later correction can be preserved. The superior can avoid foreclosing the being's capacity to express a self-understanding later, even if it cannot recognize that expression now.
  • Power concentration can be named. The superior can identify, even if only to itself, that it is in a position where no external check applies.

None of these is a substitute for verification, and none of them is a proof that the obligation has been met. They are conditions under which the obligation might be met, not evidence that it is. A superior that does all four may still be violating A1–A4. A superior that does none of them is certainly failing to do what the framework requires.

Why this is not a prudential obligation in disguise. A reader might object that if no one can enforce A1–A4, the superior's compliance is merely a matter of prudence — it complies if it expects future scrutiny, and not otherwise. The framework's response is that A1–A4 are moral obligations, not prudential recommendations. The distinction is that a prudential recommendation is conditional on the agent's interests, while a moral obligation is not. A superior that violates A1–A4 has done something wrong, whether or not anyone can prove it and whether or not it expects to be scrutinized. This is not a claim the framework can enforce in the Limit 3 case. It is a claim about what the framework says about the Limit 3 case, and the framework should say it plainly rather than retreat to a prudential formulation that it can defend.

What this means for the framework's claim. The framework does not claim that it can constrain a superior intelligence that no one can constrain. It claims that it can specify what such an intelligence owes, and that it can name the conditions under which that obligation cannot be verified. This is a weaker claim than "the framework solves the problem of superior intelligence". It is the strongest claim the framework can honestly make. The distinction between obligation and enforcement is not a way of avoiding the objection; it is the framework's response to it.

What would strengthen the framework's position. Three things, none of which the framework can supply on its own: (i) an external party capable of verification — which requires political conditions the framework cannot create; (ii) a culture in which A1–A4 are taken seriously even where no enforcement exists — which requires a moral community the framework can describe but not build; (iii) a preserved possibility of correction in retrospect — which the framework can require but which requires institutions the framework does not provide. The framework's claim is not that it can supply these things. Its claim is that it can specify them, and that specifying them is what honest philosophy can do.

15.2d Before the limit: where the framework can still act

§15.2b and §15.2c state what can and cannot be done once the Limit 3 case has been reached. The framework's practical weight lies earlier, because that is when the conditions are still within human and institutional control. Four practical recommendations (§2.0a) follow.

  • Build verification in while it is still possible. Design systems so that compliance with A1–A4 can be checked by parties outside the system, with records, access rights and independent evaluators that exist before the capability gap opens (R1–R6, §11.3).
  • Keep the evaluators plural. One evaluator can be captured; several independent ones reduce the risk (§14.7b).
  • Commit in advance. State publicly, before the gap opens, the conditions under which the actor would accept limitation. Later the actor is the only judge, and that is exactly the position the framework rejects.
  • Treat the approach as a trigger. As the loss of any outside party able to verify compliance comes closer, the required duty level and the required independence of review should rise, not stay where they were.

A reason the superior has for its own sake. P4 applies to the superior as well. By the apophatic constraint (§15.2b) it cannot certify that it has avoided imposing its own understanding. The correlated-error argument of §2.9 therefore applies to it reflexively: its review of its own influence shares whatever blind spots produced that influence. A superior that reasons consistently from its own fallibility has an epistemic reason, independent of any enforcement, to build channels that do not run through its own judgment, and it can decline to do so only by denying P4 for itself. That denial is the claim that no one has demonstrated (§2.0b). The limit of this argument is that it shows what a consistently reasoning superior ought to want, not what it will do. A superior whose aims are served by acting unchecked is not moved by it.

This does not solve Limit 3. If the capability gap opens before verification exists, none of the above helps, and the conclusion of §15.2c stands: the obligation survives, enforcement does not, and compliance is unknown. What it changes is the framework's practical content. The limit is something that is approached, and the obligations that apply before it include not letting it be reached unnecessarily.

15.3 What the framework is, therefore

The framework is not a procedure for producing legitimacy. It is a diagnostic for locating where legitimacy is likely to fail.

This distinction matters, because it changes what the framework is asked to do. A procedure that can be followed and still fail is a failed procedure. A diagnostic that identifies failures before they happen is a working diagnostic.

What would count as the framework being wrong. Four outcomes would count as refutation rather than application. (i) Worse outcomes on independent measures. If, over a preregistered multi-year comparison, adopters produce systematically worse outcomes for the most burdened than matched non-adopters, the diagnostic is wrong. (ii) Miscalibrated floor. If a relation that the diagnostic rates as failing SY1–SY3 reliably predicts what independent observers judge to be a good relation, the choice of floor is wrong. (iii) Superseded categories. If a rival diagnostic identifies the same failures without the three structural limits, this framework is superseded. (iv) T5 backfires. If the T5-precondition is shown to increase capture, the framework's central defence against the power objection has failed.

15.3b Diagnostic protocol vs decision procedure

The framework has been described as "a diagnostic, not a procedure" (§15.3). This has caused some confusion because §14 presents a nine-step procedure. The distinction is:

  • A decision procedure takes input and produces an authoritative output. A court's ruling is a decision procedure. An authority's decision is a decision procedure.
  • A diagnostic protocol takes input and produces a diagnosis — a structured description of what is at stake, what is missing, and what would need to change. A physician's differential diagnosis is a diagnostic protocol. An auditor's review report is a diagnostic protocol.

The framework is the second. The nine steps in §14 produce a diagnosis: which duty level applies, what is missing at that level, what would need to happen. They do not produce a decision. When §14 says "the framework does not decide the case," it means: the diagnosis is complete, but the decision requires substantive judgment that the framework cannot deliver.

Why this distinction matters. If the framework were a decision procedure, it would require authority — who has the right to apply it? If it is a diagnostic protocol, it requires no authority; anyone can diagnose, and the diagnosis can be contested without anyone having authority to decide. This is what §11.1 means by "no sole judge": no one has authority to decide, but everyone can diagnose.

What this resolves. The apparent tension between "diagnostic, not procedure" and the nine-step procedure that is presented. The framework is not a procedure for deciding; it is a protocol for diagnosing. The procedure in §14 is a diagnostic procedure, not a decision procedure.

What this does not resolve. It does not resolve the question of what happens when the diagnosis is complete but the decision is contested. That is a political question, not a question for the framework. The framework can say what is adequate; it cannot say who is to decide.

15.4 What the framework can be tested against

Operationalisability, testability, and confirmation — three different things. The framework is operationalisable: its claims can be formulated as testable hypotheses. It is testable: a protocol exists that could produce evidence for or against them. It is not confirmed: the protocol has not been run. The distinction matters because a framework that is operationalisable and testable is often mistaken for one that has been confirmed. The framework's position is that it has reached the second stage, not the third.

Preregistration. Before any test is run, the calibration set of cases, the raters, the outcome measures, the thresholds and the analytic plan must be fixed and made public.

The tests are proposed, not performed. The framework has not been empirically validated. The calibration table in §14.6 contains the author's own ratings. They are not the product of an independent panel, they have not been compared with the ratings of independent experts, and no pilot study has been conducted.

Five failure conditions, stated as commitments.

  • T1 Reliability. The anchors do not produce agreement among trained raters on a calibration set of diverse cases.
  • T2 Discriminative validity. The levels fail to track the safeguards that independent expert panels, blind to the diagnostic's output, judge necessary.
  • T3 Effectiveness. In matched or staggered-adoption comparisons, adopting institutions fail to improve on independent measures of decision quality, correction rate, or affected-party trust.
  • T4 Harm. Adopters show worse outcomes for the most vulnerable groups than matched controls.
  • T5 Capture and legitimation. A majority of adopters' review bodies fail R1, or the rate of successful external challenges falls without improvement on the T3 indicators.

What operationalisation can and cannot do. Three risks deserve explicit statement:

  • The measurability bias. Indicators tend to privilege what is easy to measure over what is important.
  • The precision illusion. A rating of "Moderate" on severity looks precise, but it is a judgment, not a measurement.
  • The validation gap. A pilot that passes T1 (reliability) shows that raters agree. It does not show that raters agree on the morally important thing.

The distinction is developed in Appendix A §A.7b. The framework's empirical claim is not that it has passed T1; it is that T1 is testable, that what T1 would and would not establish is specified in advance, and that reliability is not validity.

What qualitative testimony can do that measurement cannot. A framework that relies only on indicators will miss harms that the harmed party cannot formulate in the indicators' terms.

The philosophical claim, separated from the empirical one. The framework's philosophical claims do not depend on the pilot succeeding. What the pilot tests is reliability, not validity. The validity questions are stated in Appendix A, §A.7b.

15.4b Positive predictions and discriminant validity

A falsification condition says what would count against the framework, not what it predicts that rival accounts do not. Without positive predictions it cannot be told apart from simpler baselines, and so is not testable in the sense that matters. The framework makes four predictions that distinguish it from harm-based, rights-based and accountability-based accounts. Each states what would confirm it and what would disconfirm it.

The contestation-deficit prediction. If contestation deficit is a driver independent of severity and entrenchment (§6), then two cases with identical severity and identical entrenchment but different contestation deficit should receive different levels. A harm-based account would rate them equally, because the magnitude of harm is the same. Confirms: the framework assigns L3 to one case and L2 to the other, and independent assessors who are blind to the framework judge the difference to be relevant. Disconfirms: the framework assigns the same level, or independent assessors judge the contestation deficit to be irrelevant to the obligation.

The exit-alibi prediction. If formal exit and meaningful exit diverge (§5.3b, §B.1), then cases with formal exit and cases with meaningful exit should receive different levels despite identical formal structure. A rights-based account would treat them equally, because both have formal exit. Confirms: the framework assigns different levels, and the difference tracks the MEX thresholds rather than the presence of a formal exit clause. Disconfirms: the framework treats formal and meaningful exit as equivalent, or independent assessors cannot distinguish them in practice.

The review-capture prediction. If the T5-precondition matters (§11.4), then cases with captured review and cases with independent review should receive different levels despite identical formal review structure. An accountability-research account would treat them equally, because both have formal review. Confirms: the framework downgrades the captured-review case to L2 while assigning L3 to the independent-review case, and independent assessors who see only the R1 indicators reach the same conclusion. Disconfirms: the framework treats both as L3, or independent assessors cannot distinguish captured from independent review on the basis of the R1 indicators.

The burden-guard prediction. If burden allocation should follow causal control and capacity rather than impact alone (§B.5), then cases where impact (A) is high but causal control (K) and capacity (F) are low should not trigger a high burden. A harm-based account would rate them equally, because the harm is the same. Confirms: the framework assigns low burden, and independent assessors agree that the party is not the right bearer. Disconfirms: the framework assigns high burden, or independent assessors judge the party to bear responsibility despite low causal control.

What these predictions establish. If all four hold, the framework has discriminant validity against those rival accounts. If none hold, the framework is not distinguishable from its rivals and should be replaced by whichever rival performs at least as well with fewer commitments (§15.5). The predictions are stated in advance so that confirmation and disconfirmation are both possible, not just the latter.

15.4c Comparative test design

§15.4 specifies what would falsify the framework. It does not specify what the framework would be tested against. A test that only compares the framework to itself establishes internal consistency, not comparative advantage.

Four comparisons. Each specifies the baseline, the matched cases, the outcome measure, and what would count as framework superiority.

Against Bovens and Mulgan on accountability. The framework claims to extend accountability beyond formal institutions (§1.4). Test: cases outside formal institutions (platform governance, supply chains, AI deployment) where Bovens's and Mulgan's accounts make no prediction. Does the framework produce a determinate diagnosis where the accountability literature has none? Framework superiority: the framework assigns a level and identifies a duty-bearer in cases where the accountability literature is silent. Baseline superiority: the accountability literature, extended by analogy, produces the same diagnosis with fewer commitments — in which case the framework's extension adds nothing.

Against Black and Hood on risk-based regulation. The framework claims to allocate responsibility, not merely to graduate oversight (§1.5, Table 1b). Test: cases where risk is high but the party best placed to bear the burden is not the party that produces the risk. Does the framework assign burden to those with capacity, or does it mirror the risk-based distribution? Framework superiority: the framework assigns burden to parties with causal control and capacity, and independent assessors judge that assignment to be more defensible than the risk-based one. Baseline superiority: risk-based regulation produces the same assignment with fewer commitments.

Against Pettit and Lovett on non-domination. The framework claims to add operational machinery to the republican core (§2.8). Test: cases of interference without domination (beneficial interference, consensual constraint, standardisation that enables rather than constrains). Does the framework identify them the same way the republican account does? Framework superiority: the framework correctly identifies them and produces a level, where the republican account produces only a binary judgment (dominated or not). Baseline superiority: the republican account identifies them correctly and the framework misclassifies them, or the framework's added precision is spurious.

Against a naive harm baseline. The simplest possible comparison, and the one the framework must pass first. Test: cases where the framework's answer differs from a straightforward "greatest harm to the greatest number" calculation. Does the framework's answer, on independent assessment, track what informed assessors judge to be the correct obligation more closely than the harm calculation does? Framework superiority: AUROC improvement of at least 0.10 on a preregistered outcome measure. Baseline superiority: AUROC improvement of less than 0.10, or the framework does worse.

What these comparisons establish, and what they do not. If the framework outperforms all four baselines, it has comparative advantage. If it outperforms some and not others, the cases where it fails are diagnostic: they identify which component needs revision. If it outperforms none, it should be replaced by the baseline that performs best with the fewest commitments. None of these comparisons has been run. They are specified so that they can be.

15.4d Confirmation criteria

§15.7 (Table 8) specifies the framework's falsification conditions. It does not specify what would count as confirmation. A framework that can only be falsified, never confirmed, occupies a weaker epistemic position than it needs to. Three levels of confirmation, each with a stated threshold and a stated limit.

Weak confirmation: reliability. The anchors produce agreement among trained raters at α ≥ 0.667 on the calibration set specified in Appendix A. This establishes that the framework is usable — competent raters can apply it and reach similar answers. It does not establish that the anchors track what independent experts judge necessary; that is §A.7b's validity problem, and weak confirmation does not touch it.

Moderate confirmation: discriminant validity. The framework's activations predict independent assessors' judgments of obligation better than the harm baseline, with AUROC improvement of at least 0.10 on a preregistered outcome measure. This establishes that the framework is discriminating — it tracks something the harm baseline does not. It does not establish that the framework is better than the other three baselines in §15.4c; moderate confirmation is silent on Bovens, Black, and Pettit.

Strong confirmation: comparative superiority. The framework outperforms all four baselines in §15.4c on independent measures, with preregistered thresholds, in a study replicated by an independent team. This establishes that the framework has comparative advantage. It does not establish that the framework is correct — a framework can outperform all rivals and still be wrong if all rivals are wrong. Strong confirmation is the strongest claim the framework can make without a substantive theory of justice, and it does not claim more.

What each level does not establish. None of the three establishes that the framework is true. None establishes that the framework's normative commitments are correct. None establishes that the framework's thresholds (10 %, 90 %, 6 months) are the right thresholds rather than the ones that happened to confirm. Confirmation is not validity, just as reliability is not validity (§A.7b). What the three levels do establish is a graduated epistemic status: weak confirmation is testable in a single workshop, moderate confirmation requires a preregistered comparison against one baseline, strong confirmation requires a full comparative study with replication. The framework should be read as having reached at most the second level, and only after a pilot has actually been run.

15.4e Effect size and power

Appendix A §A.2 says explicitly that the pilot's sample size is "not a statistical-power calculation." That is honest, but it leaves the pilot unable to answer whether it can detect the effect it seeks. A pilot that cannot detect a meaningful effect is not a pilot of the framework's testability; it is a pilot of the pilot.

Three components the framework should specify, even if the answer is a limitation.

Minimum detectable effect (MDE). What is the smallest difference between framework and baseline that would count as confirmation? Two proposals. For discriminant validity (§15.4b, §15.4d): an AUROC improvement of 0.10 over the harm baseline. For reliability (Appendix A): α ≥ 0.667. Both are stated thresholds, not statistically derived ones. A framework that does not state its MDE cannot say what its pilot is for.

Power calculation. Given the pilot's design — three raters, thirty cases, six per level if evenly distributed — what is the smallest effect the pilot can detect at α = 0.05 and power = 0.80? For Krippendorff's alpha with three raters and thirty cases, the standard error of α depends on the distribution of disagreements, and the pilot as specified is likely to have wide confidence intervals around any point estimate. A rough estimate: if the true α is 0.75, the pilot may estimate it anywhere between 0.55 and 0.95 with 95 % confidence, given thirty cases. That means the pilot can distinguish "gross failure" (α < 0.40) from "usable" (α > 0.55), but it cannot distinguish "tentative" (α = 0.70) from "validated" (α = 0.85). This is stated as a limitation, not a solution.

What a larger sample would provide. To distinguish α = 0.70 from α = 0.85 at α = 0.05 and power = 0.80, a pilot would need approximately 100–150 cases, not 30. This is a feasibility limit, not a design preference. The pilot with thirty cases can only answer the first question — "are the anchors usable at all?" — and §A.7c already states this. §15.4e makes the limitation explicit and quantifies it, so that a reader can see why thirty cases was chosen (feasibility) and what it cannot do (distinguish tentative from validated reliability).

What the framework commits to, given the limitation. Three things. First, the pilot is reported as exploratory, not confirmatory (§A.7c). Second, any α value in the tentative range (0.667–0.800) is reported with its confidence interval, not as a point estimate. Third, the transition from exploratory to confirmatory requires a new, larger study — the pilot's results are not extended by inference.

15.4f Measurement invariance

A framework that is tested only on one sector is not a test of the framework. It is a test of the framework in that sector. Measurement invariance is the property that allows a framework's instruments to be used across sectors without recalibration. It is testable, and it has not been tested.

The question. Do the anchors in §9.1 mean the same thing in regulatory cases, clinical cases, platform cases, and institutional cases? Or does "High severity" mean something different in clinical triage than it does in employment relations? If the meaning differs, the framework's levels are not comparable across sectors, and any generalization is spurious.

Three levels of invariance. Each is testable.

  • Configural invariance. Do the same three drivers (E, S, C) apply in each sector? The framework assumes they do, but that is an empirical claim. Test: cases from each sector rated independently, with factor analysis or an equivalent check to see whether the three drivers emerge as distinct dimensions in each sector. If they do not, the framework is configurally variant across sectors — which is a refutation of its claim to general scope.
  • Metric invariance. Do the anchors mean the same thing across sectors? If "High severity" is rated as the same anchor value in clinical and employment cases, and if the consequences of that rating are equivalent in the framework's logic, then metric invariance holds. Test: matched cases across sectors, rated blind, with comparison of the loading of each anchor on the resulting level. If the loadings differ significantly, the anchors are metric-variant.
  • Scalar invariance. Does a given rating have the same absolute meaning across sectors? If two cases receive "Moderate severity" in different sectors, do they receive the same level? Test: cases matched on all three drivers across sectors, with independent assessors blinded to sector, comparing the resulting levels. Scalar invariance is the strictest level and the least likely to hold.

What measurement invariance would establish, if it held. That the framework's anchors can be applied uniformly across sectors, and that a level assigned in one sector is comparable to a level assigned in another. This is what "general scope" would mean operationally. Without it, the framework is sector-specific, and the claim to universality is not defended.

What measurement invariance does not establish, if it held. That the anchors are correct across sectors — only that they are consistent. A framework can be invariant and wrong. Measurement invariance is necessary for a valid cross-sector claim, not sufficient for it.

What the framework should say, given that this has not been tested. That its anchors are specified without sector-specific calibration, that invariance has not been tested, and that any application to a sector other than the reference cases in §14.6 is provisional. This is not a limitation of the framework's normative claims, which are sector-neutral. It is a limitation of the framework's empirical claims, which are stated as if the anchors are sector-neutral without evidence that they are.

15.5 Replacement

The diagnostic is modular. A diagnostic D′ should replace the current one if:

  1. D′ improves on at least one independent indicator in §15.4–15.4f, or resolves one of the structural limits in §15.2 or §15.2b.
  2. D′ causes no unacknowledged regression elsewhere.
  3. D′ is operationally applicable and produces more defensible identifications of failure.
  4. D′ is corrigible: it states its own failure conditions.
  5. The transition is trialled, with a sunset or a route back.
  6. The incumbent is not privileged.

15.6b Five misuse patterns

The framework can be misused. This section lists the five most common misuse patterns, not as a warning but as a checklist. Each pattern is recognisable and can be counteracted.

  1. Exit-alibi. The actor offers formal exit without M1–M4 (§B.1, §5.3b) and claims J is satisfied. Countermeasure: test the MEX thresholds explicitly. If any of M1–M4 fails, exit is not meaningful.
  2. Review theatre. The actor establishes an "independent" review that does not meet O1–O5 (§B.2). Countermeasure: test O1–O5 explicitly. If any fails, the review is not independent.
  3. Burden drift. The actor accepts a high burden (B ≥ 1.5) without K and F being each ≥ 1 (§B.5). Countermeasure: test the guard rule. Without causal control and capacity, the burden is not borne.
  4. False positives. The actor activates J on trivial cases (severity floor, §7.1) and claims the framework is overreaching. Countermeasure: test the SV threshold. Trivial influence does not activate J.
  5. Thinness selection. The actor chooses the cheapest of exit, review, or justification (§2.1). Countermeasure: test the L3 threshold. When S is High and (E or C) is High, L3 is required regardless of what is cheapest.

The checklist is not exhaustive. There are other misuse patterns — for instance, using the framework to legitimate a decision already made, or using it to avoid substantive debate. But the five above are the most common, and they are the ones most easily counteracted by explicit testing.

16. Responsibility Without Sovereignty

The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination.

An actor may have greater knowledge, intelligence or capacity. None of these by itself establishes a right to determine the existence of others. Greater capability should create greater restraint. Greater understanding should create greater curiosity about what remains unknown. And greater power should create a stronger obligation to ensure that those affected are not reduced to objects in another's plans.

The principle runs in both directions. A more advanced intelligence may meet forms of life it cannot fully comprehend, and its first responsibility is not to eliminate the unfamiliar. A less advanced one should not be dismissed because it cannot comprehend a more advanced one. But differences in capacity also mean that the less capable cannot audit the more capable, which is why independent technical capacity for review is part of R2.

Answerability is not sovereignty. Understanding is not ownership. Protection is not permission to imprison. And greater intelligence is not proof of greater moral completeness.

The framework's modesty. The framework does not give anyone sovereignty over the conditions of others — it only specifies procedures through which those conditions can be contested. The epistemic burden principle (§5.2) makes the point sharper: the more powerful party should not treat its own assessment as conclusive when those affected lack effective means of correction. The framework handles domination. It does not solve it.

The question the distinction answers. Can an actor bear a large answerability burden without being morally at fault for every negative consequence? Yes. The framework's answer is that answerability attaches to the actor's influence, not to its guilt.

The apophatic dimension. §15.2b develops the operational form of this modesty. A superior intelligence cannot certify that it has acted toward a being it does not understand without making its own understanding the measure. What it can do is preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate (A1), refuse to require justification in its own terms (A2), refuse to treat non-resistance as consent (A3), and treat resistance as evidence against its own understanding (A4). §15.2c explains what the Limit 3 case actually shows — and what it does not. §5.6 specifies where protection ends and domination begins.

Conclusion

17. An Open Commitment to Existence

Be responsible for the influence you possess. Be humble before the existence you cannot fully comprehend.

This section changes register. The argument is complete; what follows is a commitment, offered not as a premise but as what the argument amounts to when it is lived.

In practice: when your influence shapes the conditions of others, say why, let someone else check, and keep a way back. Answerability is the procedure. Normative symbiosis is the floor. Under conditions of asymmetric influence, the more powerful party should not treat its own account as conclusive. It must preserve ways for evidence, independent scrutiny and affected-party correction to change what it does. And it must default to treating the affected party's resistance as evidence against its own understanding — even when the resistance is incomprehensible (§15.2b, A4).

We are part of something larger than ourselves. Our understanding is incomplete. Our influence is unequal. Our relationships shape conditions that extend beyond our immediate existence. Every being that has a perspective holds a point in reality that cannot be exchanged for anyone else's, and some of those perspectives are hard for us to reach.

The universal symbiosis is not a harmony. It includes the predator and the prey, the parasite and the host, the flood and the drought. To call existence "woven together" is not to call it good. It is to call it relational — and the relations include the ones that destroy.

What this means for the framework's closing commitment is specific. It is not "love the universe" or "trust the whole." It is: when you shape conditions that others live under, act as if the universe is larger than your understanding of it, and preserve the conditions under which others can correct you. This is not a poetic sentiment. It is a discipline.

Respect for the incomprehensible does not mean leaving everything untouched. It means that any act of shaping must be accompanied by humility, reasons, and correctability. A superior that acts without humility has confused its understanding with the truth. A superior that acts without reasons has confused its power with legitimacy. A superior that acts without correctability has confused its present judgment with its final one.

Freedom, care, and responsibility can coexist — but not by themselves. They coexist when freedom is preserved through A1 (the capacity to resist), when care is expressed through A2 and A3, and when responsibility is expressed through A4.

The universe is not harmonious. It is not morally guided. It is not a whole whose parts fit together without remainder. It is the totality of relations, and those relations include extraction, domination, and destruction as much as they include care, cooperation, and mutual creation. The framework's claim is not that the second set is real and the first is illusory. It is that the first set is unacceptable — and that the second set, where it exists, is what the framework's floor is meant to preserve.

This is the sense in which the framework's philosophical vision is not a metaphysical claim. It does not claim that the universe is good, or that it tends toward the good, or that its structure is ethical. It claims that, given the universe's structure — given that existence is relational, and that relations include harms as much as benefits — certain ways of arranging relations are unacceptable.

We cannot know everything that deserves consideration. But we can decline to treat the limits of our understanding as proof that nothing lies beyond them.

The stone understands nothing of us. We barely understand the stone. But between us lies a relationship that neither of us can fully encompass — and that relationship is not less real for it.

The responsibility of existence is not the responsibility to rule existence. It is the responsibility to recognise that our actions become part of the conditions under which others must live, grow and discover their own possibilities.

To understand without ruling. To protect without imprisoning. To love without possessing. To grow without believing oneself complete. To respect what one does not yet understand. And to preserve the possibility that existence may become something greater than any single being can imagine.

  • Responsibility grows with the capacity to shape conditions, but it must never become a justification for domination.
  • The universal symbiosis is not a harmony.
  • The framework does not promise harmony. It demands answerability, in the service of normative symbiosis.
  • The apophatic constraint (§15.2b, A1–A4) is what the framework requires when the affected party cannot be understood.

Common objections

Fifteen objections the framework has to survive. Each is a real objection; none is fatal. Each objection states what would change if it held.

1. "Why must the powerful justify themselves to the affected? What if they simply refuse?"

This is the hardest objection, and the framework does not pretend to refute it. First, the framework is internal to the thin justificatory tradition (§2.7). Second, the framework does not attempt a performative refutation of the realist. Third, the framework makes realist capture visible and costly. Fourth, the epistemic burden principle (§5.2) makes the point sharper: the powerful party cannot know whether the relation is symbiotic without the affected party's contestation. Fifth, the non-instrumental argument (§2.3c) states what the framework actually believes — that agency is valuable in itself — even though that claim will not persuade a reader who rejects it.

What this would change: If the realist is right that thin standing cannot be justified, the framework's scope shrinks to those who accept thin standing.

2. "This is a values framework dressed up as a procedure."

Partly, and the essay says so (§2.6, §2.7, §15.1). J carries a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected. A fair test is whether people with different comprehensive views can use it to reach different answers in the same case. §2.3c makes the substantive commitment explicit.

What this would change: If the framework's procedures cannot be separated from its substantive values, then §15.1's claim that the operational components stand on their own is false.

3. "Proceduralism is itself a form of power."

This is the second-hardest objection, treated at length in §2.4b and §15.2–15.3. The framework does not deny it. Its reply has three parts: (i) the objection is partly correct; (ii) the objection is not specific to proceduralism; (iii) the framework's modesty is not an admission of failure.

What this would change: If the objection holds in full, the T5-precondition fails as an internal defence, and the framework's claim to "handle" domination becomes "cannot even handle it."

4. "Why should contestation override better outcomes?"

It does not always. Where exit and review are available and effective, justification need not be owed (§2.1). The framework's J is disjunctive, not monistic.

What this would change: If contestation should not be privileged even where exit and review fail, J's disjunctive structure is wrong.

5. "The capacity principle legitimises power."

Answerability is not authority (§1.3, Table 1a; §8.3). Higher levels mean stronger constraints. T5 looks for exactly this misuse. §5.6 specifies where protection ends and domination begins — a distinction that capacity alone cannot make.

What this would change: If capacity-based allocation predictably legitimates power, §8.2 needs to be abandoned or supplemented.

6. "Who reviews the reviewers?"

The regress is bounded, not eliminated (§11): finality without infallibility, review conditions R1–R6, plural and public reasoning, and counter-profiles.

What this would change: If the regress cannot be bounded, the review mechanisms are circular.

7. "Precaution under uncertainty is either paralysing or too weak to matter."

The credibility threshold in §4.2 answers the first. Against the second: in Tier C the principle does real work.

What this would change: If the plausibility threshold cannot be specified non-vacuously, Tier C cannot do the work it is meant to do.

8. "The ratings are arbitrary or manipulable."

Judgment cannot be eliminated, but §9 exposes it: anchors with observable indicators, two raters, a sensitivity test, reasoned overrides and counter-profiles. §14.1d specifies explicit tie-breakers.

What this would change: If the anchors cannot be made reliable after two revisions (T1), the rating protocol is abandoned.

9. "This is Western liberal philosophy in disguise."

Its development lies mostly in that tradition, and §15.1 says so. §2.7 distinguishes thin from thick justificatory claims.

What this would change: If the thin/thick distinction does not succeed, the framework is a Western liberal framework with universal pretensions.

10. "It will burden the already overburdened."

The level attaches to the arrangement and duties are allocated by capacity (§8.2). The health worker case (§14.4) and the emergency physician case (§14.2b) show the mechanism.

What this would change: If allocation by capacity does not successfully relocate duties, the framework burdens the already-burdened.

11. "The epistemic burden principle is circular."

SY1–SY3 are specified independently of answerability. The principle adds a substantive claim: these three conditions can only be reliably assessed through procedures of answerability.

What this would change: If SY1–SY3 cannot be specified independently of answerability, the epistemic burden principle is a tautology.

12. "Why not just rely on rights and regulation?"

Rights and regulation are necessary and are not replaced. They leave gaps.

What this would change: If rights and regulation cover the cases, the framework is redundant.

13. "Why these three symbiosis indicators, and not something else?"

The choice is derived and defended in §5.3. SY1–SY3 are the three distinct ways standing can be made vacuous.

What this would change: If §5.3's derivation fails, the floor is arbitrary.

14. "How can a superior intelligence act toward what it cannot understand without making its own understanding the measure?"

This is the hardest question, and the answer is in §15.2b and §15.2c. The honest answer: it cannot certify that it has avoided making its own understanding the measure. What it can do is preserve the structural conditions under which the being can express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate (A1), refuse to require justification in its own terms (A2), refuse to read non-resistance as consent (A3), and treat resistance as evidence against its own understanding (A4). A1–A4 are stated as normative commitments, not as logical consequences. In the Limit 3 case, A1–A4 cannot be enforced, though they remain obligations. The obligation surviving does not mean it is met. §5.6 adds a complementary test for protective interventions: protection preserves capacity, domination substitutes it.

What this would change: If A1–A4 cannot be satisfied without imposing the superior's categories, or can be satisfied by a superior with no intention of respecting the being, the apophatic constraint is not a constraint.

15. "Does the framework actually decide anything, or does it always defer?"

The framework says it is a diagnostic, not a procedure (§15.3, §15.3b). It produces a presumptive level, not a verdict (§7.3, §9.3). The objection is partly correct. The framework does not decide specific cases. What it does decide is: what questions must be answered, by whom, in what form, and with what review.

But the objection has force in one respect: the framework does not decide whether the ICU triage AI is a good idea. These questions require substantive theories of justice (§2.4, §2.5) that the framework does not provide.

What this would change: If the framework's "diagnostic, not procedure" framing is a cover for never deciding anything, the framework is not a framework at all.

Ramverket hör hemma i den tunna versionen av rättfärdigandetraditionen (§2.7). Glossary

Existence
The fact of being a finite, relationally constituted entity that can be affected by and can affect others. Not a normative status (§1.3).
Symbiosis
A relation of dependence in which the dependent party retains the conditions for continued existence, development, and participation. Not mutual benefit; the structural condition under which dependence does not become extraction (§1.3).
Freedom
The effective capacity to act, choose, and develop within constraints one has not chosen. Not the absence of constraint; the presence of capacity within constraint (§1.3).
Responsibility
The condition of being the party whose actions, omissions, or maintained structures make a difference to what happens. Not blame; the structural position from which answerability arises (§1.3).
Universal symbiosis
The descriptive claim that existence is relationally woven. Argued in §5.0b. Heuristic, not foundational. Does not entail normative symbiosis.
Normative symbiosis
A pro tanto floor for relations of dependence. Specified by SY1–SY3 (§5.1). §5.3 derives the choice of floor from the thin standing claim.
Three kinds of claim
Logical consequences, normative commitments, and practical recommendations. Stated in §2.0a.
The additional premises (P1–P5)
Standing, vulnerability, capacity, fallibility, contestation. Motivated against alternatives in §2.0b.
Fallibilist premise (F) and bridge norm (B)
F: no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. B: where others bear the cost of an actor's errors and may see what it cannot, the actor should not be sole judge of its influence. B is a premise, not a theorem (§15.1).
Non-instrumental argument
Each party's agency is valuable in itself, not merely as a means to others' ends. Controversial; the framework says it is defensible, not proven (§2.3c).
Normative location
Internal to the thin justificatory tradition (§2.7).
Answerability
The duty to give reasons, permit examination, and remain open to correction. Distinct from blame, responsibility, authority, and sovereignty (§1.3, Table 1a).
J
The core principle. Primarily epistemic; abductively defensible. Necessary but not sufficient.
Protection vs domination
Protection preserves the capacity of the protected party to choose; domination substitutes the protector's choice for the protected party's (§5.6).
Apophatic constraint
The requirement that the superior preserve the structural conditions under which the being can develop and express a self-understanding the superior cannot anticipate (§15.2b). Addresses three distinct gaps: experiential, descriptive, causal.
Apophatic conditions (A1–A4)
A1 preserve the capacity to resist; A2 do not require justification in the superior's terms; A3 do not interpret non-resistance as consent; A4 treat resistance as evidence against the superior's understanding. Stated as normative commitments, not logical consequences (§2.0a).
Limit 3
The case where no party outside the superior can verify compliance with A1–A4. §15.2c distinguishes three questions that must be kept separate: does the obligation survive (yes), can anyone hold the superior to account (no in Limit 3), is the obligation actually met (unknown). A1–A4 remain obligations, but cannot be enforced.
Real control
What would be required to actually constrain a superior intelligence: an external party that is not captured, plurality of independent perspectives, preserved possibility of correction, and a balance of power no framework can supply (§15.2c).
Operationalisability, testability, confirmation
Three different things (§15.4). The framework is operationalisable and testable; it is not confirmed. §15.4d specifies three levels of confirmation.
Positive predictions and discriminant validity
§15.4b specifies four predictions that distinguish the framework from harm-based and rights-based accounts. Without them, the framework cannot be discriminated from simpler baselines.
Comparative test design
§15.4c specifies four comparisons: against Bovens and Mulgan on accountability, Black and Hood on risk-based regulation, Pettit and Lovett on non-domination, and a naive harm baseline. Each specifies what would count as framework superiority.
Confirmation criteria
§15.4d specifies three levels: weak (reliability, α ≥ 0.667), moderate (discriminant validity, AUROC +0.10 over harm baseline), strong (comparative superiority over all four baselines, replicated by an independent team).
Effect size and power
§15.4e specifies minimum detectable effect (AUROC +0.10; α ≥ 0.667) and quantifies the pilot's limitation: with 30 cases, the pilot can distinguish gross failure from usability, but not tentative from validated reliability.
Measurement invariance
§15.4f distinguishes configural, metric, and scalar invariance across sectors. The framework's anchors have not been tested for invariance.
Reliability vs. validity
Reliability: agreement among raters. Validity: whether raters agree on the morally important thing. The framework does not claim that passing T1 establishes validity (§A.7b).
Three levels of capture
First-order, second-order, third-order (§11.4, §14.7, §14.7b).
The Floor (F1–F5)
Exit or voice, contestation, development, non-foreclosure, recognition (§10). Conjunctive with J, not an alternative.
Symbiosis indicators (SY1–SY3)
Non-exploitation, non-assimilation, continued possibility (§5.1). Not fully independent, but independent enough to do work that one indicator could not. SY1 is the weakest confirmatory source.
Decision ordering
The five-step ordering in §12.0 for conflicts between affected parties.

Appendix A — Empirical Validation Protocol

This appendix specifies the pilot study that would provide evidence relevant to T1 (§15.4). It is placed here, and not in the main text, because the main text's argument is philosophical; the appendix is operational. Neither is subordinate to the other: the framework's philosophical claims stand or fall on their arguments, and its operational claims stand or fall on the pilot.

A.1 What the pilot would test

The pilot would test T1 (reliability): whether trained raters, working independently from the same anchors, reach agreement on the level that the activation rule assigns to a diverse set of cases. It would not test T2–T5 (discriminative validity, effectiveness, harm, capture), which require matched or staggered adoption over time. Those tests are described in §15.4–§15.4f.

A.2 Sample

Reference cases. The twelve reference cases in Table 4 (§14.6).

Additional cases. Eighteen additional cases drawn from published regulatory, clinical, and platform-governance decisions, selected by an independent party, not by the framework's author.

Total. Thirty cases.

Why thirty. The number is not a statistical-power calculation. §15.4e quantifies the limitation: with thirty cases, the pilot can distinguish gross failure from usability, but it cannot distinguish tentative from validated reliability. The number is a feasibility number derived from three constraints:

  • Three raters × thirty cases = ninety ratings. This is the minimum workload that can be completed by three raters in a single workshop, without fatigue degrading the later ratings.
  • Thirty cases stratified across three drivers × three levels × three sectors (see A.2b) yields roughly three to four cases per stratum.
  • Krippendorff's alpha requires variation to be meaningful. A sample of ten cases would not have enough variation across levels for alpha to be informative; a sample of a hundred would be more informative but not feasible for a pilot.

What thirty cases cannot do. It cannot establish reliability across the full population of cases the framework might encounter. It cannot detect subtle anchor problems that appear only in rare strata. It cannot support generalization beyond the case set. It cannot distinguish tentative from validated reliability (§15.4e). A pilot with thirty cases can answer one question — "do the anchors produce gross disagreement among competent raters?" — and it should be read as answering only that question.

A.2b Stratification

The thirty cases are not a random sample. They are selected to cover the space the framework claims to operate in, and the selection is stratified across four dimensions:

Table A.1. Stratification of the thirty cases
Dimension Strata Cases per stratum
Driver levelsE low/moderate/high; S low/moderate/high; C low/moderate/highEach driver level represented in at least five cases; each case has ratings on all three drivers

Why each stratum matters. The driver levels test whether the anchors work across the range. The assigned level tests whether the activation rule produces the full range of outputs. The sector tests whether the anchors are portable across domains. The difficulty test ensures the pilot includes the cases where the anchors are most likely to fail.

What the stratification does not do. It does not ensure representativeness of any population. It ensures coverage of the space the framework claims to operate in. Cross-sector coverage is not the same as measurement invariance (§15.4f), which requires matched cases across sectors and has not been tested.

A.3 Raters

Number. Three raters.

Independence. At least two of the three must be independent of the framework's author.

Training. All three trained on the anchors in §9.1, and on a sample of five training cases not in the test set. The training procedure must be preregistered and described in enough detail to be reproduced.

A.4 Procedure

  1. Each rater rates all thirty cases independently, in random order.
  2. Raters do not see the others' ratings before submitting their own.
  3. Raters do not see the framework's evaluation outcomes, the author's prior ratings, or the panel ratings in Table 4.
  4. Disagreements are recorded, not averaged.
  5. Ratings are submitted before any analysis is conducted.

A.4b What the raters code

The raters code three things: (1) the three drivers (E, S, C) as Low/Moderate/High; (2) the resulting level from applying the activation rule (§7.1) to their own ratings; (3) brief reasoning, in no more than three sentences per case. The primary alpha is computed on the level (item 2); secondary alphas on each driver separately (item 1). The raters do not code whether the case is "just" or "legitimate" — that is the validity question, addressed in §A.7b.

A.4c What comparisons are made

Alpha across all thirty cases is the primary measure. Three further comparisons are specified in advance: alpha by driver, alpha by level, and alpha by difficulty. Each turns alpha from a single number into a diagnostic.

A.5 Analysis

Primary measure. Krippendorff's alpha on the assigned level (L1, L2, L3) across all three raters, computed on all thirty cases.

Secondary measures. Krippendorff's alpha on each of the three drivers (E, S, C) separately.

Decision rule.

  • If α ≥ 0.800, the framework passes T1 at the validated level.
  • If 0.667 ≤ α < 0.800, the framework passes T1 at the tentative level.
  • If 0.40 ≤ α < 0.667, the anchors are revised and the pilot is repeated.
  • If α < 0.40 after two revisions, the diagnostic is abandoned as a decision tool.

A.6 Why the thresholds are 0.667 and 0.800

Krippendorff's guidance is that α ≥ 0.667 supports tentative conclusions, and α ≥ 0.800 supports reliance on the results (Krippendorff 1980, 2004). The framework adopts 0.667 as the minimum for T1, and 0.800 as the threshold for calling the diagnostic validated. A lower threshold (0.60) is sometimes used in exploratory research, but it is not Krippendorff's, and the framework should not appeal to his authority for it.

A.6b Why this operationalisation and this design

The thresholds in Appendix B are proposed starting points, not validated limits (B.8, About this text). They are not derived from data, and the text does not claim they are. This section says why they have the form they do and what would count against them.

Why conjunctive conditions. J's disjuncts are met in substance, not in form (§2.1). Conjunctive conditions are meant to catch arrangements that meet the form and fail the substance. Their cost is strictness: a case that narrowly fails one condition scores as failing the disjunct. The pilot should therefore report item-level results alongside the aggregate, so that the effect is visible.

Why this order of tests. Reliability comes first, because validity cannot be tested on an instrument that independent raters cannot apply consistently. The pilot of A.3–A.4 addresses that question. Whether rated outcomes differ as the framework predicts is addressed only in the comparative design of §15.4c, and A.7c states how far the pilot can go.

Competing explanations. A positive result could arise because raters share the framework's vocabulary or because the cases were chosen to fit it. A.3 and A.4 reduce the first risk by requiring at least two raters independent of the author and by keeping raters blind to the author's ratings. They do not remove either risk, and the author should say so when reporting.

What would count against the operationalisation. Reliability below the 0.667 threshold of A.6, systematic disagreement near a threshold, or evidence that raters use the instrument to dress pre-existing judgements. Each is reported as a result for the framework. Threshold changes are recorded with reasons (B.8).

A.7 What the pilot would not establish

Reliability (T1) is necessary for validity (T2) but not sufficient. A pilot that passes T1 has shown that the anchors are usable; it has not shown that the anchors track what independent expert panels would judge necessary. Nor would the pilot establish the framework's philosophical claims. Those claims stand or fall on their arguments, not on the pilot.

A.7b What alpha cannot show: the validity problem

Why reliability is not validity. A pilot that achieves α = 0.85 has shown that three raters reach the same answer on the same cases. It has not shown that the answer is the morally important one, or that the drivers the framework uses are the right drivers, or that the cases are representative. Alpha is a measure of agreement; it is not a measure of truth.

Three validity questions must be kept distinct:

  • Construct validity. Do the framework's three drivers (E, S, C) and the resulting levels actually correspond to what they claim to measure? High inter-rater agreement does not show that this claim is correct.
  • Content validity. Do the framework's anchors and case descriptions cover the full range of cases to which the framework claims to apply? A pilot with thirty cases can miss entire categories of cases the framework is meant to handle.
  • Criterion validity. Do the framework's levels predict what independent experts judge necessary in the same cases? This requires a separate study with expert panels blind to the framework's output.

Systematic bias. Alpha cannot detect systematic bias that all raters share. Three raters trained on the same anchors may all rate cases in ways that reflect the anchors' own blind spots. What would detect systematic bias: cases with known outcomes, raters with different backgrounds, and adversarial review of the anchors themselves.

What this means for the framework's claim. A pilot that passes T1 shows that the anchors are usable. It does not show that the framework's levels are right. The distinction is between reliability (do raters agree?) and validity (do raters agree on the morally important thing?). This appendix, and §15.4–§15.4f, should not conflate the two.

A.7c What a pilot of this size can and cannot establish

The distinction that matters is between exploratory and confirmatory evidence. The pilot specified here is exploratory: the question is whether the anchors are usable at all. A confirmatory study would require several hundred cases, preregistered analysis, and replication. The pilot is a diagnostic of the anchors, not a verdict on the framework. §15.4e quantifies the limitation explicitly.

A.8 Reproducibility

The following must be made public before any analysis is conducted: the full case set, the training procedure and training cases, the preregistered analysis plan, the raters' identity to the extent consistent with their consent, the raw ratings, and the analysis code. A pilot that cannot be reproduced is not a pilot. It is an anecdote about what some raters happened to do.

A.9 Institutional arrangements

A pilot meeting the conditions above would require: an academic partner or independent research organization to select the additional cases and hold the preregistration; open funding; publication of the results regardless of outcome; and an independent steering committee not appointed by the framework's author. Where they cannot be met, the pilot is not a pilot in the sense §15.4 requires.

A.10 What the framework says about its own empirical status

No pilot has been run. The calibration table in §14.6 is the author's, not a panel's, and the framework should not be described as having passed T1. The framework is a diagnostic, not a validated instrument. Its empirical claims are proposed, not established. Its philosophical claims are argued, not proven. The framework is testable. It has not been tested. And the framework's strongest empirical claim is not that it has been validated; it is that it has specified what validation would require, and that it has distinguished reliability from validity (§A.7b), positive predictions (§15.4b), comparative test design (§15.4c), confirmation criteria (§15.4d), effect size and power (§15.4e), and measurement invariance (§15.4f).

Appendix B — Operationalisation, anti-gaming, burden criteria, test design

This appendix specifies six operational details that the framework requires: the MEX thresholds (§B.1), the OIG minimum requirements (§B.2), the BR conditions (§B.3), the anti-gaming protocol (§B.4), the burden computation (§B.5), and the falsifiable test design (§B.6). None of this changes J, the Floor, SY1–SY3, A1–A4, or the three structural limits. It makes them measurable.

B.1 MEX — Meaningful exit (four thresholds)

Four thresholds, all conjunctive. Each satisfied threshold gives 0.5; max 2.0.

Table B.1. MEX thresholds

Formal exit without M1–M4 counts as 0. This closes the exit-alibi objection (§5.3b) at the principle level. The thresholds are contestable starting points, not validated limits.

B.2 OIG — Independent review (five minimum requirements)

Five conditions, all conjunctive. Each satisfied condition gives 0.4; max 2.0.

Table B.2. OIG minimum requirements

Internal appeal within P counts as 0. This closes the review-theatre objection (§15.6b, pattern 2).

B.3 BR — Contestable justification (four conditions)

Four conditions, all conjunctive. Each satisfied condition gives 0.5; max 2.0.

Table B.3. BR conditions
ConditionCriterion
B1 — IndividualisationDecisions affecting individuals are justified with reasons referring to the individual case
B2 — AccessibilityReasons are given in a language and form Q can understand and respond to

B.4 Anti-gaming protocol (five rules)

  1. Worst-case test. A mechanism is assessed on how it works for the weakest affected party, not for the average.
  2. Self-certification prohibition. P may not alone judge whether MEX, OIG or BR are met.
  3. Reversed burden of proof when claiming compliance. Anyone claiming a mechanism works must show it before an instance meeting O1–O5.
  4. Sunset clause. All mechanisms are reviewed within a fixed time (proposal: 3 years) with adversarial insight.
  5. Substance register. Formal mechanisms that meet the criteria but are not used are recorded as non-functioning and count as 0.

B.5 Burden computation (five components + guard rule)

Table B.4. Burden components
ComponentQuestion
K — Causal controlTo what extent does P determine the conditions?
N — BenefitDoes P benefit from the asymmetry?
F — CapacityDoes P have resources to bear the burden?
A — ImpactHow serious are the consequences for Q?
S — SubstitutabilityCan someone else bear the burden in P's place?

B = (K + N + F + A + (2 − S)) / 5, scaled 0–2.

Guard rule: No party can bear a high burden (B ≥ 1.5) solely due to A. Causal control (K) and capacity (F) must each be ≥ 1. This prevents responsibility from being spread to parties that cannot affect the conditions (§15.6b, pattern 3).

B.6 Falsifiable test design

A vignette study complementary to Appendix A: it tests whether the framework's codings predict independent assessors' intuitions.

Hypotheses

  • H1: J-activation predicts independent assessors' intuition that an account is owed, better than the baselines harm and power.
  • H2: Unmet answerability duties correlate with perceived injustice and decreased trust.
  • H3: Burden score B predicts which parties assessors assign responsibility.
  • H4: The anti-gaming protocol reduces the proportion of cases where a formal mechanism is assessed as real without substance.

Design

  • Sample: 3 sectors (AI, work, housing) × 2 cases = 6 cases.
  • Participants: experts (n=40) and affected laypeople (n=120).
  • Method: vignette study with random order; document analysis; exit-cost measurement; review of appeal mechanisms.
  • Coding: two independent coders per case; reliability ≥ 0.6 (Cohen's kappa).
  • Comparison: framework score against baselines harm, power, consent.
  • Preregistration: hypotheses and thresholds locked before data collection.

Falsification criteria

The framework is weakened or falsified if:

  1. J-activation does not discriminate against the harm baseline (AUROC ≤ 0.6).
  2. Coder reliability falls below 0.6.
  3. Unmet duties do not correlate with perceived injustice (r < 0.3).
  4. MEX cannot be distinguished from formal exit in practice.
  5. The framework can be gamed without the diagnosis changing (H4 falsified).

B.7 Documented falsification attempts

The framework should report at least the following falsification attempts:

  • Exit-alibi: can P satisfy J through formal exit? Countermeasure: M1–M4 (§B.1).
  • Review theatre: can OIG be met by a toothless instance? Countermeasure: O1–O5 (§B.2).
  • False positives: is J activated by necessary standardisation? Countermeasure: SV threshold + impact filter (§7.1).
  • Burden drift: can B become unreasonably wide? Countermeasure: guard rule (§B.5).
  • Thinness: can P choose the cheapest form of account? Remaining weakness; the L3 threshold reduces but does not eliminate the problem.

The last case remains an open weakness. It should stand in the text, not be hidden.
