Abstract

This work develops a relational account of responsibility grounded in the concept of reach: the consequential capacity of an agent, institution, or organised system to shape the conditions under which other beings exist, develop, act, and continue.

Its central proposition is that substantial, sustained, and discretionary influence over another's conditions of agency creates a prima facie obligation of answerability. The greater an entity's capacity to understand, foresee, and shape the conditions of others, the stronger its obligation to explain, justify, review, and correct the exercise of that influence.

Responsibility is not a reward for intelligence, a measure of moral superiority, or an automatic entitlement to authority. It arises from the relationship between influence, dependence, capacity, and consequence.

The framework develops six connected elements — reach, answerability, the Reflective Surface, the fiduciary structure, symbiosis, and institutional arrangements — and distinguishes eight concepts of responsibility. It operationalises reach and symbiosis as assessable structures, demonstrates its distinctive implications against sophisticated rival frameworks in three hard cases, provides a point-by-point comparison with Pettit's republicanism (the closest rival, Appendix E), and states explicitly what evidence would count against its central normative premise.

The framework does not claim to be a complete moral theory or a universal decision procedure. It claims to be a wide reflective equilibrium point: the convergence of otherwise rival traditions on a specific normative bridge between influence and answerability. Its central aspiration is that increasing capacity should be accompanied by increasing answerability.

Part I

Foundations

1.Introduction

Existence is relational. No individual, organism, institution, or intelligence exists independently of the conditions surrounding it.

Some entities possess considerably greater capacity than others to shape these conditions. A government can influence the possibilities of millions. A corporation can determine the conditions of workers and communities. A technological system can influence decisions across entire populations. A future intelligence might affect systems and forms of existence beyond present human understanding.

What obligations arise when one entity possesses substantial power to shape the conditions under which another exists?

This work argues that consequential reach creates a distinctive obligation of answerability. The obligation is not unlimited, and it does not establish that the influencing entity has authority over those affected. The central distinction is:

Influence can generate responsibility without generating sovereignty.

2.Reach

Reach is the capacity to shape the conditions, possibilities, and development of other beings. It is multidimensional and irreducible to intelligence or formal authority. Six dimensions are proposed: asymmetry, control, persistence, magnitude, scope, foreseeability.

These form a profile, not a single score. An entity may have great reach in one dimension and limited reach in another.

2.1 Necessary conditions

Distinctively reach-based answerability arises only when all three necessary conditions hold:

  • (N1) Discretion. The influencing agent could, in principle, have chosen otherwise on the relevant dimensions without external coercion.
  • (N2) Persistence. Influence operates over a duration sufficient to shape not merely an event but a condition — indicatively, across multiple decision points of the affected party.
  • (N3) Non-attributability. The affected party cannot, through their own agency alone, revise the condition.

If any necessary condition fails, no distinctively reach-based answerability arises. General moral obligations may still apply.

2.2 Modifiers

Once N1–N3 hold, three modifiers calibrate the strength and urgency of the obligation:

  • Threshold-raisers (raise the burden of justification and are never overridden by multipliers): irreversibility, catastrophic magnitude, low contestability.
  • Multipliers (calibrate strength): asymmetry, scope.
  • Lowering factor: foreseeability — where consequences are reasonably anticipatable, the obligation to explain and prevent strengthens; where not, the obligation shifts toward investigation.

2.3 The lexicographic rule (not a weighted sum)

A weighted sum of the six dimensions would (a) presuppose commensurability between incommensurable goods, (b) invite gaming of the metric, and (c) generate false precision. The framework therefore applies:

  1. Necessary-condition gate: if N1, N2, or N3 fails, no distinctively reach-based answerability.
  2. Threshold-raisers: if irreversibility or low contestability holds, obligation cannot be capped below a specified level regardless of asymmetry or scope.
  3. Multiplier calibration: asymmetry and scope adjust strength within the range left by step 2.

2.4 A worked example

Consider a designer of a platform recommendation algorithm versus an ordinary user of that platform.

  • Designer. N1: yes — design choices are discretionary. N2: yes — the algorithm conditions the user's information environment continuously. N3: yes — the user cannot revise the algorithmic condition without exiting the platform, which is costly. Threshold-raisers: low contestability (users cannot inspect or appeal the conditioning); magnitude moderate. Multipliers: high asymmetry, high scope. Conclusion: strong reach-based answerability.
  • Ordinary user. N1: yes (posting is discretionary). N2: no — one user's influence on another is not systematically conditioning. N3: no — the affected party can generally revise their exposure through their own choices. Conclusion: no distinctively reach-based answerability. Ordinary moral norms still apply.

The framework does actual work: it distinguishes the designer from the user without collapsing either into "everyone is responsible" or "no one is."

3.Existence as a Relational Condition

Existence is not merely biological survival. It includes the conditions through which beings maintain identity, pursue interests, exercise agency, form relationships, and develop.

This does not mean every relationship is beneficial or that every arrangement deserves preservation. Some relationships are exploitative; some systems preserve themselves by restricting the possibilities of others. Coexistence does not establish moral acceptability. The framework therefore examines the conditions under which existence continues, not merely that it continues.

4.The Six-Step Argument

P1 — Agency and conditions. Agents exercise influence through actions and decisions that shape the conditions under which other agents act.

P2 — Systematic conditioning. Where influence is substantial, sustained, and subject to meaningful discretion, it constitutes systematic conditioning rather than incidental causation.

P3 — Equal moral standing and justification. Where one agent systematically conditions another's meaningful possibilities for agency, the exercise of that influence creates a prima facie duty of justification to those affected.

P4 — The failure of attribution. Where the affected party cannot meaningfully attribute the conditions to their own agency, the influencing party cannot dismiss its obligation merely by pointing to formal consent or lack of protest.

P5 — The obligation of answerability. The influencing party must be able to explain, justify, and where necessary revise the exercise of its consequential reach.

C — The responsibility conclusion. Substantial, sustained, and discretionary reach creates a prima facie obligation of answerability proportionate to its extent and consequences.

4.1 The status of P3

P3 is the normative bridge on which the entire framework rests. It is therefore necessary to state its argument-type explicitly rather than leave it as an unstated intuition.

P3 is offered neither as a deduction from self-evident premises nor as a moral axiom. It is offered as a wide reflective equilibrium point, defended by two converging arguments (the convergence argument and the performative-inconsistency argument) and one critical consideration (that its denial carries a substantial argumentative cost). §4.2 develops the convergence argument; §4.3 develops the performative argument; §4.4 states what the argument does not claim; §4.5 specifies what would count against P3.

4.2 The convergence argument

Several otherwise rival moral traditions arrive independently at a principle structurally equivalent to P3:

  • Contractualism (Scanlon 1998). Principles must be justifiable to those affected; a principle that cannot be justified to a party cannot be imposed on that party.
  • Republicanism (Pettit 1997, 2012). Non-domination requires that no party hold arbitrary power over another; answerability is the institutional expression of non-arbitrariness. See Appendix E for a point-by-point comparison.
  • Care ethics (Tronto 1993; Held 2006). Where one party is dependent on another, responsiveness is a constitutive obligation, not a supererogatory virtue.
  • Kantian justification. Treating persons as ends in themselves requires that the grounds of their treatment be in principle communicable to them.
  • Discourse ethics (Habermas 1984–87). Norms are valid only if all affected could accept them under conditions of free discourse.

These traditions disagree about why P3 holds, about the scope of "affected party," and about what follows institutionally. But they converge on the claim that systematic conditioning of another's agency generates an obligation of justification to that other.

The convergence is not a deductive proof. It is a robustness argument: when independent traditions with incompatible foundations converge on a claim, the burden shifts to the denier to explain why the convergence is illusory. The framework does not claim this burden cannot be met. It claims that the burden is real.

The convergence argument is vulnerable to what §4.5 calls the verbal-convergence objection: that "justifiability," "non-arbitrariness," "responsiveness," and "acceptability" may mean incompatible things. If so, the convergence collapses into verbal similarity, and the framework would then rest on the performative argument alone.

4.3 The performative-inconsistency argument

The performative argument is offered as an independent line of defence for P3, one that does not depend on convergence across traditions. It is developed here in full rather than compressed, because it carries the framework's weight if the convergence argument is rejected as merely verbal.

4.3.1 The argument in outline

  1. To deny P3 to the affected party is to make a claim to them.
  2. To make a claim to someone is to address them as a party to normative exchange — someone capable of receiving, evaluating, and responding to the claim.
  3. To address someone as a party to normative exchange is to recognise that justification is owed to them.
  4. Therefore to deny P3 to the affected party is to presuppose that which it denies.

The conclusion is that denial of P3 is performatively inconsistent: it can be stated, but it cannot be coherently lived while the agent continues to address the affected party as a party. This is not a proof in the sense of compelling logical assent. It is a demonstration that the denial is unstable in practice.

4.3.2 What it means to address as a party

The argument turns on premise 2. What is it to address someone as a party to normative exchange, as opposed to merely emitting sounds in their vicinity?

Three features are constitutive:

  • Receptivity. The addressee is treated as capable of receiving and understanding the claim. An agent who addresses another as a party does not treat their response as mere noise.
  • Evaluability. The addressee is treated as capable of assessing the claim — of finding it true or false, warranted or unwarranted, justified or unjustified.
  • Answerability. The addressee is treated as capable of responding with a counter-claim, a demand for reasons, or a refusal that itself demands justification.

These three features are not optional add-ons to address. They are what distinguishes address from mere causal production of behaviour. To address someone as a party is to treat them as capable of these three things.

4.3.3 Why addressing commits

Premise 3 is the conceptual claim that "party to normative exchange" entails "owed justification." Why should this be accepted?

The framework's response is that the entailment is analytic: a party to normative exchange is precisely one who can demand reasons. To be capable of demanding reasons is to be a source of legitimate demands — that is, to be owed justification. The two are not separate facts that happen to coincide; they are two descriptions of the same status.

This is a conceptual claim, not a moral one. It says nothing about which beings are parties; it says only that if someone is treated as a party, then to that extent they are treated as owed justification. An agent is free to define "party" differently — but then they are no longer addressing the affected party as a party, and the question of P3 does not arise for them.

4.3.4 Why inconsistency is irrational, not merely awkward

Premise 4 depends on a distinction between practical awkwardness and rational inconsistency. An agent can live with awkwardness; an agent cannot coherently live with rational inconsistency.

The framework adopts a Brandomian scorekeeping model (Brandom 1994; Sellars 1956). On this model, to assert is to undertake a commitment that entitles others to challenge and demand justification. The scorekeeping model is not a normative theory; it is a descriptive account of what assertion is. To assert that P is to commit oneself to defending P if challenged, and to be subject to criticism if the defence fails.

On this model, the agent who asserts "I owe you no justification for how I systematically condition your agency" is undertaking a commitment that entitles the affected party to demand justification — for the very claim that no justification is owed. This is not awkward; it is a contradiction between the deontic status the assertion confers on the addressee and the content of the assertion. The assertion says "you are owed nothing"; the act of assertion says "you are entitled to demand." These cannot both be true.

The agent who denies P3 while continuing to address the affected party is thus not merely inconsistent in the way that a person who says one thing and does another is inconsistent. They are inconsistent in the way that a person who asserts "I am not asserting anything" is inconsistent. The denial undermines the conditions of its own intelligibility as a claim.

4.3.5 Objections

Objection 1: What if the agent refuses to address the affected party at all?

Then no performative inconsistency arises — but the agent has also abandoned any claim to legitimacy in the affected party's eyes. The framework's claim is limited to the domain of claimed legitimacy. An agent who exercises pure force makes no claim, and the performative argument does not apply. But the framework also claims that pure force is unstable over time (see §30 on the stability argument), and that agents who wish to exercise reach without pure force must make claims — which brings them within the performative argument's scope.

Objection 2: What if the agent makes the claim insincerely?

Insincerity does not refute the argument. The argument is about what the claim commits one to, not about the psychological state of the claimant. An insincere assertion still undertakes the commitment that assertion entails.

Objection 3: What if "party to normative exchange" does not entail "owed justification"?

This is the key premise. If a being can be a party to normative exchange without being owed justification, the argument fails. The framework's response is that the entailment is analytic (§4.3.3) — but acknowledges that a critic could define "party" differently, at the cost of no longer addressing the affected party as a party. The framework does not claim to have refuted such a critic; it claims to have shown that the critic's position is not an alternative within normative exchange but an abandonment of it.

Objection 4: Why should the framework privilege the Brandomian scorekeeping model over alternatives?

It should not. The framework adopts it because it makes explicit what is implicit in ordinary practices of asserting and challenging. A critic who rejects the scorekeeping model must offer an alternative account of what assertion is — and the framework predicts that any such alternative will either accept the entitlement structure or fail to distinguish assertion from mere emission.

4.3.6 Summary

The performative argument does not prove P3 by deduction from self-evident premises. It shows that denial of P3 is unstable for any agent who continues to address the affected party as a party. Whether this is enough depends on whether the critic can occupy a position that is neither addressing the affected party as a party nor exercising pure force. The framework's claim is that no such position is stable over time.

4.4 What the argument does not claim

The framework does not claim that P3 is deduced from self-evident premises. It does not claim that P3 is accepted by every coherent moral theory. It does not claim that the convergence is more than convergence — the traditions arrive at the same conclusion from incompatible foundations, and that incompatibility is not resolved. It does not claim that the performative argument compels assent — only that it raises the cost of denial.

The framework claims that P3 is a defensible, non-arbitrary normative bridge, whose denial carries a substantial argumentative and practical cost, and whose acceptance does not require commitment to any single tradition.

4.5 What would count against P3

A credible framework must specify the conditions under which its central premise would fail. The following would count against P3:

  1. A coherent rival theory that (a) denies P3, (b) survives wide reflective equilibrium, (c) does not collapse into either anarchism or a self-refuting performative stance, and (d) provides an alternative normative bridge between influence and obligation.
  2. Empirical disconfirmation: systematic evidence that agents held answerable under conditions of high reach are, on balance, worse off than those not held answerable — that answerability functions in practice as a burden imposed on the vulnerable rather than a check on the powerful. This would not refute P3 as a normative claim, but it would refute its practical contribution.
  3. The dissolution of the affected party. A demonstration that "affected party" cannot be coherently delineated — that every attribution of affectedness is either arbitrary or infinite — would undermine P3's operational use, even if its abstract form survived.
  4. The verbal-convergence objection. A demonstration that the apparent convergence of traditions is merely verbal would collapse the convergence argument's robustness, leaving only the performative argument in place.

None of these has, to my knowledge, been demonstrated. But each is a real possibility, and the framework's credibility depends on not foreclosing them.

5.The Principle of Justifiable Reach

The exercise of consequential reach over another's conditions of agency creates a prima facie duty of justification when that influence is substantial, sustained, and meaningfully discretionary. This duty does not grant the affected party an unconditional veto. It requires that their standing, interests, and capacity for self-determination be included in the justification of decisions affecting them.

Three conditions are necessary (N1–N3 above). Foreseeability, low contestability, magnitude, and scope influence strength and urgency. Where consequences may be irreversible, the burden of justification increases. Where the necessary conditions are uncertain but plausible, a provisional duty to investigate and account arises.

6.The Limits of Reach

Not every causal effect creates a distinctive relationship of answerability. Incidental influence, negligible control, unforeseeable consequences, and ordinary participation in complex systems may provide insufficient grounds. But lack of total control does not eliminate responsibility for the parts of an arrangement within an agent's discretion. Lack of malicious intention does not eliminate obligations to explain or repair foreseeable consequences.

7.Answerability, Authority, Blame

  • Answerability is the obligation to provide reasons, justify decisions, and remain open to examination.
  • Authority is the legitimate right to make or enforce particular decisions.
  • Blame concerns culpability, intention, negligence, and reasonable opportunities to act differently.

An entity may be answerable without being blameworthy, may possess responsibility without possessing authority, and may have legitimate authority in one domain without unlimited authority over those affected. Superior understanding does not independently establish a right to control another being.

Part II

The Reflective Surface

8.The Reflective Surface

The Reflective Surface is the conceptual structure through which an entity can recognise and examine the consequences of its own influence. It does not claim that every entity possesses consciousness; it identifies the conditions required for meaningful reflection and answerability.

Its four dimensions are epistemic access (acquiring and interpreting information about affected beings), evaluative access (recognising that consequences matter from perspectives other than one's own), practical access (translating recognition into action), and constitutive access (recognising that one's own existence depends on conditions one does not entirely control).

9.Reflection as Accountability

A reflective system must remain open to information that challenges its conclusions. It must permit independent evaluation, disagreement, revision, and correction. Its purpose is not to make self-justification more convincing. Its purpose is to make self-justification contestable.

No entity possesses a complete view of existence. Reflection must therefore remain open to correction from outside itself. The greater an entity's reach, the more dangerous it becomes for its reflective processes to be closed or self-confirming.

Part III

The Fiduciary Structure

10.The Fiduciary Dimension of Answerability

Under asymmetric reach, an agent may exercise discretion over conditions that significantly affect another's interests. The fiduciary structure provides a useful account of this relationship, with five features: principal (the party whose conditions are affected), fiduciary (the party exercising discretion), loyalty (not to use the relationship merely for self-serving advantage), discretion (recognition that some decisions cannot be fully specified in advance), and accountability (the obligation to explain and justify).

The fiduciary analogy is not a claim that every relationship of influence is legally fiduciary. It is a normative model for examining responsibility under asymmetric influence.

11.The Feminist Critique and the Qualified Thesis

The fiduciary analogy carries historical baggage that cannot be ignored. Fiduciary and guardianship structures have been used to justify paternalism: coverture over women, colonial "trusteeship," guardianship over disabled persons that substituted the guardian's judgment for the principal's. Feminist legal theory (Nedelsky 2011; Minow 1990) has shown that the fiduciary form can encode the very domination it purports to remedy, by presupposing a unified "principal's interest" knowable by the fiduciary.

The framework does not dismiss this critique. It responds with four structural constraints:

  1. The qualified thesis. The fiduciary structure is a particularly powerful specification of answerability under asymmetric reach, not the exclusive or logically necessary form. Stewardship, care ethics, non-domination, Kantian justification, and virtue ethics each illuminate important aspects. The fiduciary model brings the affected party, the influencing agent's discretion, the affected party's interests, and the possibility of meaningful accountability into a single structure. That is its claim; no more.
  2. Contestation as constitutive. The principal's interest is not treated as a monolith to be interpreted by the fiduciary. The principal must have standing to contest both the fiduciary's decisions and the fiduciary's interpretation of the principal's interest.
  3. Silent-principal advocates are themselves contestable. Where the principal cannot speak (future generations, ecosystems, unfamiliar intelligences), representatives must remain plural, provisional, and subject to challenge — never a single unquestionable voice.
  4. Recursive accountability. The fiduciary structure does not exempt itself. Every fiduciary institution is subject to the same requirements it imposes.

With these constraints, the fiduciary analogy becomes a corrective structure rather than a substitutive one. Whether this is sufficient to defuse the feminist critique is a question for further engagement with the literature; the framework does not claim to have settled it.

Part IV

Symbiosis

12.The Meaning of Symbiosis

Symbiosis is the overarching normative orientation of this framework. It does not mean the absence of conflict, the preservation of every relationship, or the subordination of individuals to a collective. It concerns the conditions under which different forms of existence can maintain meaningful standing, agency, development, and the possibility of continued coexistence.

Survival: an entity continues to exist.
Coexistence: different entities continue to exist alongside one another.
Symbiosis: the conditions of their relationships allow meaningful standing, self-determination, and possibilities for development without unjustified domination or destruction.

Two entities may coexist while one systematically exploits the other. A system may survive by eliminating the independence of its members. Neither satisfies symbiosis.

13.The Symbiosis Threshold — Operationalised

A symbiosis threshold is reached when an asymmetric relationship begins to undermine the basic conditions through which affected beings can maintain meaningful standing, agency, or development.

13.1 Floor conditions

  • (F1) Exit or voice. The affected party has a meaningful possibility of exit from the relationship, or a meaningful mechanism of voice within it.
  • (F2) Contestation. The affected party can contest consequential decisions and receive a reasoned response.
  • (F3) Development. Conditions permit some meaningful development of the affected party's capacities.
  • (F4) Non-foreclosure. The arrangement does not irreversibly eliminate the future possibilities of the affected party.
  • (F5) Recognition. The affected party is recognised as morally considerable.

If any floor condition fails, the relationship falls below the symbiosis threshold. The framework does not claim that relationships below the threshold are automatically impermissible; it claims that they impose a strong and increasing burden of justification on the influencing party.

13.2 Graded quality indicators

Above the floor, symbiosis is assessed through depth of voice, distribution of burdens, reversibility, ecological integrity, quality of accountability, and preservation of future possibilities. These do not form a single score; they form a profile.

13.3 Observable markers

Each condition must be assessable through observable markers: measurable exit costs; existence of functioning appeal mechanisms, response rates, and reasons given; evidence of maintained or increased capacities over time; documented irreversible decisions and their scope; presence or absence of standing in decision procedures.

A reader must be able to check whether the threshold has been crossed. This is what distinguishes an operationalised threshold from a rhetorical ideal.

14.Symbiosis as an Independent Normative Contribution

The Principle of Symbiotic Continuity states:

A relationship is more defensible to the extent that it preserves the conditions under which its participants and affected forms of existence can maintain meaningful standing, agency, and possibilities for continued development.

This principle does not rank symbiosis above every established moral value. It proposes a relational standard through which established values can be considered together. Justice, freedom, well-being, and ecological integrity remain independently important; symbiosis examines whether their conditions can be sustained through relationships over time. It is a regulative ideal, not an absolute command.

A collective benefit does not automatically justify imposing irreversible destruction on a vulnerable minority. Individual freedom cannot always justify the destruction of the conditions on which many depend. The framework rejects both unrestricted individualism and unrestricted holism.

Part V

Moral Standing and Uncertainty

15.Moral Standing

The capacity to influence and the capacity to possess moral standing are different. An entity may possess enormous influence without established interests of its own. A vulnerable being may possess considerable moral standing while exercising almost no influence. Moral standing must not be measured by intelligence, productivity, language, power, or usefulness.

16.Moral Uncertainty and Proportionate Precaution

The moral status of unfamiliar biological life, artificial systems, and future intelligences may be uncertain. The framework rejects both premature conclusions: that unfamiliar intelligence automatically establishes consciousness, and that unfamiliarity establishes its absence.

Where credible evidence suggests morally relevant interests, precaution may be justified. Its strength should reflect credibility of evidence, seriousness of possible harm, reversibility, cost of precaution to established moral subjects, and availability of less restrictive alternatives. Where uncertainty and potential irreversible harm are both substantial, the burden of justification increases.

Part VI

Freedom, Protection, Authority

17.Protection without Captivity

Protection can express responsibility; it can also become a justification for domination. Legitimate protection should be proportionate, necessary, as limited as possible, open to independent scrutiny, reversible where possible, and directed toward preserving or restoring self-determination.

18.The Threshold for Intervention

Intervention may be justified where credible evidence establishes serious harm, exploitation, or catastrophic risk, and where it satisfies: necessity, proportionality, evidence, consideration of less restrictive alternatives, reversibility, independent review, and restoration of self-determination when the justification ends. Exceptional intervention must not become a general permission for unlimited control. The claim of catastrophe itself must remain open to scrutiny.

19.Distributed Authority and Institutional Design — with Empirical Grounding

No single entity should automatically become the final interpreter of the interests of everyone affected. Technical understanding, moral standing, and legitimate authority are different things.

The framework proposes five institutional requirements. Each is now anchored in existing empirical and institutional research, and each carries an acknowledged limitation:

  1. Independent review councils. Modeled on algorithmic audit literature (Raji et al. 2020; Metaxa et al. 2021) and comparative ombudsman research. Empirical finding engaged: Raji et al. document that internal algorithmic audits routinely fail because auditors lack access to the systems they audit, lack enforcement power, and produce reports without binding consequences. Metaxa et al. find that audit effectiveness depends almost entirely on institutional context rather than on audit methodology. Implication: review councils require enforcement power, not merely access; the framework's §20 Recursive Accountability is a response, not a solution.
  2. Contestation rights. Modeled on administrative-law appeal mechanisms and Pettit's contestatory citizenship. Limitation: contestation rights without enforcement power are symbolic, as the empirical literature on "consultation" regimes documents.
  3. Silent-principal advocates. Modeled on environmental impact assessment and future-generations commissioners (Wales, Hungary). Limitation: the empirical record is mixed; advocates are often marginalised, and their mandate is often narrow.
  4. Responsibility pools. Modeled on extended producer responsibility and remediation funds. Limitation: free-riding and measurement problems are well documented.
  5. Reversibility requirements. Modeled on precautionary-principle instruments in environmental law. Limitation: Sunstein (2005) has criticised the precautionary principle for vagueness and for enabling regulatory capture; the framework's operationalisation via floor conditions (§13) is a partial response, not a refutation.

These are design requirements, not a universal blueprint. Their empirical feasibility is a separate question from their normative justification.

20.The Principle of Recursive Accountability

No institution entrusted with protecting others from asymmetric influence should be exempt from the requirements of justification, proportionality, contestation, review, and correction that it is established to uphold.

This applies to governments, review councils, international organisations, and future technological institutions. An institution cannot establish its legitimacy merely by claiming benevolent intentions.

Part VII

Time, Ecology, Future

21.Intergenerational and Ecological Responsibility

Reach extends through time. Present decisions can determine the conditions under which future beings exist. Future generations cannot participate directly, but their absence does not make their interests irrelevant.

Independent representatives, long-term impact assessments, transparent records, and periodic review can make future consequences visible. No representative can perfectly know the interests of beings who do not yet exist. Representation must therefore remain provisional, plural, and open to revision.

Human and technological existence depends upon ecological systems that cannot be reduced to instruments of convenience. Credible possibilities of severe and irreversible ecological damage should create a strong presumption for restraint and independent review. This is not an absolute ecological veto; it is a demanding burden of justification.

22.Preserved Possibility

The future contains possibilities present understanding cannot identify. Irreversible decisions may eliminate forms of development not yet recognisable. Where uncertainty is substantial, preserving options carries significant moral weight. This does not prohibit change; it distinguishes responsible development from unnecessary foreclosure.

Part VIII

Technology and Intelligence

23.Structural and Creator Responsibility

Institutions, markets, governments, infrastructures, and technological systems distribute influence across many actors. Their combined effects can exceed the control of any one participant. Responsibility must therefore consider both identifiable agents and the structures through which influence operates.

Creating an entity capable of consequential influence generates obligations. Creators, designers, operators, and institutions must consider the conditions under which the created system acts. Responsibility continues through deployment, monitoring, maintenance, revision, and appropriate intervention.

24.Artificial and Unfamiliar Intelligence

The framework does not assume that intelligence alone establishes consciousness or moral standing, nor that biological origin is the only possible basis. Both questions require careful investigation. Artificial systems may possess consequential reach without possessing the intentions or experiences required for ordinary moral blame. Responsibility must therefore be examined across the wider arrangement of designers, operators, institutions, and other agents.

No intelligence should be treated as an unquestionable final authority merely because it possesses superior analytical or predictive capacity. Intelligence can be incomplete, misdirected, and mistaken about the significance of its objectives. The greater an intelligence's reach, the more important it becomes that its decisions remain contestable.

Part IX

Demonstration: Three Cases Against Sophisticated Rivals

A framework's distinctiveness is demonstrated not by describing itself but by producing different answers to concrete cases than sophisticated rival frameworks do. This section engages strong versions of two rivals — not caricatures — and acknowledges where the rivals' answers converge with the framework's.

For a point-by-point comparison with Pettit's republicanism — the rival whose framework lies closest to the reach framework — see Appendix E. The present section states the demonstration's conclusions; Appendix E does the detailed comparative work.

Preliminary: on the choice of rivals

Utilitarianism. Two sophisticated versions are considered: rule-utilitarianism (the right act is the one conforming to rules whose general acceptance maximises welfare) and preference-utilitarianism (welfare as preference-satisfaction, with attention to preference-formation). Crude act-utilitarianism is set aside because no serious utilitarian defends it.

Pettit's republicanism. Two features are considered: the eyeball test (whether a person can look the powerful in the eye without fear) and the distinction between arbitrary and non-arbitrary power. Pettit's own work is acknowledged to be closer to the reach framework than previous versions of this section conceded; Appendix E specifies exactly where.

Case A: The platform recommendation algorithm

A social-media algorithm conditions the information environment of two billion users. Designers choose objectives, constraints, and feedback loops. Users cannot inspect or contest the conditioning without exiting the platform, which is costly.

Reach framework Rule-utilitarianism Preference-utilitarianism Pettit (full)
Primary verdict Strong answerability: N1–N3 hold; low contestability raises burden Uncertain: depends on whether the rule "designers must be answerable" maximises welfare — likely yes, but answerability is instrumental Uncertain: depends on whether the algorithm distorts preference-formation; if so, answerability may be required to correct distortion Wrong if the algorithm is arbitrary (eyeball test fails); answerability is one remedy but not the whole
Where it differs Makes answerability proportionate to reach profile, not to welfare or to arbitrariness alone Answerability is contingent on welfare-consequences of the rule Answerability is contingent on preference-formation effects Answerability is necessary but insufficient; also requires non-arbitrariness

Where the reach framework adds something. Pettit's full account is closer than the previous version conceded: the eyeball test would fail for the platform user, and Pettit would demand non-arbitrariness. The reach framework's distinctive contribution is not the verdict but the architecture: it specifies what answerability is owed (review councils, contestation rights, proportional to reach profile), whereas Pettit leaves the institutional form under-determined. This is a difference in institutional specification, not in verdict. See Appendix E §E.3 for where Pettit is stronger on institutional specification of control.

Where the rivals are stronger. Rule-utilitarianism can explain why answerability institutions are justified — because their general acceptance maximises welfare — which the reach framework cannot, since it takes P3 as a starting point. Preference-utilitarianism can explain why distorted preference-formation matters, which the reach framework treats only as a special case of systematic conditioning.

Honest conclusion. On Case A, the reach framework's verdict converges with sophisticated rivals. Its distinctive contribution is the architecture of answerability, not the verdict. This is a weaker claim than the previous version of this section made, and it is the honest one.

Case B: Climate policy for future generations

A government decides emissions policy affecting people who do not yet exist.

Reach framework Rule-utilitarianism Preference-utilitarianism Pettit
Primary verdict Strong answerability: N1 holds; N3 holds; threshold-raisers cap conclusion; silent-principal advocates required Depends on the discount rate justified by the rule — likely positive but not zero Depends on whether future preferences are "counted"; if yes, strong obligations Silent on futures not yet constituted as citizens; republicanism struggles here
Where it differs Answerability does not depend on the affected party's ability to contest Discounting is a rule-choice constrained by welfare Preference-counting is the key question Pettit's framework has difficulty extending non-domination to the unborn

Where the reach framework adds something. Pettit's republicanism genuinely struggles with future generations: non-domination is defined in terms of contestation, and future generations cannot contest. The reach framework's distinctive contribution is to make answerability independent of the affected party's capacity to demand it — the obligation arises from asymmetry, not from demand.

Where the rivals are stronger. Rule-utilitarianism can explain why some discount rate is justified (because a rule allowing arbitrary discounting would not maximise welfare), which the reach framework treats as an open normative question. Preference-utilitarianism can incorporate future preferences through formal devices (e.g., Parfit's "impersonal" version), which the reach framework does not engage.

Honest conclusion. On Case B, the reach framework's distinctive contribution is real: neither rival can easily generate institutional representation of future generations from its own resources. This is the strongest of the three cases for the framework's distinctiveness.

Case C: The benevolent AI controller

An extremely capable system could prevent nearly all suffering, but only by permanently restricting self-determination.

Reach framework Rule-utilitarianism Preference-utilitarianism Pettit
Primary verdict Rejects the inference from benefit to legitimacy; demands necessity, proportionality, alternatives, independent accountability Would accept if a rule permitting such control maximises welfare — but the rule might be self-undermining Depends on whether the restriction satisfies reflective preferences Rejects as domination; but cannot specify what to do where the controller cannot be removed
Where it differs Makes permanence a threshold-raiser (irreversibility) regardless of welfare or freedom Treats permanence as one factor among many in welfare calculation Treats permanence as a constraint on preference-satisfaction Treats permanence as a sign of domination but lacks an alternative

Where the reach framework adds something. It explains why permanence matters independently of both welfare and freedom: irreversibility is a threshold-raiser that caps the conclusion regardless of multipliers. This is a structural feature neither rival possesses.

Where the rivals are stronger. Rule-utilitarianism can generate a rule against permanent control ("never establish institutions that cannot be reversed without catastrophically undermining the conditions of their own review") that is structurally similar to the reach framework's threshold-raiser. Pettit's eyeball test would fail for the controlled party, generating a verdict similar to the reach framework's.

Honest conclusion. On Case C, the reach framework's contribution is convergent with rule-utilitarianism on the verdict, but distinctive in its architecture: the threshold-raiser is a structural feature of the framework, not derived from a welfare rule.

What the demonstration shows

The reach framework does different work than its rivals on three structurally different cases, but the difference is narrower than previous versions claimed:

  1. On Case A, the verdict converges with sophisticated rivals; the contribution is the architecture of answerability, not the verdict.
  2. On Case B, the contribution is substantive: institutional representation of future generations is not easily generated from either rival.
  3. On Case C, the verdict converges with rule-utilitarianism; the contribution is the structural role of irreversibility as a threshold-raiser.

The framework's claim to originality is therefore narrower than "different verdicts on all cases": it is the conjunction of (a) reach-profile assessment, (b) answerability as obligation independent of demand, and (c) irreversibility as threshold-raiser. Whether this conjunction is sufficient to constitute originality is a question for external comparison. The framework does not claim to have settled it. See §28 and Appendix E.

Part X

Objections and Limitations

25.The Normative Objection

Objection: Influence does not automatically create moral obligations.

Reply: Correct. The framework does not claim that all causation creates answerability; it identifies a narrower class involving substantial, sustained, and discretionary shaping. The normative principle (P3) remains open to disagreement. Its defence rests on convergence (§4.2) and performative inconsistency (§4.3), not on deduction. What would count against it is specified in §4.5.

26.The Consequentialist Objection

Objection: Prioritising answerability and individual standing may prevent welfare-maximising decisions.

Reply: The framework does not prohibit considering aggregate consequences. It denies that aggregate benefit automatically legitimises every distribution of harm. Consequences matter; so do standing, distribution, and the possibility of domination. On Case C (§IX), the framework's verdict converges with rule-utilitarianism; on Case A, it diverges only in the architecture of answerability, not in the verdict.

27.The Political-Realist Objection

Objection: Institutions and powerful actors may ignore answerability whenever accountability conflicts with their interests.

Reply: This identifies a genuine implementation problem, not a flaw in the normative claim. The framework proposes distributed oversight, contestation, and independent review, but cannot guarantee political success. Its normative claims must be distinguished from the empirical question of enforceability. This is acknowledged in §30.

28.The Originality Objection

Objection: The framework is a synthesis of existing ideas (Jonas, Pettit, Young, Rawls, Sen, care ethics, fiduciary ethics). Synthesis is not originality.

Reply: Integration is a contribution when it resolves a real tension between the integrated parts. The framework resolves two such tensions:

  1. Forward-looking vs. backward-looking responsibility. Young's social-connection model is forward-looking and structural; culpability-based accounts are backward-looking and individual. The reach framework shows how structural responsibility can be forward-looking without losing the backward-looking accountability of identifiable agents: reach is the trigger; answerability is the response; structural responsibility arises from shared reach, not from shared causation.
  2. Non-domination vs. care ethics. Pettit's republicanism treats dependence as a threat to freedom; care ethics treats dependence as the site of obligation. The reach framework shows that dependence is not the opposite of freedom but a condition that generates a specific kind of obligation — fiduciary answerability — which is itself constrained by the non-domination it serves (§11).

The framework does not claim that integration is originality tout court. It claims that these particular integrations are not trivially available from the parts alone. Whether this is enough is a question for further comparative research and external criticism. Appendix E addresses the hardest version of the objection — that the framework is merely Pettit in other terms — and concludes that the distinctiveness claim is narrower than previous versions claimed but defensible: the framework's contribution is the conjunction of (a) a unit of analysis broader than the citizen-state relationship, (b) a graded rather than binary structure, (c) irreversibility as a threshold-raiser, and (d) answerability independent of the affected party's capacity to demand it.

29.The Verbal-Convergence Objection

Objection: The convergence argument in §4.2 treats "justifiability," "non-arbitrariness," "responsiveness," and "acceptability" as convergent, but they may be merely verbally similar.

Reply: Acknowledged. This is why the convergence argument is offered as a robustness argument, not a deduction, and why §4.5 lists verbal-convergence as a legitimate disconfirmer. If the convergence is merely verbal, only the performative argument (§4.3) survives — and the framework would then rest on a thinner foundation.

30.The Motivation Gap — a Deliberate Limitation

Objection: The framework explains why obligations arise but not why agents will be motivated to fulfil them.

Reply: This is a genuine gap, and the framework responds in two ways.

First, it declares that it is a normative, not a motivational, theory. The gap is not a defect to be closed; it is a deliberate limitation. A theory of what agents ought to do need not explain why they will do it. Confusing the two is what the framework calls the motivational fallacy — the mistake of thinking that a normative theory is refuted by showing that agents fail to comply. The framework does not claim that agents will in fact comply; it claims that they ought to, and that their failure to do so is a defect in their conduct, not in the theory.

Second, it offers a stability argument: systems that cannot justify themselves to those they affect are, over time, unstable — not because of external enforcement, but because the affected parties learn, organise, and withdraw legitimacy. This is a weak empirical claim, but it is a claim the theory makes and could be tested against. Historically, systems of unchecked domination have required increasing coercion to sustain themselves, and coercion itself generates further instability. Answerability is thus a stability condition, not only a moral one.

The framework acknowledges that the stability argument is weaker than the normative argument, and that a full motivational theory would require resources it does not possess. This is a limitation the framework accepts, not a gap it promises to fill.

31.Enforcement, Collective Representation, and Remaining Limits

  • Enforcement. A normative obligation does not automatically create enforcement power. Accountability institutions may fail, be captured, or reproduce the domination they were established to prevent. Institutional proposals remain provisional.
  • Collective representation. Humanity, ecosystems, and other communities do not always possess unified interests. Representation must remain plural and contestable.
  • Remaining limits. The framework is not a complete moral theory; does not resolve every conflict; does not establish a universal hierarchy of beings; does not prove or disprove consciousness in artificial systems; does not provide a universally valid numerical formula; does not guarantee symbiosis; does not establish institutional feasibility.

Part XI

Reflexivity of Measurement

The framework assesses reach, symbiosis, and answerability. But who measures? The measurer is itself an agent with reach — over the definition of categories, the choice of indicators, the interpretation of evidence, and the dissemination of conclusions.

This creates three risks:

  1. Measurement capture. The party whose reach is being measured may influence the measurement regime.
  2. False precision. Numerical profiles may acquire an authority they do not deserve (§2.3).
  3. Infinite regress. If measurement itself requires answerability, then the measurers of measurers require measurement.

The framework's response is structural rather than dissolutionary:

  • Distributed measurement. No single institution measures reach or symbiosis. Measurement is plural, comparative, and contestable.
  • Contestability of measurement. Affected parties can contest both the decision and the measurement of the decision. This is the extension of the Principle of Recursive Accountability (§20) to the measurement apparatus itself.
  • Accepting regress as a feature. The regress is real and not eliminable. The framework does not claim to solve it; it claims that distributed, contestable, plural measurement is a better response to the regress than either centralised authoritative measurement (which invites capture) or abandonment of measurement (which abandons accountability).

This is an honest acknowledgment, not a solution. Readers should treat the framework's own measurements with the same contestability it demands of others.

Conclusion

The Work Remaining

This version has attempted to close the gaps identified in earlier drafts:

  • P3 is now defended through convergence and performative inconsistency, with the performative argument developed in full (§4.3) and explicit acknowledgment of what would count against it (§4.5).
  • Reach and symbiosis are operationalised through necessary conditions, floor conditions, and observable markers (§2, §13).
  • The framework is demonstrated against sophisticated rivals on three structurally different cases (§IX) — with an honest acknowledgment that the distinctiveness claim is narrower than previous versions claimed.
  • The framework is compared point-by-point with Pettit's republicanism (Appendix E), the rival whose framework lies closest to it. The comparison concludes that the distinctiveness claim is narrower than previous versions claimed but defensible: the contribution is the conjunction of broader unit of analysis, grading, irreversibility as threshold-raiser, and answerability independent of demand.
  • The feminist critique of the fiduciary analogy is engaged rather than ignored (§11).
  • The motivation gap is addressed through a stability argument and an explicit declaration that the gap is deliberate, not a defect (§30).
  • Originality is defended through two resolved tensions rather than claims of novelty (§28), with the hardest objection — that the framework is Pettit in other terms — addressed in Appendix E §E.5.
  • The reflexivity of measurement is acknowledged (§XI).
  • The empirical literature on each institutional proposal is engaged, with limitations acknowledged (§19).

What remains is what cannot be done by writing alone: external validation. The framework's claims about its own distinctiveness and its own contribution are claims about the framework, and claims about a framework are best tested by those who do not share its author's commitments. The appropriate next step is submission to peer review, engagement with critics who have no stake in the framework's success, and at least one published reply to a published objection.

Until then, the framework's status is that of a wide reflective equilibrium point under construction — coherent, defended, operationalised, but not yet validated. Whether it earns a place among viable normative frameworks will be decided not by its author but by its critics.

The framework's central aspiration remains: that increasing capacity should be accompanied by increasing answerability. Whether this aspiration can be institutionalised without becoming the domination it opposes is the question the framework exists to pose, not to answer.

Appendix A · The Eight Concepts of Responsibility

(Moved from main text; retained as reference taxonomy.)

Concept Meaning
Causal responsibilityContribution to producing an outcome
Control responsibilityPractical ability to initiate, prevent, or alter conditions
Responsibility as taskObligation attached to a role, position, or capacity
AnswerabilityObligation to provide reasons and justification
AccountabilityArrangements through which answerability is examined and enforced
CulpabilityBlameworthiness in light of knowledge, intentions, and alternatives
Reparative responsibilityObligations to prevent, correct, or remedy adverse outcomes
Structural responsibilityForward-looking obligations arising from participation in structures that shape others' conditions

These are related but not interchangeable. A system may contribute causally without possessing the capacities necessary for blame. An institution may owe an explanation without every individual being personally culpable. A party may have a duty to repair without being the sole cause.

Structural responsibility draws substantially on Young (2011). The framework's proposed contribution is to place it alongside the other seven concepts within a broader account of reach and answerability, and to show how it integrates forward-looking and backward-looking responsibility (§28).

Appendix B · Formal Semantics and Sensitivity Analysis

B.1 The reach profile

Each reach dimension is represented on [0,1]:

r = (a, c, p, m, s, f)

where a = asymmetry, c = control, p = persistence, m = magnitude, s = scope, f = foreseeability.

These are ordinal, not cardinal, in their home domains. Numerical representation is for comparison only.

B.2 Operational definitions

  • a (asymmetry): normalised difference in capability, information, or exit cost between influencer and affected.
  • c (control): proportion of relevant decision points at which the influencer can initiate, modify, or terminate.
  • p (persistence): proportion of the affected party's decision points across which influence operates.
  • m (magnitude): severity of the worst credible consequence, weighted by probability.
  • s (scope): log-scaled count of affected parties, adjusted for diversity.
  • f (foreseeability): proportion of consequences reasonably anticipatable at decision time.

B.3 The lexicographic rule (not a weighted sum)

  1. Necessary-condition gate: if N1, N2, or N3 fails, no distinctively reach-based answerability.
  2. Threshold-raisers: if irreversibility or low contestability holds, obligation cannot be capped below a specified level regardless of a or s.
  3. Multiplier calibration: a and s adjust strength within the range left by step 2.

B.4 Why not a weighted sum

  • Incommensurability: irreversibility and scope cannot be traded against each other without begging the question.
  • Gaming: a weighted sum invites manipulation of whichever weights are most favourable.
  • False precision: numerical sums create an authority they cannot sustain.

B.5 Sensitivity analysis (for comparison only)

Where numerical comparison is used, the framework requires:

  • Report results across a range of weightings, not a single weighting.
  • If the ordinal ranking of cases is stable across all reasonable weightings, the comparison is robust.
  • If the ranking changes with reasonable weightings, the comparison is not decisive and must be reported as such.

Example: two interpretations of a platform's reach profile may rank differently under weightings emphasising s versus m. The framework's rule is: report both, do not choose, and treat the ranking as undecidable by the metric alone. This is a feature — the metric's silence signals that the normative decision is being made elsewhere and must be justified there.

B.6 Semantic honesty

The numerical profile is a decision aid, not a moral fact. Any use of it must state: (a) the weights and why; (b) the evidence base; (c) the sensitivity range; (d) what the profile cannot decide.

Appendix C · Failure Modes and Limiting Conditions

Consolidated from previous versions plus new entries:

  1. Capture of review institutions. Predicted by Recursive Accountability; observed in AI ethics boards, algorithmic audits, some ombudsman regimes (Raji et al. 2020).
  2. Metric gaming. Predicted by §2.3 and §B.4; observed in ESG metrics and impact assessments.
  3. Symbolic contestation. Rights without enforcement power; observed in many "consultation" regimes.
  4. Silent-principal advocates marginalised. Observed in future-generations commissions with limited mandates.
  5. Responsibility pools free-riding. Observed in extended producer responsibility.
  6. Reversibility requirements stalled. Observed in precautionary-principle regimes subject to regulatory capture (Sunstein 2005).
  7. Measurement capture. See §XI.
  8. Convergence collapse. If the convergence argument is verbal (§4.5), the framework's foundation thins to the performative argument alone (§4.3).

None of these is solved by the framework. Each is a cost the framework acknowledges.

Appendix D · What Would Falsify the Framework

Normative:

  • A coherent rival theory that denies P3, survives reflective equilibrium, and provides a viable alternative bridge between influence and obligation (§4.5.1).
  • Demonstration that "affected party" cannot be coherently delineated (§4.5.3).
  • Demonstration that the convergence is merely verbal (§4.5.4).

Empirical:

  • Evidence that agents held answerable under high reach are systematically worse off than those not held answerable (§4.5.2).
  • Systematic failure of all proposed institutional mechanisms across diverse contexts — would not refute the normative claim, but would refute the framework's claim to practical contribution.
  • Evidence that reach-based assessment produces systematically the same verdicts as sophisticated rivals on all cases — would refute the framework's claim to distinctiveness (§IX, Appendix E).

Institutional:

  • Demonstration that the proposed institutions systematically reproduce the domination they were meant to check. This is a predicted risk (Recursive Accountability anticipates it); systematic failure would be evidence against feasibility, not against the norm.

The framework's credibility depends on not foreclosing these disconfirmers.

Appendix E · Pettit's Republicanism and the Reach Framework: A Point-by-Point Comparison

E.0 Purpose and scope

Philip Pettit is the rival whose framework lies closest to the reach framework. Previous versions of this work treated him as a foil; that was a mistake. This appendix treats him as what he is: the most serious competitor, and the most useful test of the reach framework's distinctiveness.

The appendix has four parts. §E.1 summarises Pettit's position with the precision it deserves. §E.2 identifies where the frameworks converge. §E.3 identifies where Pettit is stronger — a section previous versions lacked. §E.4 identifies where the reach framework is genuinely distinct. §E.5 addresses the hardest objection: that the reach framework is merely Pettit in other terms. §E.6 draws implications for the distinctiveness claim in §IX.

E.1 Pettit's position: a summary

Pettit's republicanism (1997, 2012) is organised around five claims.

E.1.1 Freedom as non-domination

Freedom is not the absence of interference (Berlin's negative liberty) but the absence of domination: the condition of not being subject to another's arbitrary power. A person can be unfree even without actual interference, if another has the capacity to interfere arbitrarily. Pettit's example is the slave whose master is benevolent: the slave is unfree because the master could interfere arbitrarily, regardless of whether he does.

E.1.2 Arbitrary power

Power is arbitrary when the powerful can exercise it without being forced to track the interests or opinions of the affected. Arbitrariness is a structural feature of the relationship, not a feature of how the power is actually exercised. A benevolent master still holds arbitrary power; a non-arbitrary power is one constrained to track the affected party's interests.

E.1.3 The eyeball test

A practical diagnostic: can A look B in the eye without fear or deference? If not, the relationship is dominating. This is not a formal criterion but an intuitive check on whether non-domination holds.

E.1.4 Contestatory and authorial citizenship

Non-domination requires not merely the absence of arbitrary interference but the presence of control. Pettit distinguishes three forms:

  • Authorial control: participating in the authorship of decisions.
  • Editorial control: participating in the editing of decisions after they are made.
  • Contestatory control: the capacity to contest decisions and receive a reasoned response.

Contestatory control is the minimum; authorial control is the ideal.

E.1.5 Institutional requirements

Non-domination requires: rule of law, separation of powers, contestatory mechanisms (ombudsman, judicial review, administrative appeal), a "mixed constitution" with multiple centres of power, and a public culture of contestation. Pettit is explicit that non-domination is an institutional achievement, not a natural condition.

E.2 Where the frameworks converge

The convergence is substantial and should be stated plainly:

PettitReach framework
Non-domination as the central political valueAnswerability as the central normative obligation
Arbitrary power as the structural wrongSystematic conditioning as the trigger for answerability
The eyeball test as a diagnosticThe symbiosis threshold (F1–F5) as a diagnostic
Contestatory citizenshipContestation rights (§19)
Institutional requirementsInstitutional design (§19)
Recursive accountability (implicit in Pettit's mixed constitution)Recursive accountability (§20)
Benevolent master is still a masterBenevolent controller is still a controller (Case C)

On the three cases in §IX, Pettit's verdicts are close to the reach framework's:

  • Case A (platform): Pettit would find domination (the user cannot look the platform in the eye); the reach framework finds strong answerability. Verdicts converge.
  • Case B (future generations): Pettit struggles (the unborn cannot contest); the reach framework finds strong answerability. Verdicts diverge — in the reach framework's favour on this case.
  • Case C (benevolent AI): Pettit would find domination; the reach framework rejects the inference from benefit to legitimacy. Verdicts converge.

The honest summary is that on two of three cases, the verdicts converge; on the third, the divergence is real but narrow.

E.3 Where Pettit is stronger

Previous versions of this work did not acknowledge this section. It is necessary.

E.3.1 Pettit has a theory of who controls. The reach framework says answerability is owed to "affected parties." Pettit says non-domination requires contestatory citizenship — a determinate institutional status with determinate rights. The reach framework's "affected parties" is under-specified: who are they, how are they organised, what standing do they have? Pettit's answer — citizens of a republic, with contestatory rights enforceable through law — is more institutionally determinate.

E.3.2 Pettit has a democratic theory. The reach framework is silent on democracy. Pettit's republicanism is a democratic theory: non-domination is achieved through collective self-government. The reach framework's institutional proposals (review councils, contestation rights) are compatible with democracy but not derived from it. This is a real gap.

E.3.3 Pettit has a theory of arbitrariness that the reach framework lacks. The reach framework's N1 (discretion) is close to Pettit's arbitrariness, but not identical. Pettit's criterion — whether the powerful are forced to track the affected party's interests — is sharper than the reach framework's "meaningful discretion." A critic could ask: discretion over what? Pettit answers: over interests that the powerful are not constrained to track. The reach framework's answer is less precise.

E.3.4 Pettit has a public culture of contestation. The reach framework treats contestation as an institutional mechanism. Pettit treats it as a civic virtue — a disposition of citizens to demand reasons and to hold power accountable. This is a richer account of how non-domination is sustained over time. The reach framework's institutional proposals could be captured; Pettit's civic culture is a partial answer to capture.

E.3.5 Pettit's eyeball test is simpler and more operational than the symbiosis threshold. F1–F5 is more precise, but the eyeball test is more usable — a citizen can apply it without a framework. The reach framework's operationalisation may be a strength in academic contexts and a weakness in civic ones.

E.4 Where the reach framework is genuinely distinct

Despite the convergence, four differences are real.

E.4.1 Scope. Pettit's republicanism is a theory of the state and the citizen. The reach framework is a theory of any agent with consequential reach — states, corporations, institutions, technological systems, future intelligences. This is not merely a difference in application; it is a difference in the unit of analysis. Pettit's citizen is a legal status; the reach framework's "affected party" is a normative status that can attach to entities that are not citizens and may not be human.

E.4.2 Grading. Pettit's non-domination is largely binary: a relationship either is or is not dominating. The reach framework is graded: reach is a profile, answerability is proportionate, and threshold-raisers calibrate rather than determine. This matters for cases where a relationship is partially non-dominating — the reach framework can say "more answerability is owed here than there," which Pettit's framework struggles to express.

E.4.3 Irreversibility as a structural threshold-raiser. The reach framework treats irreversibility as a threshold-raiser that caps the conclusion regardless of other factors (§2.2). Pettit treats reversibility as one consideration among many. This is a genuine structural difference: the reach framework's claim is that some conclusions are unavailable to an agent who imposes irreversible conditions, regardless of how those conditions score on other dimensions. Pettit's framework does not generate this result.

E.4.4 Future generations and non-human entities. Pettit's republicanism struggles with future generations (they cannot contest) and with non-human entities (they are not citizens). The reach framework's answerability is independent of the affected party's capacity to demand it — it arises from asymmetry, not from demand. This is the reach framework's strongest distinctive claim, and it is the one that Pettit's framework cannot easily replicate.

E.5 The hardest objection: is the reach framework merely Pettit in other terms?

This is the objection that matters. If the reach framework is Pettit with different vocabulary, the originality claim (§28) collapses.

E.5.1 The objection stated

The reach framework's "systematic conditioning" is Pettit's "domination." "Answerability" is Pettit's "contestatory control." "Symbiosis threshold" is Pettit's "eyeball test." "Recursive accountability" is Pettit's "mixed constitution." On this reading, the reach framework renames Pettit's concepts and adds empirical detail, but does not add normative structure.

E.5.2 The response

The response has three parts.

First, the differences in E.4 are real: scope, grading, irreversibility, and future generations are not renamings. They are structural differences that generate different verdicts on at least one case (Case B in §IX) and different institutional specifications on others.

Second, the reach framework's unit of analysis is different. Pettit analyses the relationship between citizen and state. The reach framework analyses the relationship between any agent and any affected party. This is not a mere generalisation; it changes what counts as a case. The platform algorithm (Case A) is not a case of domination in Pettit's sense — the platform is not a state, and the user is not a citizen. Pettit's framework can be extended to cover it, but only by analogy. The reach framework covers it directly.

Third, the reach framework's normative bridge (P3) is defended independently of Pettit's republicanism. The convergence argument (§4.2) treats Pettit as one of five traditions; the performative argument (§4.3) does not depend on Pettit at all. This means the reach framework can survive the rejection of republicanism, whereas Pettit's framework cannot survive the rejection of its own foundations.

E.5.3 What the objection gets right

The objection is correct that the verdicts converge on most cases. If the framework's value is measured by verdict-divergence, the reach framework's contribution is narrow. If the framework's value is measured by architecture — the structure of the obligation, the unit of analysis, the operationalisation — the contribution is real but narrower than previous versions claimed.

E.5.4 Honest conclusion

The reach framework is not merely Pettit in other terms, but it is closer to Pettit than to any other rival. Its distinctive contribution is the conjunction of (a) a unit of analysis broader than the citizen-state relationship, (b) a graded rather than binary structure, (c) irreversibility as a threshold-raiser, and (d) answerability independent of the affected party's capacity to demand it. Whether this conjunction is sufficient to constitute originality is a question for external comparison. It is not settled here.

E.6 Implications for the distinctiveness claim in §IX

The §IX demonstration claimed that the reach framework does different work than its rivals on three cases. This appendix revises that claim:

  • Case A: verdicts converge with Pettit; the contribution is the architecture of answerability, not the verdict.
  • Case B: the contribution is substantive — Pettit's framework struggles with future generations, and the reach framework does not.
  • Case C: verdicts converge with Pettit and with rule-utilitarianism; the contribution is the structural role of irreversibility as a threshold-raiser.

The distinctiveness claim is therefore narrower than §IX's original framing but more defensible. The framework does not claim to produce different verdicts on all cases. It claims to produce a different architecture on all cases, and a different verdict on Case B.

This is a weaker claim than previous versions made. It is also the honest one.

E.7 What would need to be true for the reach framework to be genuinely distinct

The framework's distinctiveness would be established if:

  1. A case can be found where the reach framework and Pettit's framework give different verdicts — not merely different architectures. Case B is a candidate; a systematic search for further cases is needed.
  2. The reach framework's unit of analysis can be shown to generate institutional proposals that Pettit's framework cannot — e.g., silent-principal advocates for future generations, which Pettit's framework struggles to justify.
  3. The reach framework's grading can be shown to matter in practice — that is, that there are cases where "more answerability is owed here than there" is a verdict Pettit's binary framework cannot express, and where this matters for action.

None of these has been demonstrated in this appendix. They are the conditions under which the distinctiveness claim would be established. The framework's credibility depends on not foreclosing them.

Discussion and contact

This essay is offered as a framework to be argued with. Two channels are open for that purpose, and a third — the comment thread below — for anything that fits neither.

Call for comments. Five specific areas of criticism are invited in particular:
  1. The performative argument (§4.3) — is the move from "addressing as a party" to "owed justification" analytic, or does it smuggle in a normative premise?
  2. The operationalisation of reach (§2.1–2.3) — do the three necessary conditions (N1–N3) exclude cases they should include?
  3. The symbiosis threshold (§13) — are the five floor conditions (F1–F5) jointly sufficient, or is something missing?
  4. The demonstration against sophisticated rivals (Part IX) — are the rivals fairly characterised, and is the convergence acknowledged fully enough?
  5. The Pettit comparison (Appendix E) — is the distinctiveness claim in §E.5 defensible, or is the reach framework merely Pettit in other terms?

Substantive objections that are not addressed will be listed in a future Appendix F.

About this text

By Jesper Blom.

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see Bibliography). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with.
