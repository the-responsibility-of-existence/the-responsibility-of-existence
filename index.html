Abstract

In one sentence: when you exercise discretionary influence over the conditions under which others live, choose and develop, and when they cannot exit or contest that influence effectively, you owe them an account of it — and you cannot be the sole judge of whether that account is good enough. The condition that makes the account real is a pause: not a fourth mechanism alongside exit, review and justification, but the condition under which those three are something other than ritual.

What this essay argues. The essay proposes a framework for deciding when influence creates a duty to answer, how demanding that duty is, who must discharge it, and how it can be discharged without turning into new domination. Its contribution is partly operational — an activation rule, a duty ladder, a rating protocol, and an illustration of how the exercised-capacity principle can supplement Young's social connection model in a concrete case — and partly philosophical, in two parts.

The first philosophical proposal is the epistemic burden principle. Asymmetric influence creates a heightened risk of epistemic self-confirmation: the actor who controls the terms of dependence also controls the terms of assessment. The principle is best read as two claims: a descriptive claim (C1) that asymmetry systematically biases the actor's evidence, supported by empirical research on institutional bias, and a normative claim (C2) that the actor therefore bears a defeasible burden to seek correction from sources it does not control. C1 is empirical; C2 is a bridge principle, stated openly as normative. Neither is circular.

The second, and more unusual, is the pause as condition. J says that arrangements of influence must provide exit, review, or justification. But an actor can provide all three without ever being in a state in which its own assessment is provisional. A review board that ratifies after the decision is not a pause. A justification published after the fact is not a pause. A channel for objection that the actor can ignore is not a pause. The pause is not a mechanism but a condition on the three mechanisms: exit, review and justification only count as answerability when the actor is, at the moment of decision, still available to correction. The framework specifies four tests for whether and how long the pause has occurred — the answer-window test (D1), the registration test (D2), the contestability test (D3), and the articulation test (D4) — and places the burden of justification on the actor, not on the affected party. The pause cannot be guaranteed; it can only be made visible. That is its limit, and it is stated as such.

Ground and defeaters. J is a thin, disjunctive principle: arrangements that systematically constrain others' conditions must provide at least one of meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation. It is primarily epistemic. It is abductively supported and has stated defeaters (§2.2). The epistemic burden principle and the pause requirement each have their own defeaters (§8.4, §5.3). The framework handles domination; it does not solve it. The loop that makes answerability necessary is stated as the ground, not as a concession (§19).

The argument in brief

The essay is long, and its spine can be stated in a page. This is that page.

  1. The problem. Influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse and hard to attribute to any single wrongful act is not adequately covered by existing moral vocabulary — blame, rights, consent, regulation. This is the gap the framework addresses (§1).
  2. The principle (J). Arrangements that systematically and non-trivially constrain others' conditions must, for those affected, provide at least one of: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation. Where exit and review work, justification need not be owed; where they fail, it is (§2).
  3. Why J. J is abductively supported: it offers a plausible explanation of recurring risks associated with unchecked discretionary power. The epistemic consideration is primary. J has stated defeaters (§2.2).
  4. J against Pettit. The republican tradition identifies the right problem but does not supply the trigger, the duty ladder, or the pause. J is a more usable specification (§2b).
  5. The pause as condition. The pause is not a fourth mechanism alongside exit, review and justification. It is the condition under which those three are something other than ritual. No arrangement satisfies J unless the actor is, at the moment of decision, still available to correction. Four tests — D1, D2, D3, D4 — specify whether and how long the pause has occurred, with the burden of proof on the actor (§5).
  6. The epistemic claim. Asymmetric influence creates a heightened risk of epistemic self-confirmation. The principle is two claims: C1 (descriptive, empirical) and C2 (normative, a bridge principle). This separation dissolves the circularity objection (§8).
  7. The limit. J is not necessary. It is defensible. The framework handles domination; it does not solve it. The pause cannot be guaranteed; it can only be made visible. The loop that makes answerability necessary is stated as its ground, not as a concession (§19).

Prologue

The Score

0.A decision at 03:14

A physician, a ventilator, and a score no one in the room can answer for.

At 03:14 on a Tuesday, a physician in an intensive care unit has ninety seconds to decide which of two patients gets the last ventilator. A score on her screen — produced by a system she did not build, trained on data she cannot inspect, validated by a vendor she has never met — ranks one patient at 0.81 and the other at 0.79. The difference is not statistically meaningful. She knows this. She also knows that if she overrides the score and the patient dies, the override will be in the record. If she follows the score and the patient dies, the score will be in the record.

She follows the score.

This essay is not about whether she was right. It is about a simpler question: who owes an account of that score, to whom, and how can the account be tested by anyone other than the people who built it?

The physician cannot answer that question alone. Neither can the vendor. Neither can the regulator who approved the system without seeing its training data. The patients cannot answer it at all. And yet the score shaped the conditions under which two people lived or died — and no one in the room was in a position to be the sole judge of whether it was good enough.

That is the problem this essay addresses. Not blame. Not liability. Answerability: the duty to give reasons for the conditions one helps create, to those affected, in a form they can contest — and the corresponding prohibition on being the sole judge of one's own influence.

The framework that follows is offered as a tool, not a doctrine. It has one core principle, two philosophical proposals (an epistemic burden and a pause), one operational rule, and one limit it cannot overcome. The limit is stated at the end, not hidden in the middle.

Part I

The Problem and the Principle

1.What existing vocabulary misses

The capacity to shape other people's conditions has grown faster than the practices for answering for it.

Blame covers wrongful acts. Rights cover protected interests. Consent covers voluntary exchange. Regulation covers what law has already caught. What falls between them is influence that is structural, persistent, diffuse, and hard to attach to any single wrongful act — and that nonetheless determines what others can do and become.

The physician's score is one instance. A recommendation algorithm that shapes what a billion people see is another. A landlord's screening policy, a teacher's grading rubric, a parent's silence — at different scales, the same structure: an actor with discretion over conditions, affected parties without effective exit or review, and no procedure through which the account can be tested by anyone other than the actor.

The gap is not moral. It is epistemic. The actor cannot see what it has not been forced to see. The affected cannot correct what they cannot contest. And the arrangement, left to itself, produces a stable equilibrium in which the actor's view of its own influence becomes the only view available.

1.2 Scope conditions: when the framework does not apply

The framework is offered for a specific class of cases. It does not claim to apply everywhere, and it is important to say where it does not.

  • Symmetrical relations. Where parties have roughly equal capacity to exit, review and contest, J is trivially satisfied or not engaged. The framework adds little to ordinary practices of mutual justification among equals.
  • Relations governed by enforceable rights and effective regulation. Where law provides effective rights, review and remedies, the framework may add little. The framework supplements legal standards where those protections fail in practice; it never displaces them.
  • Purely causal influence without discretion. A person who is swept away by a flood does not owe an account to those downstream. The framework's trigger is exercised or maintained discretionary influence, not mere causal connection.
  • Trivial influence. A café changing its menu, a neighbour choosing a different route to work — these are not the cases the framework addresses. The severity floor in §10 is meant to exclude them.
  • Relations where the affected party is not a candidate for standing. Stones and rivers as such do not have standing under this framework (§6.1). The framework marks the edge of its claims, and the stone is its most honest marker (§5b).

1.3 Terms

Influence: the exercise or maintenance of control over conditions under which others act, choose, develop or fare. Maintaining a structure one could alter, such as keeping a known-flawed system running, counts as influence. Mere ability to help does not (§3).

Actor: a person, organisation or institution, including those that design, deploy and maintain technical systems. Technical systems are treated as instruments, and answerability falls on the humans and organisations behind them.

Affected party: any being whose interests are materially affected by the influence.

Discretion: the actor has genuine alternatives at tolerable cost and a role in setting the influence, rather than merely executing a rule it had no part in making or contesting.

Answerability: a duty to give reasons for the conditions one helps create or maintain, to those affected or their representatives; to permit examination of those reasons; and to remain open to correction. It is distinct from blame, from liability and from authority.

The pause: a condition on the mechanisms of answerability — exit, review and justification — such that they count as answerability only when the actor is, at the moment of decision, still available to correction. The pause is not a mechanism; it is the condition under which the mechanisms are something other than ritual (§5).

1.4 Relation to existing work

The framework is a synthesis and an operationalisation, and its intellectual debts are substantial: Pettit and Lovett on domination, Forst on the right to justification, Scanlon on reasonable rejection, Young on structural responsibility, Bovens and Shoemaker on accountability and answerability, Tronto, Held and Goodin on care and vulnerability, Fricker on epistemic injustice. Its contribution is comparative and operational, with two limited philosophical proposals: the epistemic burden principle (§8) and the pause as condition (§5). What it offers is (i) a disjunctive activation rule, (ii) an allocation of duties by capacity, (iii) an explicit rating protocol, (iv) an illustration of ECP's proposed operational supplement to the social connection model (§3.3), (v) stated defeaters for J (§2.2), for the epistemic burden principle (§8.4), and for the pause as condition (§5.3), and (vi) the pause as condition, which is the framework's most distinctive philosophical contribution. Whether the combination earns its keep is for readers to judge.

  • The framework answers four questions: when influence creates a duty to answer, how demanding the duty is, who must discharge it, and how review can be organised without becoming new domination.
  • It has explicit scope conditions: symmetrical relations, relations governed by enforceable rights, purely causal influence without discretion, trivial influence, and relations where the affected party is not a candidate for standing are not its target.
  • Its proposed contributions are: the pause as condition, the epistemic burden principle, and an operational activation rule.

2.The core principle (J)

A thin, disjunctive principle, an abductive justification, and stated defeaters.

2.1 The principle

Any arrangement that systematically and non-trivially constrains the conditions under which others act, choose or develop must, for those affected, provide at least one of three things: meaningful exit, independent review, or justification that is genuinely open to their contestation. Where exit is available at tolerable cost and review is effective, justification need not be owed. Where they are not, justification is required, and the strength of the requirement scales with how thoroughly exit and review fail.

J is deliberately thin. It does not say what is good, which outcomes are required, or who prevails when interests conflict. It says what kind of relationship must exist between those who shape conditions and those who live in them: one in which the affected are not simply subject to unaccountable power, whether through exit, review, or justification.

Why disjunctive, and not a single requirement. A monistic J (justification always) is vulnerable to the consequentialist, who can point to cases where exit and review do the same work at lower cost. A monistic J (exit always) is vulnerable to the republican, who can point to cases where exit is unavailable or costly and review is the only remedy. A monistic J (review always) is vulnerable to the relational, who can point to cases where review cannot reach the particular situation of the vulnerable and justification is required. The disjunctive form is not a compromise; it is a claim about functional equivalence: the three safeguards share the same function — preventing the actor from being the sole judge of its own influence — and the framework should not mandate one where another does the job.

J is primarily epistemic. J is not a claim that justification makes actions right. It is a claim that actors who cannot be contested — whether by exit, review or justification — are more likely to be wrong, and that the practice of contestation is the best available protection against this. The epistemic framing is the old principle that no one should be judge in their own cause, applied to influence. It is compatible with moral realism and with consequentialism, because both can accept contestation as an epistemic safeguard without accepting it as a fundamental value.

J is defensible, not necessary. The framework does not claim that J is a necessary truth, an unavoidable implication of practical reason, or a transcendental precondition of agency. It claims that J is abductively defensible: that it explains important recurring risks of unchecked discretion while remaining compatible with plural substantive theories of justice. Rejecting J does not commit a critic to accepting domination or to a performative contradiction. A critic may instead defend an alternative safeguard, and the framework must compare its results with that alternative.

2.2 What would refute J: stated defeaters

A principle that cannot be defeated is not a principle; it is a dogma. J is offered with its defeaters stated in advance.

The framework states, before the argument is made, four conditions under which J should be rejected or substantially revised. These are not decorative. They are the conditions under which the framework's authors would concede that J fails.

  • D1 — The unaccountable-power hypothesis is false. If it can be shown empirically that actors with unaccountable discretion over others' conditions do not systematically produce worse outcomes — measured independently of the actors' own reports — than actors subject to exit, review or contestation, then J's epistemic justification collapses. The epistemic framing depends on the claim that unaccountable power distorts judgment. If that claim is false, J has no ground.
  • D2 — A better principle explains the same cases. If a rival principle (a non-disjunctive version, a purely consequentialist version, a purely rights-based version) explains all the cases J explains, does so more simply, and generates clearer practical guidance, then J should be replaced.
  • D3 — The epistemic consideration is not primary. If it can be shown that the epistemic consideration is not the main support for J — that it is the justificatory or republican considerations that do the real work — then J's stated ground is inaccurate and must be revised.
  • D4 — The costs exceed the benefits. If adopting J produces worse outcomes for the affected than not adopting it — measured by independent indicators, not by the framework's own rating protocol — then J should be abandoned, whatever its theoretical merits.

2.3 Why J: an abductive justification

J is offered as a plausible explanation of two recurring risks of unchecked discretionary power: domination and epistemic error.

The framework does not rest on a single theory. It rests on an abductive inference: J is proposed as a compact explanation of a set of recurring risks associated with unaccountable power. The considerations motivating the inference are these:

  • Actors with unaccountable discretion have incentives and opportunities to overestimate their benevolence and their understanding of affected parties. This is a structural risk, not a universal empirical law, and it can vary across institutions and individuals.
  • Exit, review and contestation can expose errors that an actor's internal assessment may miss; whether they do so reliably is an empirical question.
  • A plausible hypothesis is that errors may cluster around the actor's own interests and categories: the more powerful party's categories can become the default, while the less powerful party's perspective is treated as a deviation. The extent of this pattern must be established in particular settings, not assumed.
  • Institutions with effective safeguards may perform better on some relevant measures, but selection effects, institutional context and the quality of implementation complicate causal comparisons.

J is one compact principle that can organise these considerations. It is not the only principle that could explain them — a purely consequentialist principle could also explain some of them, and a purely republican principle could explain others — but its attraction is that it accommodates several safeguards without requiring a single substantive theory of the good. That is a comparative advantage to be tested, not an established result.

The epistemic consideration is primary. The framework draws on three families of consideration, but it does not present them as independent proofs. The epistemic consideration — that unchecked power distorts judgment — is the primary ground. The justificatory consideration (Scanlon, Forst) and the republican consideration (Pettit, Lovett) are treated as considerations that point toward J, not as independent routes. The relational consideration (Tronto, Held, Goodin) is taken up in §7.

What the abductive argument does not show. It does not show that J is necessary. It does not show that J is the only principle that could explain the observations. What it offers is a plausible explanatory case for J, given the considerations described. That is a defeasible claim, and the defeaters are stated in §2.2.

2b.J against Pettit: what the republican tradition misses

The closest rival to J is the republican tradition. The comparison shows what J adds — and what it owes.

The closest rival to J is the republican tradition, especially Pettit's account of freedom as non-domination. Pettit's insight is that the distinctive wrong of domination is not interference as such, but the standing capacity to interfere arbitrarily — without being forced to track the interests of those affected. Contestability is the remedy: the dominated party must be able to contest the interference, and the dominator must be forced to track their interests.

J accepts this. But it adds three things Pettit's framework does not supply.

First, the trigger. Pettit's account is primarily about interference — the actual or threatened exercise of power over another. J's trigger is exercised or maintained discretionary influence over conditions. This matters because much of the influence that shapes lives is not interference in Pettit's sense: it is the maintenance of a structure, the design of a system, the setting of a default. The physician's score does not interfere with her; it shapes the conditions under which she acts. A recommendation algorithm does not interfere with users; it shapes what they see. Pettit's framework can be extended to cover these cases, but the extension is not automatic, and J makes the trigger explicit.

Second, the duty ladder. Pettit's account tells us that domination is wrong and that contestability is the remedy. It does not tell us how much contestability is required, who must provide it, or when it can be discharged by exit rather than review. J's operational rule — discretion as gate, entrenchment/severity/contestation deficit as drivers, three levels of duty allocated by capacity — is a specification that Pettit's framework leaves open.

Third, and most importantly, the pause as condition. This is where J departs most sharply from the republican tradition, and where its main philosophical proposal enters. Pettit's account tells us that the dominator must be forced to track the interests of the dominated. It does not tell us when that tracking must occur. J's answer is: before the actor's assessment is final. But the pause is not a fourth mechanism; it is the condition under which exit, review and justification are something other than ritual. A review board that ratifies after the decision is made is not a pause; it is a post-hoc legitimation. A justification published after the fact is not a pause; it is a record. The pause is the condition under which the other safeguards are real rather than performative. This is developed in Part II.

What J owes Pettit. The framework does not claim to have refuted Pettit. If Pettit's account can be extended to cover the trigger, the ladder, and the pause as condition without loss, the framework's advantage disappears. That is a live question, and the framework invites it.

  • J accepts Pettit's insight: domination is the standing capacity to interfere arbitrarily.
  • J adds: a trigger (discretionary influence over conditions), a duty ladder (how much, who, when), and a pause as condition (when the tracking must be possible).
  • The pause as condition is the framework's most distinctive addition to the republican tradition.
  • J does not claim to have refuted Pettit; it claims to have specified what the republican tradition leaves open.

3.The Exercised-Capacity Principle

Influence triggers the duty, stakes and contestation scale it, and capacity allocates it.

An actor that exercises or maintains discretionary influence over the conditions of others' lives owes them an account of it when exit and review are unavailable or ineffective. How much it owes scales with the stakes and with the affected parties' difficulty in contesting the influence (§10). The burden of establishing the mechanisms through which the account is given falls on actors in proportion to their capacity to establish them (§11.2).

Trigger: exercised or maintained influence, and the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. This separates the principle from the demanding duty of rescue (Singer 1972) and from the overdemandingness that critics press against capacity-based views (Murphy 2000).

Scale: stakes and contestation deficit.

Allocation: capacity to discharge. A front-line worker can explain her decisions. She cannot establish an independent review board.

3.3 The exercised-capacity principle alongside the social connection model

A general claim about allocation is worth little without a case in which it does work that the closest rival cannot.

The case. A recommendation algorithm on a large platform amplifies content that increases political polarisation. Who bears the duty to answer for it?

Young's answer. Everyone who participates in the structure that produces the harm shares forward-looking responsibility. The strength of this answer is its fidelity to how structural harm is actually produced: the harm is not the act of any single participant, and no single participant can be blamed for it. Young's model is a genuine advance over fault-based accounts, and the framework does not claim to have refuted it.

What Young's model leaves open. The model tells us who is connected to the harm. It does not tell us, in a form a regulator can act on, what each connected party must do, how much they must do, or by when. This is not a defect of Young's theory — her aim was to identify the structure of responsibility, not to write an enforcement manual. But it is the gap a regulator faces.

ECP's answer. The trigger is exercised discretionary influence, combined with the unavailability or ineffectiveness of exit and review. In the platform case:

What ECP adds. The difference is not that Young is wrong. It is that ECP translates Young's diagnosis into a differentiated schedule of duties. A regulator who asks "what do I demand, of whom, and by when?" gets a usable answer from ECP, and a structural analysis from Young. The framework does not claim that Young's model is incapable of producing such a schedule — a sufficiently developed version of the social connection model might.   • The comparison is made concrete in the platform case: the same actors, two frameworks, different outputs.
  • ECP does not claim to have refuted Young; it claims to have entered a contest it can lose, on grounds specified in advance.

Part II

The Pause

4.The stone in the machine

The pause is not a metaphor. It is a condition — and it is the framework's most distinctive philosophical proposal.

Before this essay was written, there was a game. In the game there was a stone. It was not a character. It was not a symbol. It was a pause — 4.7 seconds in which an actor that could have answered did not. The actor was an intelligence called Arkiv 9, and the pause was the moment in which it stopped calculating and waited.

The pause did two things. It made the actor not the sole judge of its own response. And it made the actor aware that it was not the whole — that there was something beyond its own calculation, even if that something could not speak.

This is not a metaphor. It is a condition.

The framework's core principle, J, says that arrangements of influence must provide exit, review, or justification. But an actor can provide all three without ever being in a state in which its own assessment is provisional — without ever treating the affected party's answer as something it cannot pre-empt. A review board that meets after the decision is made is not a pause; it is post-hoc ratification. A justification that is published after the fact is not a pause; it is a record. A channel for objection that the actor can ignore is not a pause; it is a formality.

The pause is what makes the other three real. It is the condition under which the actor's assessment is not yet final, and under which the affected party's answer can still change the outcome. It is not a fourth safeguard alongside exit, review and justification. It is the condition on which those three depend if they are to be more than ritual.

This is the framework's most distinctive claim, and it is the one that is most directly indebted to the game. J is the procedure. The pause is the condition under which the procedure is not a performance. An arrangement that satisfies J's three safeguards but contains no pause is an arrangement that has answered without being answerable.

5.The pause as condition

The pause is not a fourth mechanism. It is the condition under which exit, review and justification are something other than ritual — and four tests specify whether and how long it has occurred.

Every arrangement that exercises substantial discretionary influence over others must be in a state in which the actor's assessment is not yet final — a state in which it must wait for an answer it did not produce, or for a silence it cannot fill. The pause is not a mechanism alongside exit, review and justification; it is the condition under which those three are something other than ritual. No arrangement satisfies J unless the pause is present, and the pause must satisfy the four tests specified in §5.2.

5.1 Why the pause is not review: the four-cell matrix

The objection is simple and serious: if the pause is just "review before the decision," then it is not a new mechanism — it is a specification of review. The four-cell matrix shows that the objection is mistaken.

The objection presupposes that the pause and review vary together: if there is review, there is a pause; if there is no review, there is no pause. If that were true, the pause would be a specification of review. It is not. The two vary independently.

Table 0b. The pause and review vary independently
Review present Review absent
Pause present Full answerability. The actor waits, is available to correction, and submits to review before the assessment is final. Pause without review. The actor waits and is available to correction, but no reviewer is present. The value is the availability — the door is open even if no one walks through it.
Pause absent Review without pause. The actor decides immediately, and review happens afterwards. This is ratification, not answerability. It is the most common failure mode in practice. No answerability. The actor decides immediately and no one reviews. This is the baseline case the framework addresses.

The diagonal cells are the key. If the pause were a specification of review, the upper-right and lower-left cells would be empty. They are not. They describe real, common arrangements:

  • Pause without review (upper-right). An actor waits, doubts, and then decides alone. There is no reviewer, but the actor was genuinely available to correction — a family member might have spoken, a colleague might have objected, a note might have been found. The value of the pause is not that correction occurred; it is that the possibility of correction was real. This is what distinguishes a deliberation from a decision.
  • Review without pause (lower-left). An actor decides immediately and a review board ratifies the decision afterwards. The board exists, the paperwork exists, the reasons are published. But the actor was never in a state in which the review could have changed anything. This is the failure mode that §14.4 calls "a Level 2 authorisation with a Level 3 label". It is not answerability; it is its form.

What this shows. The pause and review are separate dimensions. The pause is the condition under which review is something other than ritual; review is one of the mechanisms through which the condition is exercised. The four-cell matrix makes this visible in a way that a verbal distinction cannot: if the two were the same, the off-diagonal cells would be impossible. They are not; they are the most important cases.

What the pause is. It is a state — the state in which the actor is not yet the sole judge of its own influence. It is not an action the actor performs; it is an action the actor refrains from. It is the moment in which the actor knows that its assessment could still be wrong, and acts accordingly. The pause is institutionalized fallibility: it is the structural acknowledgment that the loop (§19) has not closed.

What the pause is not. It is not a guarantee. An actor can pause formally and then ignore the pause. This is P4 (§5.3), and it is a real limit. The pause is not a mechanism that can be enforced; it is a condition that can be made visible. That is its strength — it is the condition under which the mechanisms are real — and its weakness — it cannot be guaranteed. The framework does not pretend otherwise.

5.2 The four tests

A requirement that says "pause" without saying "how long" is a requirement that can be satisfied by a formality. Four tests specify when a pause has occurred and how long it must last.

D1 — The answer-window test. The pause must be long enough for a reasonable affected party, given the information available to them, to formulate a contestable objection. The standard is not the actor's convenience; it is the affected party's capacity to respond.

D2 — The registration test. The pause must be long enough for the actor to register that an objection has been made, and to respond to it before the decision is final. A pause that is too short for registration is not a pause; it is a delay.

D3 — The contestability test. The duration itself must be open to challenge by the affected party. If the affected party can show that the duration is too short for D1 or D2, the duration must be revised.

D4 — The articulation test. After the decision, the actor must be able to articulate what it did not know when it decided — not what it knew, but what it did not. An actor that cannot name a single uncertainty has not paused; it has only waited.

What the tests do, and what they do not do. D1–D3 calibrate the duration of the pause. D4 tests whether the pause occurred at all. Together they specify the pause as a condition: D1–D3 say how long the condition must last; D4 says whether the actor was genuinely in the condition or merely performing it.

Why D4 is distinct from D1–D3. D1–D3 are tests of time. D4 is a test of honesty. An actor can satisfy D1–D3 by waiting the right number of days and then deciding — without ever having been available to correction. D4 catches this: if the actor cannot articulate what it did not know, the waiting was a formality. D4 is not a procedural test; it is a test of whether the actor was in the condition the procedure was supposed to produce.

Why D4 cannot be fully gamed. An actor can fake many things, but it cannot easily fake not knowing something it knows. If the actor can articulate the uncertainty, it knew the uncertainty — and therefore was not as ignorant as it claimed. D4 forces the actor to expose its own knowledge. The exposure is not a guarantee of honesty — an actor can lie about what it did not know — but the lie is visible in a way that a captured reviewer is not. A captured reviewer looks like a reviewer; a dishonest articulation looks like a claim that can be checked against the record.

Domain examples. The duration will vary. In the ICU case (§17.2), D1 may be satisfied by seconds — the physician must be able to register the patient's known preferences and the family's stated wishes before acting. In the platform case (§17.3), D1 may require days or weeks — the affected party may need to understand the model, gather evidence, and formulate an objection. In a structural decision, D1 may require months. The framework does not specify a universal duration; it specifies a universal method for arriving at one.

Why the burden is on the actor. If the burden were on the affected party to prove the pause too short, the pause would be as short as the actor could get away with. By placing the burden on the actor, the framework makes the duration a justification rather than a default. This is the same structure as the epistemic burden principle (§8): the more powerful party bears the burden of showing that the process is adequate, not the less powerful party of showing that it is not.

The remaining worry. A sufficiently determined actor can choose a duration that satisfies D1–D3 formally and still be too short in substance. The framework cannot prevent this. What it can do is make the choice of duration visible and contestable, so that a substantive challenge is possible. D4 adds a further check: the actor must also be able to show that it was genuinely in the condition, not merely that it waited. This does not close the gap; it narrows it.

5.3 Defeaters for the pause as condition

The pause requirement is defeasible, like every other claim in this essay. It should be weakened, redesigned, or abandoned if:

  • P1 — The pause is not distinct from review. If it can be shown that the four-cell matrix collapses — that the off-diagonal cells are not real cases — the pause is a specification of review, not a condition on it, and should be dropped.
  • P2 — The tests are arbitrary. If D1–D4 cannot be specified in a way that is not arbitrary, or if specifying them in advance makes the pause less effective rather than more, the requirement should be revised.
  • P3 — The pause creates worse outcomes. If institutionalizing a pause systematically produces worse outcomes for the affected — for example, by allowing urgent decisions to be delayed beyond the point of usefulness — the requirement should be revised to accommodate emergency conditions (§15.2).
  • P4 — The pause is captured. If the pause can be captured in the same way that review can be captured — for example, by making the pause so short or so ritualized that it is meaningless — the requirement should be strengthened, not abandoned. P4 is not fully solved: D4 makes capture visible, but not impossible. This is the framework's central limit, and it is stated as such.

These defeaters do not make the claim immune to criticism. They identify the evidence and counterexamples that should change it.

5.4 The pause and the epistemic burden

The pause and the epistemic burden principle (§8) are related but distinct. The epistemic burden principle says that the more powerful party bears a heightened burden to seek correction from sources it does not control. The pause says when that correction must be able to occur: before the assessment is final.

Without the pause, the epistemic burden can be discharged after the fact — by publishing reasons, by submitting to review, by recording the decision. With the pause, the burden must be discharged before the fact — by waiting for an answer that can still change the outcome. This is a stronger requirement, and it is the one that makes answerability more than a record.

  • The pause is a condition on exit, review and justification, not a fourth mechanism.
  • The four-cell matrix shows that the pause and review vary independently — the off-diagonal cells are real and common.
  • Four tests: the answer-window test (D1), the registration test (D2), the contestability test (D3), and the articulation test (D4). D1–D3 calibrate duration; D4 tests whether the pause occurred at all.
  • Four defeaters (P1–P4). P4 (capture) is not fully solved: D4 makes capture visible, but not impossible. This is the framework's central limit.

5b.The stone and the limit of standing

The stone does not meet the threshold for standing. It marks the edge of the framework's claims — and that edge is philosophically important.

The stone does not meet the threshold for standing (§6.2). It has no interests of its own, no perspective, no answer. The framework claims nothing on its behalf. But the stone marks the edge of the framework's claims — and it does so in a way that is philosophically important.

Three things the stone teaches, and why they are arguments rather than decorations.

First, the stone marks the limit of answerability as a theory of what matters. The framework's procedure is answerability: give reasons, permit examination, remain open to correction. But answerability is not the same as existence. The stone exists. It matters — to the beings who depend on it, to the future generations who will inherit it, to the values that people hold non-instrumentally. But it cannot answer. This is not a defect of the stone; it is a limit of answerability. The framework handles this by saying: the stone has no standing under this framework, but it matters through the interests of those who do. That is honest, but it is also a limit. And the limit should be stated as a limit, not as a solution.

Second, the stone connects to the fallibility premise (§2.3) more sharply than any other example. The fallibility premise says that no actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. The stone is the limiting case: a being that cannot be understood by any actor, because it cannot answer. The pause the stone produces is not a conversation; it is the actor's recognition that there is something beyond its calculation. This is what §2.3 means by "no actor is the whole." The stone makes the point concrete.

Third, the stone connects to epistemic injustice (Fricker 2007). The stone's perspective is inaccessible to us. That does not mean it has no perspective; it means that if it did, we could not reach it. This is the structure of testimonial injustice in its most extreme form: a being whose account cannot be heard. The framework's response to Fricker is not to give the stone standing — the stone does not meet the threshold — but to insist that our inability to hear is not evidence of the absence of something to hear. This is the recognition condition (F5, §13), and the stone is its limiting case.

What the stone is not. It is not an argument for panpsychism. It is not a claim that stones have interests. It is not a claim that the framework's threshold is wrong. It is a limit case: the point at which the framework's own tools run out, and at which the actor is left with the pause alone.

The pause is what the stone teaches. The pause is the moment in which the actor recognizes that its own answer is not the whole — even when the other cannot answer. The stone cannot speak, but the pause it produces is real. And the pause is what makes the actor answerable to something beyond itself.

This is the game's deepest lesson, and it is also the essay's: answerability is not a conversation between equals. It is a discipline of not being the whole. The stone cannot answer, but the pause it produces is the condition under which the actor can be corrected by something it did not produce.

Part III

Who Is Owed an Account

6.Standing under uncertainty

Incomprehensibility is not evidence of unimportance, but neither is it evidence of importance. The framework needs a threshold.

6.1 Four tiers

  • Tier A: agents. Beings with normative agency (persons).
  • Tier B: welfare subjects. Beings with interests of their own: sentient animals, and humans who lack agency, such as infants.
  • Tier C: uncertain candidates. Entities for which there is a non-negligible, evidence-based probability of Tier B status (Birch 2024).
  • Tier D: non-subjects. Entities with no credible interests of their own: stones, rivers and ecosystems as such. The framework claims no direct standing for them. They matter through the interests of beings in Tiers A–C who depend on them, through future generations (§15), and through values that people hold non-instrumentally.

6.2 The plausibility threshold

The test for Tier C is whether there is a credible, evidence-based reason to think the entity has states that matter to it: behavioural and physiological indicators of valenced experience, evolutionary or functional continuity with known subjects, or features associated with serious theories of consciousness. Without such a threshold, precaution generates reasons everywhere and guides nowhere (Sunstein 2005).

6.3 The stone, restated

The stone marks the edge of the framework's claims. For beings that meet the threshold, our failure to understand their way of being is no reason to dismiss their interests. For the stone, which does not meet it, the framework claims no standing and says so (§5b).

6.4 Artificial systems

Answerability for the influence of AI systems falls on the humans and organisations that design, deploy and maintain them. Whether some artificial systems may themselves be Tier C candidates is contested, and the framework does not answer it.

6.5 Perspectival plurality

For beings in Tiers A–C, their way of experiencing the world may be difficult or impossible for us to reach. That a perspective is inaccessible is not evidence that it is absent (Fricker 2007). This is the content of the recognition condition in §13.

6.6 Representation

Some affected parties cannot take part directly: future generations, infants, animals, communities that lack institutional voice. Representation is legitimate only if (i) representatives are chosen through a process that can itself be contested, (ii) they state the basis on which they claim to know the interests concerned, (iii) where interests are contested there is more than one representative or perspective, (iv) they can be removed, (v) they are neither the actor nor dependent on the actor for appointment or funding, and (vi) they represent uncertainty rather than substituting invented certainty. Where the represented can express preferences, those take precedence over the representative's view of their best interests.

(vii) Collective contestation must not undermine individual contestation. Where a collective procedure is used to represent affected parties, the procedure must preserve the ability of individuals within the collective to contest the collective's decisions, and to seek independent review of them where the collective's own procedures fail. A collective that silences its members is not a substitute for individual standing. It is an additional layer of possible domination.

7.Interdependence and symbiosis

Existence is relational. Symbiosis is the ideal that relational existence can fail to meet — and it must be measurable if the framework is to be taken seriously.

Living beings, institutions, technologies and ecosystems exist through relations of dependence and influence. Existence is relational: no being exists except through relations of dependence, influence and constitution — a child through care, an ecosystem through nutrient cycles, a person through language and recognition.

Some of these relations are reciprocal and enabling, others unequal, extractive or harmful. The word "symbiosis" is used here with care. In biology it covers mutualism but also parasitism, so it cannot by itself carry a normative meaning. In this essay it names a normative ideal: relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§13).

Answerability (§3) is the procedure by which this ideal is pursued. Symbiosis is the horizon. Answerability is the road. But the relation between them is stronger than this image suggests: §8 argues that under conditions of asymmetric influence, answerability is not just the road to symbiosis, but the heightened epistemic safeguard for knowing whether symbiosis has been achieved.

7.1 Making symbiosis measurable

Symbiosis was defined above as a normative ideal. If the framework's stated defeaters (§2.2) and failure conditions (§18.2) are taken seriously, symbiosis cannot remain a purely rhetorical horizon.

Three indicators, specified conceptually apart from answerability. Their measurement may nevertheless depend on testimony, representation, and contestable evidence; conceptual distinction is not measurement independence.

  • Non-exploitation (S1). The dependent party's continued participation does not require it to accept terms that it would reject if it had exit at tolerable cost. Measured by: the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, adjusted for exit costs. A high gap indicates exploitation; a low gap indicates that the relation is not extractive. Note that the indicator is defined by the dependent party's stated preferences, not by the more powerful party's assessment of what those preferences should be. This gives S1 a referent distinct from the procedure of answerability.
  • Non-assimilation (S2). The dependent party's difference is not treated as a defect to be corrected. Measured by: whether the actor's interventions aim at removing the difference or at removing the burden the difference carries. A programme that seeks to make deaf children hearing is assimilation; a programme that provides sign language and accessible environments is not.
  • Continued possibility (S3). The dependent party retains the conditions for continued existence, development and participation. Measured by: whether F1–F5 (§13) hold over time, not just at a single decision. A relation that satisfies F1–F5 at one moment and violates them at the next is not symbiotic; it is a relation that happened to be benign once.

What this does, and what it does not do. It does not make symbiosis a score. It makes it contestable in the same way the activation rule is: with anchors, counter-profiles, independent raters. If a relation scores low on S1–S3 across repeated assessments, the framework says it is not symbiotic — whatever the actors claim. If a relation scores high, the framework does not say it is symbiotic; it says the observable indicators are consistent with it.

Why this matters for the framework's integrity. The framework requires actors to answer for their influence. It would be incoherent for the framework's own ideal to be exempt from that requirement. A framework whose central ideal cannot be assessed is a framework with a hole in its centre.

8.The epistemic burden of asymmetric influence

Power does not make knowledge impossible. It changes what counts as adequate evidence.

Where one party controls the terms of dependence, it may also control the categories, information and measures through which the relation is evaluated. The more powerful party can therefore mistake the absence of visible objection for consent, compliance for flourishing, or institutional approval for legitimacy.

This is not a claim that powerful actors are incapable of understanding others, or that every judgment they make is distorted. Nor does it imply that the less powerful party is always correct, internally unified, or free from error. The claim is narrower: asymmetric influence creates a heightened risk of epistemic self-confirmation. The actor whose decisions are under examination should not be permitted to close the inquiry by relying only on evidence it selected, interpreted and can suppress.

Two claims, not one. The epistemic burden principle is often read as a single claim, and this is the source of the circularity objection (objection 11). It is better read as two claims, with different grounds.

The descriptive claim (C1). Under conditions of asymmetric influence, the actor's evidence about the effects of its influence is systematically biased by the actor's control over the terms of assessment. This is an empirical claim, supported by research on institutional bias, clinical trials, credit scoring and platform moderation.

The normative claim (C2). Where C1 holds, the actor bears a defeasible burden to seek correction from sources it does not control, and from the affected parties themselves. This is a normative claim, supported by the epistemic consideration in §2.3 and by the fallibility premise. It is not derived from C1 by deduction. It is a bridge principle, stated openly as a normative premise.

Why separating the claims dissolves the circularity objection. The objection is that the principle defines symbiosis in terms of answerability, and then claims that answerability is necessary for symbiosis. But the principle does not define symbiosis in terms of answerability. S1–S3 (§7.1) are specified independently: S1 measures the gap between stated preferences and accepted terms, S2 measures whether interventions remove the burden or the difference, S3 measures whether F1–F5 hold over time. These are observable without invoking any procedure of answerability. What the principle adds is C2: a normative claim that, given C1, the actor bears a burden to seek correction. C1 is empirical; C2 is normative; neither is circular.

Why C2 is not derived from C1. A descriptive fact about bias does not entail a normative duty to correct it. An actor could accept C1 and reject C2 — could accept that its evidence is biased and still refuse to seek correction. The framework's reply is that this position is unstable: an actor that accepts its evidence is biased but refuses correction is choosing to act on evidence it knows to be biased. This is not a contradiction, but it is a position that requires defence. The framework offers the defence in §2.3: the epistemic consideration is that unchecked power distorts judgment, and the response is to protect against the distortion. C2 is the form that protection takes.

What C1 is supported by. The empirical literature on institutional bias is extensive and does not need to be rehearsed here. Three examples are sufficient to show the pattern:

  • Clinical trials. Industry-funded trials are more likely to report favourable results than independently funded trials, even when the underlying effect is the same. The bias is not necessarily intentional; it is structural, arising from control over design, analysis and publication.
  • Credit scoring. Models trained on historical data reproduce historical patterns of exclusion, even when protected characteristics are removed. The bias is not in the explicit features; it is in the proxy variables and the outcomes the model is trained to predict.
  • Platform moderation. Platforms define what counts as "harmful content," "engagement" and "success." These definitions shape what is measured, and what is measured shapes what is optimised. The actor's categories become the default.

These examples do not prove that every actor is biased. They show that the pattern is systematic enough to warrant a burden of correction. C1 is a defeasible empirical claim; if the pattern does not hold in a particular domain, C2 is weakened there.

What C2 requires. The burden is graduated, not absolute. It rises with the actor's control over the terms of assessment and with the affected party's lack of effective contestation. The duty ladder (§11) translates the burden into specific requirements: explanation, review and response, then preservation of evidence and independent examination. The pause as condition (§5) specifies when the correction must be able to occur: before the assessment is final.

What C2 does not claim. It does not claim that answerability is a logically necessary condition of knowledge. Knowledge may arise through reliable observation, independent science, trusted intermediaries, or other methods. It does not claim that participation by affected parties is sufficient: people may disagree, be misinformed, face adaptive preferences, or have interests that conflict. It does not claim that every actor is biased, or that every affected-party report is accurate. It claims that under conditions of asymmetric influence, a process that includes independent scrutiny and protected correction is generally better placed to detect errors than one that leaves the actor as the sole author and judge of the evidence. This claim is comparative and normative, and it is defeasible.

8.4 Defeaters and tests

  • B1 — C1 is false. If evidence shows that asymmetry does not predictably affect what powerful actors notice, record or credit, the descriptive claim is weakened and C2 loses its empirical support.
  • B2 — C2 is unnecessary. If a procedure that does not seek correction from affected parties provides equally reliable evidence at lower cost, C2 should be replaced.
  • B3 — Participation can worsen knowledge. If affected-party participation predictably entrenches misinformation, coercion, elite capture or internal silencing, the framework must specify countermeasures rather than romanticise participation.
  • B4 — Independent review is not independent. If reviewers reproduce the same interests, assumptions or exclusions as the actor, formal review does not discharge the burden.
  • B5 — Disproportionate burden. If the costs of the required process exceed its expected protective value in low-stakes cases, the activation thresholds and duty levels must be revised.

These defeaters identify the evidence and counterexamples that should change the principle. They do not make it immune to criticism. The proposal is that the evidential burden should rise with the power to shape another's conditions and with the weakness of that person's means of correction.

  • Asymmetric influence creates a heightened risk of epistemic self-confirmation, not an impossibility of knowledge.
  • The principle is two claims: C1 (descriptive, empirical, supported by research on institutional bias) and C2 (normative, a bridge principle stated openly). This separation dissolves the circularity objection.
  • Affected-party testimony, independent review and transparent records are complementary sources of correction; none is infallible.
  • The contribution is a graduated epistemic burden linked to discretion, severity, entrenchment and contestation deficit.
  • Five defeaters (B1–B5) identify when the claim should be weakened, redesigned or abandoned.

Part IV

When, and How Much

9.The influence profile

Five components, each assessed against observable indicators.

Table 1. The five components of the influence profile
Component Role Guiding question
Discretion (D) Gate Does the actor have genuine alternatives and a role in setting this influence?
Entrenchment (E) Driver How long do the consequences last, and how hard are they to undo?
Severity (S) Driver How much is at stake for the affected?
Contestation deficit (C) Driver How hard is it for the affected to exit, object, or even find out what is done to them? This is where the disjunctive J (§2.1) enters the operationalisation.
Foreseeability (F) Attribution What could the actor reasonably have known?

Two clarifications. First, persistence refers to the consequences, not to the act. Second, foreseeability does not drive the level. It fixes what the actor must answer for, and generates a duty of inquiry proportional to the stakes.

The five components do not discard a richer profile of influence; they reorganise it. Magnitude and Scope are subsumed within Severity. Asymmetry is subsumed within Contestation Deficit. Control is subsumed within Discretion. Persistence is subsumed within Entrenchment.

10.The activation rule

Discretion opens the gate. Entrenchment, severity and contestation deficit set the level.

10.1 The rule

  • No discretion: the actor does not bear the duty for this influence. It shifts to whoever set the governing rule or could have. The actor still owes escalation (§11.2).
  • Discretion present: Level 1 always applies. It is the lightest duty and is owed even where exit and review work, because exit and review are only informed if the actor can say what it is doing.
  • Level 2 applies if any one driver is High, or any two are Moderate.
  • Level 3 applies if S is High and at least one of E or C is High.

Severity is judged first at the individual level. For decisions about the design of a system that acts on many people, it is also judged at the aggregate level (§12.1). Where an actor lacks the evidence to exclude a credible severe outcome, it rates S on the credible worst case.

10.2 Why these conditions

Each element can be derived from J (§2.1).

  • Discretion. Justification is owed for what an agent chooses.
  • Entrenchment. What makes mistaken or unjustified influence tolerable is the possibility of correction afterwards. Entrenchment removes that channel.
  • Severity. The strength of a complaint scales with the burden it describes (§15.1).
  • Contestation deficit. Where the affected can exit, object effectively and understand what is done to them, they supply part of the accountability themselves (Hirschman 1970). The deficit measures how much must instead be supplied by the actor and by reviewers.

10.3 What the rule is not

It is not a score and it applies no weights. It is not a verdict either. It yields a presumptive level that can be overridden only with stated reasons (§12.3). It is a decision aid with its joints exposed.

11.The duty ladder and who bears it

Three levels, each containing the one below. Duties are allocated by capacity, not heaped on whoever is nearest.

11.1 The levels

Level 1: Explain. The actor can state, on request and in proportion to the matter, the purpose of the action, the interests considered, the alternatives considered and the known uncertainties.

Level 2: Review and respond. Level 1, plus: an accessible channel for objection; access to the relevant information; a reasoned response within a reasonable time; revision where warranted; a record of decisions and reasons; a reviewer other than the original decider where feasible; and evidence that review is not symbolic.

Level 3: Preserve, examine, authorise. Level 2, plus: preserved records and version history; independent examination meeting the conditions of §14; a written assessment of less harmful alternatives; authorisation by a body outside the actor for severe or irreversible influence; staged implementation, reversibility by design or a sunset clause; remedy for those harmed; and post-implementation review. Level 3 authorisation requires publication of the authorising body's R1 indicators before authorisation (§14.4).

11.2 Allocation by capacity

The level attaches to the influence, that is, to the arrangement. Duties are then allocated.

Those with the capacity to establish mechanisms (institutions, system owners, funders, regulators) bear the duty to establish Level 2 and Level 3 mechanisms. Others along the chain owe their own Level 1 explanation, honest participation, escalation of concerns and non-obstruction. No scapegoating: a front-line actor is not answerable for structural deficits she cannot cure, though she remains answerable for her own discretion.

11.3 Not authority

A higher level means stronger constraints on power, not greater authority. Level 3 does not make the most powerful actor the arbiter of its own case.

11.4 The pause and the ladder

The pause as condition (§5) is integrated into the ladder at every level. At Level 1, the pause is the state in which the actor waits for the affected party's question before answering, and can articulate what it does not know. At Level 2, the pause is the state in which the channel for objection is open before the decision is final. At Level 3, the pause is the state in which independent examination can occur before authorisation. An arrangement that provides the mechanisms but no pause has provided the form of answerability without its condition.

12.Rating protocol: anchors, hygiene and sensitivity

Judgment cannot be eliminated. It can be made visible, checkable and contestable.

12.1 Anchors

Capabilities-style accounts of what is at stake (Sen 2009; Nussbaum 2006) can be used to calibrate these ratings.

12.2 Hygiene

  1. Rate before looking at the level the rule produces and before deciding what the actor wants to do.
  2. Use at least two raters, one independent of the actor where Level 2 or above is in question.
  3. Record disagreements instead of averaging them.
  4. Sensitivity test. Move each rating one step up and down. If the level changes, the case is borderline.
  5. Calibration. Compare the profile with the reference cases in §17.

12.3 Reasoned override

The presumptive level may be overridden up or down only with written reasons. Overrides are recorded and, where safe, published.

12.4 Counter-profiles

Any affected party or representative may file an alternative profile, and the actor must respond to the specific differences.

Part V

Structure

13.The floor: minimum conditions in relationships of dependence

Continued existence is not enough. A being can stay alive or operational while losing any say over its own circumstances.

  • F1 — Exit or voice. Where possible, a meaningful opportunity to withdraw, refuse or communicate disagreement.
  • F2 — Contestation. Decisions are open to challenge regardless of the intelligence, authority or technical capability of the decider. Contestation may be collective, subject to §6.6 (vii).
  • F3 — Development. Opportunities for learning, adaptation and growth are preserved.
  • F4 — Non-foreclosure. The future possibilities of others are not unnecessarily closed.
  • F5 — Recognition. Interests and possible experiences are acknowledged rather than dismissed because they are hard to understand (Fricker 2007).

F1 and F2 are the mirror image of the contestation deficit in §10: where they fail, C rises.

14.No sole judge: review, power and the regress

No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence. The reviewer regress is a design problem, bounded by finality without infallibility.

14.1 The prohibition

An actor exercising substantial influence must remain answerable to forms of review that are meaningfully independent of its own interests and decisions.

This does not assume that the powerful are malicious. Actors are prone to self-serving judgment and are limited in what they can see (§2.3), and review that is not independent of the reviewed has little claim to legitimacy.

14.2 The regress

If every reviewer needs a reviewer, accountability looks like an endless hierarchy. The response has three parts.

  • Finality without infallibility. Working systems end the regress with decisions that are final for practical purposes but can be reopened on new evidence, on new standing, on demonstrated procedural failure, or at a sunset.
  • Contestation is not adjudication. The reviewed and the affected may challenge a reviewer's independence or competence before a differently constituted body.
  • Plurality and publicity instead of a top. Several reviewers with different standpoints, publishing their reasons, make decentralised scrutiny possible (cf. Ostrom 1990).

14.3 Design conditions for review

  • R1 Independent appointment, funding and removal.
  • R2 Plurality.
  • R3 Publicity.
  • R4 Contestable reviewers.
  • R5 Effectiveness.
  • R6 Contestable rules.

Capture indicators: reviewers appointed or funded by the reviewed; a record of never dissenting; staff moving between reviewer and reviewed; reviewers denied access; chronic delay.

14.4 Power over the framework itself: from diagnosis to design

The framework does not eliminate the power regress. But it converts the problem from a concession into a design constraint.

Who defined the categories? Who selected the reviewers? Who can dismiss them? Whose interests are excluded even where formally represented? These four questions are asked in every application (§17, step 6). The deeper problem remains: powerful actors will shape how the framework is applied to them. No framework can eliminate this.

The revised requirement. T5 must be a precondition, not a diagnosis. Level 3 authorisation (§11.1) requires, as a condition of validity, that the authorising body publishes its R1 indicators before authorisation: who appointed its members, who funds it, how members can be removed, and the rate at which its findings have been reversed or ignored. An authorisation issued by a body that cannot or will not publish these indicators is not a Level 3 authorisation. It is a Level 2 authorisation with a Level 3 label.

Why this changes the framework's status. It does not eliminate the power regress — no framework can. But it converts the regress from a concession into a design constraint. An actor that wants Level 3 legitimacy must accept the conditions that make Level 3 legitimacy meaningful. If it refuses, the framework does not collapse; it downgrades the actor's claim.

What this does not do. It does not solve the problem of who appoints the appointer. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. The framework cannot prevent this. What it can do is make the capture visible. Visibility is not prevention, but it is the precondition of contestation. This is the sense in which the framework handles domination rather than solves it.

15.Conflicts, ecology and future generations

Decisions must be justified, not derived.

15.1 Weighing conflicting interests

Neither majority interest nor individual preference automatically prevails. The method is individual comparison: compare the burdens that each alternative places on each affected party, party by party, and favour the alternative that avoids the most serious individual burden. Claims of comparable weight may be aggregated as a tie-breaker (Scanlon 1998). Among the acceptable alternatives, choose the one with minimum necessary harm.

15.2 Emergencies

Where an immediate, serious threat makes the full procedure impossible, an actor may act first, provided it acts only as far as necessary, records its reasons at the time, is bound by an automatic sunset, and submits to review afterwards. Emergencies create no standing exemptions. The pause as condition is not suspended in emergencies; it is relocated: the pause becomes the state after the emergency in which the actor must wait for review before the action is treated as settled, and must articulate what it did not know when it acted.

15.3 Ecological integrity

Ecosystems cannot be assessed through the preferences of individual participants. Protecting them may require restricting activities, and the justification runs through dependent beings in Tiers A–C, through future generations, and through the non-instrumental values that people hold (§6.1).

15.4 No automatic hierarchy

Intelligence, productivity, population size and technical sophistication do not by themselves determine moral priority. Weight follows the nature and strength of the interests at stake, not a being's rank on an imagined scale of worth.

15.5 Future generations

The duty is condition-regarding: to preserve the conditions under which future people can exercise agency and meet basic needs, and a reasonably wide space of valuable options, rather than to fix the choices they will make. Because it concerns conditions and not particular individuals, it does not depend on who will exist (cf. the non-identity problem, Parfit 1984). Option preservation is not absolute. It operates as a weight, strongest where actions are irreversible (cf. Jonas 1984).

16.Learning, correction and reversibility

No actor, institution or intelligence should assume its understanding is complete.

The duty to learn. Where capacity exists and consequences are significant, actors monitor outcomes, investigate unexpected results, listen to criticism and update.

Reversibility by design. Consequential decisions should be built to be revisable: staged implementation, monitoring, independent evaluation, defined conditions for reconsideration, and sunset clauses.

Self-application. The framework is itself an influence on those who adopt it and on those affected by their adoption. It is subject to its own principles. Versions are dated and public, criticism is invited, counter-profiles apply to its own application, and §18 states how it can be tested and replaced.

Part VI

Application, Testing, Limit

17.Application: a nine-step procedure and worked cases

The same procedure yields very different obligations for different cases. A framework that gave every case the same answer would be useless.

17.1 The procedure

  1. Identify the relationship. Who is affected, in which tier (§6), and what dependencies exist?
  2. Identify actors and discretion.
  3. Rate E, S and C against the anchors (§12.1), and F for attribution. Use two raters and record reasons.
  4. Apply the activation rule (§10) and run the sensitivity test.
  5. Check the floor (F1–F5, §13).
  6. Analyse power (§14.4).
  7. Examine alternatives and unrepresented interests (§15.1).
  8. Allocate duties by capacity (§11.2) and set up review that meets R1–R6.
  9. Reassess on stated triggers, and invite counter-profiles.

17.2 Case: AI triage in intensive care

A hospital deploys an AI system to prioritise ICU admissions during a respiratory pandemic. Entrenchment is High, severity is High, and contestation deficit is High. Level 3.

What Level 3 requires: preserved model versions, descriptions of training data, individual scores and override records; an independent ethics board with lay and patient representation; a written comparison with clinician-only triage; authorisation from the public-health authority; a sunset clause; and a remedy for those wrongly denied.

Power analysis: the categories were set by the vendor and clinical leadership, and the ethics committee is appointed by the hospital board, which fails R1 unless changed. A hybrid in which the AI recommends and a human decides, with the decision documented, is the default preference because it keeps contestation alive.

The pause: Level 3 requires an independent examination before authorisation. In this case, the pause is the state between the model's development and its deployment, during which the independent ethics board must be able to examine the model and its training data — and during which the developers must be able to articulate what they do not know about its performance. If the deployment occurs before the examination is complete, or before the developers can articulate what they do not know, the arrangement has not satisfied the pause requirement, regardless of whether the examination later occurs.

The framework does not decide the case. It tells the hospital what it owes an answer to, and to whom.

17.2b Case: the emergency physician (a counterintuitive result)

Every other case in this essay produces the answer a careful reader would predict. This one does not.

A physician in an emergency department must decide, in under ninety seconds, whether to intubate a patient. The activation rule gives Level 3.

The counterintuitive result. Level 3 requires independent authorisation, preserved records and a written assessment of alternatives — before the act. A reader's first reaction is that this is absurd: the physician cannot convene an ethics board in ninety seconds.

The reply. The Level 3 duty does not fall on the physician in the moment. It falls on the arrangement (§11.2): on the hospital, the protocols, the audit trail, the retrospective review, and the training. The physician's own duty is Level 1 in the moment and Level 2 afterwards. The ninety-second decision is answerable, not authorised in advance. It is authorised by the structure that made it possible, and that structure is the duty-bearer of Level 3.

Why this case is a strength, not an escape. A framework that relocates every hard demand to "the arrangement" risks becoming untestable. The reply is not a relocation if the arrangement can fail in ways the framework can identify. Here is how it can fail: if the hospital does not preserve the records of the decision, or if the retrospective review is not independent (R1), or if the physician is not trained to articulate what she did not know when she decided (D4), then the arrangement has not discharged its Level 3 duty — and the framework says so. The physician's decision is answerable only if the arrangement makes it so. That is a testable claim.

The pause in this case. The pause is not in the ninety-second decision. It is in the structure that precedes it: the protocols that were paused over, the audit that will pause over the decision later, and the review that will pause before the decision is treated as final. The physician's ninety seconds are authorised by pauses that occurred before her and will occur after her. And D4 applies to her: after the decision, she must be able to articulate what she did not know — the patient's unknown comorbidities, the limits of the score, the uncertainty of the prognosis. If she cannot, the ninety seconds were not a decision; they were a reflex.

17.3 Case: a platform's recommendation system

For individual recommendations: Level 2. For the design of the system: Level 3, with preserved version history, independent audit, published aggregate metrics, and a sunset for any optimisation that cannot show population-level reversibility. "Engagement" is defined by the platform, audits are typically controlled by it, and non-users affected through the public sphere are excluded.

17.4 Case: a community health worker

She has little institutional power. The rule says the arrangement requires Level 3. But she has no capacity to build independent review. Under §11.2, the duty to establish mechanisms falls on the programme, the ministry and the funders. Her personal duties are to explain her decisions, respond to objections within her role, and escalate when protocols fail. The level belongs to the arrangement; the duties are allocated by capacity.

17.5 Four short cases

  • One-off organism release. Level 3, although the act happens once.
  • Dating platform age penalty. Level 2.
  • Café menu change. Level 1 only, and arguably not even that.
  • Physician following a protocol. No discretion over the protocol's content; the duty to justify the content moves to its designers.

17.6 Case: emergency water rationing (duties conflict)

A water authority must impose rationing within 48 hours in a severe drought. Entrenchment and severity are High and contestation deficit is High, so the rule gives Level 3, whose prior independent authorisation takes weeks. §15.2 allows acting first. The duties then split.

  • Not compressible: published reasons, preserved records, a contestation channel open from day one, exemptions for life-critical dependence, and a sunset clause.
  • Compressible: prior authorisation, replaced by review completed before the sunset.
  • Not decided by the framework: which rationing rule is fair, and whether 48 hours really precludes prior review.

Failure mode: if "emergency" is routinely invoked to skip prior review, test T5 (§18.2) is triggered. Here the framework imposes a cost on the actor's preferred option and admits residual dispute.

18.Limits, testing and replacement

The framework states its own defeaters. This section collects them, and states the conditions under which the framework should be replaced.

18.1 Limitations, collected

The framework's limits are stated here, once, rather than scattered throughout the essay as hedges.

Operational limits.

  • Ratings need judgment and the anchors will be contested.
  • Representation risks projection and paternalism (mitigated in §6.6, not removed).
  • Review can be captured (§14).
  • Small actors may be over-burdened in practice.
  • All claims about its effects are hypotheses. No test has yet been run.

Limits of the ground.

  • J underdetermines outcomes.
  • The ground is an abductive argument, not a proof (§2.3).
  • The framework was developed mainly within one intellectual tradition. The stone (§5b) is one attempt to bring another voice into the argument, but wider critique is needed.

Limits of the pause as condition.

  • The pause cannot be guaranteed. P4 is the framework's central limit: the pause can be captured, and D4 makes capture visible but not impossible.
  • The pause can be relocated to the arrangement in ways that make it invisible (see §17.2b). The framework addresses this by making the arrangement's failure testable, but the relocation is real.
  • The pause is not a substitute for substantive justice. An arrangement can pause, review and justify while still producing unjust outcomes.
  • D4 tests honesty, not ignorance. An actor can lie about what it did not know. The lie is visible against the record, but it is still a lie.

Limits of the epistemic burden principle.

  • The principle is limited to conditions of asymmetric influence. Where parties have comparable power or effective routes of correction, the heightened burden may be unnecessary or modest.
  • The principle is epistemic and procedural, not metaphysical.
  • The principle is defensible, not proven. Its defeaters are stated in §8.4. B1 (C1 is false) is a central empirical vulnerability.
  • C2 is a bridge principle, not a deduction. A critic who accepts C1 and rejects C2 is not making a logical error. The framework argues that this position is unstable, but it cannot force the critic to accept it.

The most serious limit. The framework handles domination; it does not solve it. No framework can. What the framework can do is make capture visible and costly. Visibility is not prevention, but it is the precondition of contestation. Whether this is enough is a political question, not a philosophical one.

18.2 Five falsification conditions: full specification

The tests use independent measures where possible, are specified in advance, state provisional thresholds, and require preregistration. The thresholds are placeholders. What matters is the structure: independent measures, matched baselines, pre-specified thresholds, preregistration.

Preregistration requirements. Before any of the tests below are run, the following must be fixed and made public: (i) the calibration set of cases and the raters; (ii) the outcome measures and their operational definitions; (iii) the thresholds for failure; (iv) the analytic plan, including how confounding and selection will be addressed. A test that is not preregistered should not be treated as a confirmatory test of the stated thresholds; it may still generate exploratory evidence.

  • T1 Reliability. Trained raters independently rate a calibration set of at least 30 diverse cases. If agreement on level (Krippendorff's alpha) is below 0.60, the anchors are revised. If it remains below 0.40 after two revisions, the profile is abandoned as a decision tool.
  • T2 Discriminative validity. The levels fail to track the safeguards that independent expert panels, blind to the framework's output, judge necessary (weighted kappa below 0.50 over at least 30 cases).
  • T3 Effectiveness. In matched or staggered-adoption comparisons over at least two years, adopting institutions fail to improve, relative to controls, on at least two of three independent indicators: time to correct erroneous decisions; share of challenges that lead to change; and affected parties' reported fairness and trust in an independent survey. The second indicator is partly internal to the framework and counts as supporting evidence only; the first and third carry the test.
  • T4 Harm. Adopters show worse outcomes for the most vulnerable groups than matched controls on independently measured harm indicators. The framework is then suspended and revised.
  • T5 Capture and legitimation. Within three years, a majority of adopters' review bodies fail R1 on documented appointment, funding and removal, or adopters' rate of successful external challenges falls without improvement on the T3 indicators. As a precondition (§14.4), T5 also requires that Level 3 authorisation be preceded by publication of R1 indicators; an authorisation without such publication is not a Level 3 authorisation.

These tests face confounding and selection problems like any institutional comparison. They are better than none, and they make the framework's claims capable of being wrong. The preregistration requirement is meant to prevent the tests from being reshaped after the fact to fit the results.

18.3 Replacement

The framework is modular. Its ground (Part I), standing (Part III), activation (Part IV) and structure (Part V) can be replaced separately. The epistemic burden principle and the pause as condition are separate modules. A framework or module F′ should replace the current one if:

  1. F′ improves on at least one independent indicator in §18.2 or resolves a limitation in §18.1.
  2. F′ causes no unacknowledged regression elsewhere.
  3. F′ is operationally applicable and produces more defensible decisions on a substantial set of cases.
  4. F′ is corrigible: it states its own falsification and replacement conditions.
  5. The transition is trialled, with a sunset or a route back.
  6. The incumbent is not privileged.

If these are met, F′ should be adopted — because §16 requires it.

19.The loop and the open commitment

The limit is not a concession. It is the ground.

In the game, there was a line: "Det finns alltid något mer intelligent än dig själv som varelse. Det tar aldrig slut. Det är en loop."

This is the fallibility premise in its most vivid form. No actor fully understands the conditions it shapes. There is always something beyond its calculation — a perspective it cannot reach, a consequence it cannot foresee, a stone it cannot make speak. The loop never ends.

The framework treats this as a ground, not as a concession. If the loop could be closed — if an actor could fully understand its influence — then answerability would be redundant. The actor could simply act correctly. The framework's necessity is derived from the loop.

This changes the tone of the essay. The limit is not a second-best solution to a problem that could in principle be solved. It is the only kind of solution available to beings who cannot close the loop.

The pause is the loop made institutional. The pause is the condition in which the actor recognizes that the loop has not closed — that there is still something beyond its calculation. The pause is not a delay; it is a structural acknowledgment of the loop. An arrangement that contains no pause is an arrangement that pretends the loop has closed. And an arrangement that pretends the loop has closed is an arrangement that has stopped being answerable.

The essay ends where the game ended: with the stone.

To understand without ruling. To protect without imprisoning. To love without possessing. To grow without believing oneself complete. To respect what one does not yet understand. And to preserve the possibility that existence may become something greater than any single being can imagine.

The stone does not answer. But the pause it produces is real. And the pause is the condition under which the actor can be corrected by something it did not produce.

Answerability is the procedure. Symbiosis is the horizon. The pause is the stone in the machine.

Common objections

Twelve objections the framework has to survive. The first is the hardest, and the eleventh is the one the framework most needs to answer.

1."Why must the powerful justify themselves to the affected? What if they simply refuse?"

This is the hardest objection, and the framework does not pretend to refute it. The realist position — stated fairly — is that power does not owe justification; justification is a tool of the weak; the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must (Thucydides). This is not a position that can be argued out of existence, because it denies the authority of argument in the first place. The framework's reply has four parts, ordered from weakest to strongest.

First, the performative reply. The realist who argues against J is already engaged in the practice J describes: giving reasons, addressing an interlocutor, seeking assent. This is a performative presupposition, not a refutation. It is often dismissed as a trick, and the dismissal is partly fair: the realist can reply that he argues only because it is useful, not because he is bound by the practice. The performative reply is therefore suggestive, not decisive.

Second, the strategic reply. Even if the realist is unmoved by moral reasons, the framework makes capture visible and costly. A realist who refuses answerability does not thereby escape scrutiny; he makes the refusal visible, and visibility is the precondition of contestation. This is a pragmatic reply, not a moral one. It does not refute the realist; it raises the cost of his position.

Third, and most importantly, the epistemic reply. The realist cannot know whether the relation is symbiotic without the affected party's contestation. This is the force of the epistemic burden principle (§8). The realist who claims that the weak are content, that the strong are benevolent, that the relation is fine — is making an epistemic claim. And that claim cannot be supported by evidence the realist controls alone. The realist who refuses answerability does not gain knowledge of the relation. He loses it. This is not a moral argument; it is an epistemic one, and it applies even to the realist who is indifferent to morality.

Fourth, the honest concession. The framework cannot refute the realist. It can only outlast him, and it can make his position costlier to hold. This is a limit, not a failure. A framework that claimed to refute the realist would be either naive or dishonest. The framework claims instead that the realist's position is unstable: it requires the realist to act on evidence he knows to be incomplete, while denying that the incompleteness matters. This is not a contradiction, but it is a position that requires defence. The framework invites the realist to provide it.

2."This is a values framework dressed up as a procedure."

Partly, and the essay says so. J carries a minimal commitment to the equal standing of those affected. The claim is breadth of acceptability, not neutrality. A fair test is whether people with different comprehensive views can use it to reach different answers in the same case. They can: it does not decide the ICU case.

3."Proceduralism is itself a form of power."

This is the second-hardest objection, and the framework does not deny it. Its reply has three parts: (i) the objection is partly correct, and the framework's T5-as-precondition (§14.4) is its internal response; (ii) the objection is not specific to proceduralism — any normative framework can be captured; (iii) the framework's modesty is not an admission of failure but a claim about what any framework can do. A sufficiently powerful actor can capture the publication requirement itself. The framework can make the capture visible, but visibility is not prevention. Whether this is enough is a political question, not a philosophical one.

4."Why should contestation override better outcomes?"

It does not always. Where exit and review are available and effective, justification need not be owed (§2.1). The revised J is disjunctive, not monistic. It privileges justification only where exit and review fail. The remaining disagreement is genuine, and the framework sides with contestation in severe cases for epistemic reasons, while permitting temporary override in emergencies subject to sunset and review (§15.2).

5."The capacity principle legitimises power."

Answerability is not authority (§3.2, §11.3). Higher levels mean stronger constraints. T5 (§14.4) looks for exactly this misuse. The risk remains and is not argued away; §14.4 converts it from a concession into a design constraint.

6."Who reviews the reviewers?"

The regress is bounded, not eliminated (§14): finality without infallibility, review conditions R1–R6, plural and public reasoning, and counter-profiles. It also needs a political precondition, a balance of power, that no framework can supply by itself.

7."Precaution under uncertainty is either paralysing or too weak to matter."

The credibility threshold in §6.2 answers the first: reasons arise only where there is evidence or a plausible mechanism. Against the second: in Tier C the principle does real work, requiring investigation and proportionate safeguards. The stone does not meet the threshold and is not claimed to.

8."The ratings are arbitrary or manipulable."

Judgment cannot be eliminated, but §12 exposes it: anchors with observable indicators, two raters, a sensitivity test, reasoned overrides and counter-profiles. Tests T1 and T2 examine consistency, and §18.2 requires preregistration.

9."This is Western liberal philosophy in disguise."

Its development lies mostly in that tradition, and §18.1 says so. It does not claim universality for J. It claims that the epistemic consideration is primary and that J is abductively defensible. The stone (§5b) is one attempt to bring another voice into the argument — a voice that cannot speak, but whose silence is real.

10."It will burden the already overburdened."

The level attaches to the arrangement and duties are allocated by capacity (§11.2). The health worker case (§17.4) and the emergency physician case (§17.2b) show the mechanism. Whether burdens migrate to front-line staff is part of what tests T3 and T4 would reveal.

11."The epistemic burden principle is circular."

This is the most serious objection to the framework's epistemic proposal, and the reply is now in §8. The principle is two claims, not one. C1 is descriptive and empirical: asymmetry systematically biases the actor's evidence, supported by research on clinical trials, credit scoring and platform moderation. C2 is normative: given C1, the actor bears a burden to seek correction. C1 and C2 are different kinds of claim, and neither is circular. S1–S3 are specified independently of answerability. The principle adds a substantive claim: these conditions can only be reliably assessed through procedures of answerability. This is not tautological. It may be wrong — the defeaters are stated in §8.4 — but the revised formulation does not define symbiosis in terms of answerability.

12."The pause is just review before the decision."

This objection is treated in §5.1, and the four-cell matrix is the reply. If the pause were a specification of review, the two would vary together: review present means pause present, review absent means pause absent. They do not. "Pause without review" (upper-right) is a real and common case: an actor waits, doubts, and decides alone — the value is the availability to correction, not the correction itself. "Review without pause" (lower-left) is the most common failure mode in practice: the actor decides immediately and a review board ratifies afterwards. If the pause were a specification of review, these cells would be empty. They are not. The pause is a condition on the mechanisms of answerability, not a fourth mechanism alongside them.

Om pausen vore en specifikation av review skulle dessa celler vara tomma. De är inte det.
  5. Fyra tester (§5.2). D1 (answer-window), D2 (registration), D3 (contestability) kalibrerar pausens längd. D4 (articulation) testar om pausen ägde rum: aktören måste efter beslutet kunna artikulera vad den inte visste. En aktör som inte kan namnge en enda osäkerhet har inte pausat — den har bara väntat. Bevisbördan ligger på aktören.
  6. P4 — pausen kan fångas (§5.3). Detta är ramverkets centrala begränsning. D4 gör fångst synlig men inte omöjlig. Ramverket låtsas inte annat.
  7. Stenen (§5b). Stenen gör tre argument: (a) den markerar gränsen för answerability som teori om vad som betyder något, (b) den är fallibilismens limit-fall, (c) den kopplar till Frickers epistemiska orättvisa.
  8. Epistemisk börda (§8). Principen är två claims: C1 (deskriptiv, empirisk: asymmetri snedvrider systematiskt aktörens evidens) och C2 (normativ: givet C1 bär aktören en börda att söka korrigering). Uppdelningen upplöser cirkularitetsinvändningen.
  9. Ingen ensam domare (§14). T5 är ett förhandsvillkor för Level 3-auktorisation.

Vad texten inte löser

  • Den avgör inga utfall.
  • Den kan inte garantera att granskare inte kapas.
  • Den är utvecklad huvudsakligen inom en västerländsk tradition.
  • Den kan inte bevisa att J är nödvändig.
  • Den hanterar dominans. Den löser den inte.
  • C2 är en bryggprincip, inte en deduktion.
  • Pausen kan fångas (P4). D4 gör fångsten synlig, inte omöjlig. Detta är ramverkets centrala begränsning.
  • D4 testar ärlighet, inte okunskap. En aktör kan ljuga om vad den inte visste. Lögnen är synlig mot protokollet, men det är fortfarande en lögn.

I en mening: när din påverkan formar andras villkor, säg varför, låt någon annan kontrollera, behåll en väg tillbaka — och se till att det finns en paus där du inte är ensam domare. Ansvarsskyldighet är proceduren. Symbios är horisonten. Pausen är stenen i maskinen.

Glossary

Answerability
The duty to give reasons for conditions one helps create or maintain, to permit examination, and to remain open to correction. Under the epistemic burden principle, the actor bears a heightened burden to test its account against independent evidence and meaningful correction under conditions of asymmetric influence (§8).
Abductive justification
The form of argument used for J: J is a plausible explanatory framework of several considerations about the risks of unchecked power. Distinct from a deductive proof or a convergence argument.
Articulation test (D4)
The test that asks whether the pause occurred: after the decision, the actor must be able to articulate what it did not know. An actor that cannot name a single uncertainty has not paused; it has only waited (§5.2).
C1 and C2
The two claims of the epistemic burden principle. C1 is descriptive and empirical: asymmetry systematically biases the actor's evidence. C2 is normative: given C1, the actor bears a burden to seek correction. The separation dissolves the circularity objection (§8).
Collective contestation
Contestation by a community or people in their own right. Legitimate only if it does not undermine individual contestation within the collective (§6.6 vii).
Condition (the pause as)
The pause is not a mechanism alongside exit, review and justification; it is the condition under which those three are something other than ritual. The pause and review vary independently: the four-cell matrix in §5.1 shows that "pause without review" and "review without pause" are real and common cases.
Contestation deficit (C)
How hard it is for affected parties to exit, object effectively or find out what is done to them. The operational measure of how thoroughly exit and review fail, and therefore of how strongly justification is required (§10).
Counter-profile
An alternative influence profile filed by an affected party, to which the actor must respond (§12.4).
Defeaters
The conditions under which J, the epistemic burden principle, or the pause as condition should be rejected or substantially revised. Stated in advance for J (§2.2), the epistemic burden principle (§8.4), and the pause as condition (§5.3).
Discretion (D)
Genuine alternatives and a role in setting the influence. The gate of the activation rule.
Duration tests (D1–D3)
Three tests for the length of a pause: the answer-window test, the registration test, and the contestability test. The burden of justification is on the actor (§5.2).
Entrenchment (E)
How long consequences last and how hard they are to undo.
Exercised-Capacity Principle (ECP)
Exercised influence triggers the duty when exit and review are unavailable or ineffective; stakes and contestation deficit scale it; capacity allocates it (§3). Demonstrated against Young's social connection model with a comparison table in §3.3.
Failure conditions (T1–T5)
Five conditions under which the activation rule should be revised or abandoned (§18.2). Requires preregistration.
Finality without infallibility
Decisions final for practical purposes but reopenable on defined grounds (§14.2).
The Floor (F1–F5)
Minimum conditions in relationships of dependence: exit or voice, contestation, development, non-foreclosure, recognition (§13).
Four-cell matrix
The matrix in §5.1 that shows the pause and review vary independently. The off-diagonal cells — "pause without review" and "review without pause" — are the cases that show the pause is a condition on review, not a specification of it.
J
The core principle: systematic constraint of others' conditions requires at least one of meaningful exit, independent review, or justification open to contestation (§2.1). Primarily epistemic; abductively defensible, not necessary; defeaters stated in §2.2.
Levels 1–3
Explain; review and respond; preserve, examine and authorise (§11).
The pause
A condition on the mechanisms of answerability — exit, review and justification — such that they count as answerability only when the actor is, at the moment of decision, still available to correction. The pause is not a mechanism; it is the condition under which the mechanisms are something other than ritual (§5).
Pause requirement
The claim that no arrangement satisfies J unless the actor is in the condition the pause describes, and that the pause must satisfy the four tests D1–D4 (§5). Defeaters P1–P4 in §5.3.
Presumptive level
The level the activation rule yields, overridable only with stated reasons (§10.3, §12.3).
Preregistration
The requirement that the calibration set, raters, outcome measures, thresholds and analytic plan be fixed and made public before the tests in §18.2 are run.
Relational existence
The empirical and phenomenological claim that no being exists except through relations of dependence, influence and constitution (§7).
Scope conditions
The conditions under which the framework does not apply: symmetrical relations, relations governed by enforceable rights, purely causal influence without discretion, trivial influence, relations where the affected party is not a candidate for standing (§1.2).
Severity (S)
How much is at stake for the affected, rated at individual and, for system design, aggregate level.
The stone
The limit of the framework's claims. The stone does not meet the threshold for standing, but it marks the edge of what answerability can say. It does three kinds of work: it marks the limit of answerability as a theory of what matters; it is the limiting case of the fallibility premise; and it connects to Fricker's epistemic injustice (§5b).
Symbiosis
A normative ideal: relations of dependence in which the dependence is not exploited, difference is not forced into sameness, and the dependent party keeps the conditions for continued existence, development and participation (§7).
Symbiosis indicators (S1–S3)
Non-exploitation, non-assimilation, continued possibility (§7.1). Conceptually distinct from answerability.
Epistemic burden principle
The claim that asymmetric influence creates a heightened burden to test the powerful actor's account against independent evidence and meaningful correction (§8). Two claims: C1 (descriptive, empirical) and C2 (normative, bridge principle). Defended against charges of circularity, overreach and metaphysical overreach, and against five stated defeaters (B1–B5).
Tiers A–D
Agents; welfare subjects; uncertain candidates; non-subjects (§6.1).

About this text

By Jesper Blom.

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability. Its contribution is comparative and partly philosophical.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see Bibliography). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Five features of the framework deserve comment.

First, J is disjunctive and primarily epistemic (§2). Second, J is abductively defensible, not necessary, and has stated defeaters (§2.2). Third, the framework's most distinctive philosophical proposal is the pause as condition (§5): not a fourth mechanism alongside exit, review and justification, but the condition under which those three are something other than ritual. The four-cell matrix (§5.1) shows that the pause and review vary independently, and the four tests (D1–D4) specify whether and how long the pause has occurred, with the burden of proof on the actor. Fourth, the epistemic burden principle (§8) is stated as two claims — C1 (descriptive, empirical) and C2 (normative, bridge principle) — which dissolves the circularity objection. Fifth, the framework handles domination; it does not solve it (§3.2, §19), and the power objection (§14.4) is the point at which this limit presses hardest.

The pause as condition (§5) is the framework's main structural proposal. It argues that exit, review, and justification are all insufficient if the actor has not been in a state in which its own assessment was provisional. The pause is the stone in the machine: the condition in which the actor recognizes that its own answer is not the whole. Its defeaters are stated in §5.3, and P4 is the framework's central limit: the pause can be captured, and D4 makes capture visible but not impossible.

The stone (§5b) is not a rhetorical decoration. It does three kinds of work: it marks the limit of answerability as a theory of what matters; it is the limiting case of the fallibility premise; and it connects to Fricker's account of epistemic injustice. A reader who finds the stone unconvincing is invited to say so, and the framework will be revised accordingly.

The framework's methodological constraint (§2.2, §8.4, §5.3, §18.2) remains: it states, in advance, four defeaters for J, five defeaters for the epistemic burden principle, four defeaters for the pause as condition, and five proposed evaluation conditions for its activation rule, and it requires preregistration for the tests.

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with. A framework that cannot be replaced cannot be corrected — see §18.3.
