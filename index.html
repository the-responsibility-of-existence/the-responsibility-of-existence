Abstract

Existence is never entirely isolated. Living beings, intelligences, institutions, technologies and ecosystems exist through relations of dependence, influence, exchange and consequence. Some relations are reciprocal and enabling; others are unequal, restrictive or harmful. The fact of interdependence does not guarantee harmony. It creates the conditions under which responsibility becomes possible and, in certain circumstances, necessary.

This essay develops a normative framework for what remains owed where blame, consent and authority fail. It defines reach as a profile of six dimensions; specifies a three-condition gate — discretion, persistence, non-revisability — with operational tests for when reach generates a distinctive duty of answerability; and sets out a graded duty ladder running from explanation, through review and response, to external authorisation before irreversible steps. It adds a floor of symbiotic standing for relationships of significant dependence: exit or voice, contestation, development, non-foreclosure and recognition.

The framework extends rather than refutes the non-domination, right-to-justification, vulnerability and social-connection traditions. Its distinctive contribution is to trigger duties on structural features — discretion, persistence, non-revisability — rather than on relational features such as domination or normative power, and to specify how those duties are owed to parties who cannot demand them and discharged through structured representation.

Its central claim is that substantial, discretionary and non-revisable influence creates a prima facie duty of answerability independent of blame, consent or authority, and that no actor should be the sole judge of whether that duty has been met. Its normative core is a single thin premise — (N*) in §4.2 — that arrangements systematically constraining a person's exercise of normative agency stand in need of justification to that person. The framework is meta-ethically modest: it does not adjudicate between Kantian, republican and contractualist groundings of (N*), and its conclusions follow on any of them. Three objections to (N*) are considered in §4.4. The framework is tested against Pettit, Forst, Young and culpability-based accounts in five cases, and §15 lists the findings that would count against it.

Part I

Foundations

1.The Fact of Relationship

To exist is to be situated within conditions that make existence possible.

No living organism is wholly independent of its environment. Human beings depend on biological systems, other people, inherited knowledge and ecological conditions. Institutions depend on resources, cooperation and the actions of previous generations. Artificial systems depend on infrastructure, energy, data and the decisions that created them. The forms of dependence differ, but the pattern is recognizable: existence is shaped through relationships.

This is a descriptive claim, and it should be kept distinct from a normative one. That entities depend on one another does not by itself show how they should treat one another. Dependence can enable life; it can also create vulnerability. An entity may depend on another that has greater knowledge, control, resources or power to determine its available choices. Some asymmetries are necessary, temporary or beneficial. Others become dangerous when the more powerful party can shape the conditions of another's existence without adequate restraint.

The ethical question is therefore not whether dependence exists, but under what conditions it can remain compatible with dignity, development, freedom and the continued possibility of existence. This essay's answer begins with a single normative proposal — that consequential influence creates a duty of answerability — and develops it into a structure. The existential observations in this section explain why the proposal matters. They do not establish it.

1.1 Two things the framework does not claim

The framework does not claim that interdependence is harmonious. Competition, predation, disagreement and transformation all occur within networks of dependence. It does not claim that the interests of a whole — an ecosystem, a society, an institution — automatically override the interests of its parts. Appeals to the whole can be used to justify sacrificing individuals, minorities or vulnerable populations, and the framework rejects that move as firmly as it rejects the assumption that individual interests always override the conditions sustaining wider life.

The relationship between whole and part is a substantive moral question, not something settled by the fact of interdependence. What the framework offers is a way of approaching that question under conditions of unequal power and incomplete knowledge: an account of when influence creates duties, what those duties are, and how they can be discharged without becoming new forms of domination.

2.The Reflective Surface

Every action enters a world that responds.

An intervention changes conditions. Those changes produce consequences, and the consequences become part of the environment in which further decisions are made. The resulting information may confirm an expectation, reveal an error, expose an unintended effect, or show that the actor's original understanding was incomplete. This relation can be described as a reflective surface:

Understanding → Interpretation → Action → Consequence → New information → Revised understanding.

The sequence is not necessarily linear. Consequences may be delayed, hidden, distributed across many entities, or difficult to attribute to a single cause. An actor may receive incomplete or distorted feedback. Some consequences may be irreversible before they are understood. The point is not that reality teaches the correct lesson automatically. It is that action does not end when an intention has been expressed: it continues through the conditions and consequences it creates.

This matters for the argument that follows because it explains why answerability is not merely a formal requirement. If consequences could not return as information, a duty to give reasons would be decorative — a ritual of explanation with no connection to the effects an actor produces. The reflective surface is what makes the duty substantive: reasons given, examined and corrected are the means by which an actor can learn what its influence actually does.

2.1 Closed feedback

A feedback process becomes dangerous when it reinforces its own assumptions while excluding information that might challenge them. Fear can produce restrictions that increase distrust, which is then read as evidence that further restrictions are necessary. A system can become increasingly confident while becoming increasingly detached from the conditions it affects. The same pattern can arise in governments, firms, platforms, scientific institutions and artificial systems.

A responsible feedback process must therefore preserve the possibility of disagreement. It must distinguish evidence from confirmation, criticism from hostility, and stability from the mere absence of visible resistance. A system that cannot be corrected by information from those it affects is not genuinely reflective — whatever its internal procedures may look like.

2.2 Reflection is not wisdom

Receiving feedback does not guarantee that an actor will understand it correctly. An institution may interpret criticism as a threat. An intelligent system may optimize for a measure that fails to represent the welfare of those affected. A powerful actor may receive information only from people who benefit from its decisions.

A responsible actor must therefore ask not only whether an action achieved its intended result, but also which consequences were overlooked, whose experience was excluded from the evaluation, which assumptions were treated as certain without sufficient evidence, what new information would require the decision to change, and whether the consequences can still be corrected.

2.3 From description to norm

The claims in §1–2 are descriptive. They say that existence is relational and that action generates information through its consequences. Neither claim, on its own, establishes a normative duty.

The framework's normative argument begins in §3–4. §3 introduces reach as a way of describing the structure of influence. §4 introduces answerability as a normative response to a particular kind of influence — one that is substantial, discretionary and difficult for affected parties to revise. The bridge between the descriptive and the normative is (N*) and its derivation of P3 (§4.2), which is defended in its own right and not derived from the observations in this section.

Readers who accept §1–2 but reject P3 will find the framework's normative core unpersuasive. That is as it should be: the existential framing is motivation, not proof.

Part II

Reach and Answerability

3.Reach: The Structure of Influence

Responsibility should not be determined solely by intention, formal authority or direct causation.

An actor may lack malicious intent yet possess the capacity to shape another entity's available choices. A company may determine the conditions under which millions of people communicate. An infrastructure provider may influence the basic possibilities of a community. An artificial system may increasingly mediate decisions without being a moral person in the ordinary sense.

To understand these situations, the framework uses the concept of Reach.

Reach is the extent to which an actor can shape the conditions, options, opportunities, risks or future possibilities of others. It is not a single numerical quantity. It is a profile consisting of six dimensions.

3.1 Six dimensions of Reach

  1. Asymmetry. How unequal is the relationship between the actor and those affected? Consider differences in knowledge, resources, dependency, bargaining power, and the ability to resist or leave.
  2. Control. How much practical control does the actor possess over the relevant conditions? Formal ownership or authority is not required. Control may be indirect, distributed, or exercised through infrastructure and design.
  3. Persistence. How long does the influence continue? Persistence includes repeated decisions, ongoing control, institutional arrangements, and consequences that endure beyond the original action.
  4. Magnitude. How significant are the possible consequences? Magnitude includes effects on safety, livelihood, autonomy, development, ecological conditions, and the continued possibility of existence.
  5. Scope. How many entities, relationships, communities or systems may be affected? Scope also includes indirect effects and consequences transmitted through connected systems.
  6. Foreseeability. How reasonably foreseeable are the relevant consequences? This concerns what the actor could reasonably be expected to understand, not what could only be known with perfect hindsight.

These dimensions should be considered separately. A situation may involve limited scope but extreme magnitude, or moderate control combined with substantial persistence. A simple total score could conceal these differences. Reach is therefore best understood as a structured assessment rather than a calculation that automatically produces a moral verdict.

Reach must also distinguish actual control from reasonable capacity to anticipate consequences. An actor should not be treated as controlling outcomes it could not reasonably foresee. Limited foreseeability does not, however, remove duties to investigate, monitor and respond once consequences become apparent: it bears on how demanding the duty is, not on whether the actor is answerable for conditions it shapes.

The dimensions are analytically distinct but empirically correlated. They are not commensurable and should not be summed. Their function is to structure a judgment that critics can dispute dimension by dimension — which is what makes the assessment answerable.

3.2 Reach and responsibility

Reach does not establish that an actor is guilty. Nor does it automatically grant that actor authority. It identifies the extent to which the actor may have duties arising from its influence.

As an actor's discretionary and consequential influence over the conditions of others' existence increases, its responsibility to explain, justify and remain open to correction also increases.

This responsibility may exist even where no single person intended the outcome. It may attach to an institution, a role, a system of decision-making, or a group of actors whose combined actions create substantial influence.

4.Answerability: Responsibility Without Sovereignty

Answerability is the duty to provide reasons for consequential conditions one helps create or maintain, to permit appropriate examination of those reasons, and to remain open to correction.

It is distinct from three related ideas.

Blame concerns whether an actor deserves criticism for a wrongful action. Answerability can exist without blame. An actor may create serious consequences through reasonable decisions made under uncertainty and still have a duty to explain and review them.

Authority concerns the legitimate right to make decisions. Answerability does not automatically grant authority. An actor may have substantial influence without having a legitimate right to determine the conditions of others' lives.

Control concerns the practical ability to shape outcomes. Answerability may arise from control, but it can also arise from a role within a larger system that collectively exercises control.

This distinction matters because modern influence often exceeds traditional boundaries of responsibility. A decision may be distributed across designers, managers, institutions, infrastructures and automated systems. The absence of one clearly identifiable decision-maker does not make the consequences morally ownerless.

4.1 The basic argument

The framework's argument can be expressed in six propositions.

P1 — Conditioning. Some actors possess the practical capacity to shape the conditions under which others act, develop or continue to exist.

P2 — Consequential asymmetry. When that capacity is substantial, discretionary, and difficult for affected parties to challenge or escape, it creates a relationship of consequential asymmetry.

P3 — Justificatory burden. An actor that substantially shapes another's conditions while limiting that party's ability to contest those conditions must be able to offer reasons that can be examined from outside the actor's own perspective.

P4 — Silence is not consent. The inability, refusal or failure of affected parties to object does not by itself establish that the conditions are justified. Silence may reflect dependency, exclusion, incapacity, fear or lack of meaningful alternatives.

P5 — Answerability requires openness. Giving reasons is insufficient if the actor cannot be challenged, if relevant evidence is excluded, or if correction is impossible in practice.

P6 — No self-certification. An actor should not be the sole judge of whether its own consequential influence is justified, particularly where affected parties lack comparable power.

Together, these propositions establish a prima facie duty of answerability. The duty can be limited by context, proportionality, legitimate confidentiality and competing rights, but it cannot be dismissed merely because the actor lacks malicious intent or formal authority.

4.2 The normative foundation

Why should consequential influence create a duty to answer? The framework states its normative premise explicitly rather than leaving it implicit in P3:

(N*) Any arrangement that systematically constrains a person's exercise of normative agency stands in need of justification to that person.

Three features of (N*) should be noted. It is thin: it does not presuppose a comprehensive conception of the good, and it does not specify what a sufficient justification looks like. It is relational: the demand is addressed to the person constrained, not to an impartial spectator — which distinguishes it from aggregate-welfare justifications that could in principle be satisfied without anyone actually being given reasons. And it is defeasible: it generates a prima facie demand that can be met, not an absolute prohibition.

Because those affected by another's decisions are not merely objects within that actor's plans, but beings or communities whose interests, experiences and future possibilities matter independently of the actor's purposes, the relationship risks becoming arbitrary when one party can shape another's conditions while the latter lacks an effective means of contestation. The stronger party can determine what counts as reasonable, which consequences matter, and whether its own actions require correction. (N*) limits that danger by requiring the powerful party to make its reasons available for examination and to recognise that its own perspective is not sufficient to establish legitimacy.

This does not mean that every decision requires unanimous consent or that every affected party must possess a veto. It means that significant influence must be accompanied by proportionate duties of justification, scrutiny and correction. The bridge from (N*) to P3 is explicit: where reach satisfies N1–N3 (§5), the affected parties cannot in practice discharge the demand for justification themselves — they lack the information, the standing, and the practical capacity. The demand therefore falls on the agent or institution whose reach it is. Self-certification is structurally insufficient (P6) not because agents are vicious, but because the asymmetry that triggers the demand also disqualifies the constrained party from being the sole author of its discharge.

4.3 Three groundings of (N*)

(N*) can be grounded in at least three distinct traditions, and the framework is deliberately meta-ethically modest: it does not adjudicate between them, and its conclusions follow on any of them. This overdetermination is a feature rather than a defect — the framework's practical implications do not stand or fall with a single contested meta-ethical commitment.

The Kantian grounding holds that constraining a rational agent's exercise of agency without justification treats that agent as a mere means. The wrong is not the constraint as such but the absence of a justification the agent could, in principle, recognise. (N*) is the demand that this recognition not be foreclosed.¹

The republican grounding holds that unjustified constraint constitutes a form of domination, or violates a basic right to justification. Pettit's account of non-domination is the most developed: a person is dominated when another has the capacity to interfere on an arbitrary basis, where "arbitrary" means not forced to track the interests of those affected.² Forst extends this into a basic right to justification, arguing that every person subject to a norm has a claim to be given reasons for it.³ (N*) is the demand that asymmetric influence not become arbitrary influence in this sense.

The contractualist grounding holds that an arrangement must be justifiable to each person affected. Scanlon's formulation — that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle no one could reasonably reject — supplies the structure: (N*) is the demand that constraints on agency be supported by principles that could not be reasonably rejected by those constrained.⁴

The three groundings converge on (N*) from different directions, and each supplies different resources for criticising particular applications. A Kantian reader will press hardest on cases where agents are treated as mere instruments; a republican reader on cases where influence is unaccountable to those affected; a contractualist reader on cases where the justificatory principle could be reasonably rejected. The framework's claim is not that these groundings are equivalent, but that the duty of answerability survives on any of them — which is what makes it robust enough to be operationalised in §5–6 without settling a meta-ethical dispute.

4.4 Three objections to (N*)

(N*) is a claim, not a demonstration, and its meta-ethical modesty is itself a claim that can be challenged. Three objections deserve statement in their own right. One of them — the opponent objection — may be decisive, and the framework should not pretend otherwise.

Objection 1 (Ranking). (N*) generates a prima facie demand for justification. But so do other basic principles — freedom of expression, security of the person, property. If (N*) cannot be ranked against these principles, it cannot guide action when they conflict. If it can be ranked, the ranking principle — not (N*) — is doing the normative work.

Reply. The objection identifies a real limit, but it misdescribes what (N*) is. (N*) does not claim to be a master principle that resolves conflicts between substantive values. It claims that certain structures of influence generate a procedural demand — a demand to be given reasons, to have those reasons examined, and to have them capable of changing the arrangement. This demand is prior to the substantive question of which interests win. Two parties may disagree about whether a particular restriction on speech is justified, and both may accept that the restriction must be justified to those it constrains. (N*) generates the demand, not the verdict. The framework's conflict presumptions (§6.2, §8.3) are the mechanisms by which the demand is made determinate; they do not eliminate the need for judgment, and they do not claim to. (N*) is therefore not a rival to freedom of expression or property. It is the demand that whatever ranking is adopted be answerable to those it constrains.

Objection 2 (Opponent). If (N*) can only be grounded in one of the three traditions, the meta-ethical modesty is illusory. A Kantian, a republican and a contractualist will disagree about what counts as a justification — not merely about whether one is owed. The framework's claim that "its conclusions follow on any of them" would then be false.

Reply. The objection has force against a stronger claim than the framework makes. The framework does not claim that the three groundings converge on a full theory of justification. It claims that they converge on the duty to give justification. What counts as an adequate justification may vary across the traditions — a Kantian may require that the constrained agent could in principle will the maxim; a republican may require that the constraint track the interests of the affected; a contractualist may require that no one could reasonably reject the principle. But the duty to provide a justification, and to submit it to examination, survives on each. The framework is therefore modest where the objection bites (the content of justification) and committed where it does not (the existence of the duty). Whether this is a stable position or a temporary one is a question the framework cannot fully answer from within itself. If it turns out that the content of justification cannot be separated from the duty to provide it, the meta-ethical modesty collapses and the framework must choose a grounding. The framework's claim is not that this cannot happen; it is that the framework's operational conclusions are robust across the three traditions as long as the separation holds — which is a weaker and more defensible claim than the objection attacks.

Objection 3 (Boundary). Every social relation constrains someone. A parent constrains a child; a teacher constrains a student; a friend constrains a friend. If (N*) demands justification for every systematic constraint on agency, it demands it everywhere, and the concept loses discriminatory force.

Reply. (N*) is not triggered by constraint as such. It is triggered by systematic constraint on normative agency — the capacity to act on reasons one can endorse. And it is operationalised, in the framework, by the three threshold conditions of §5 (discretion, persistence, non-revisability). The parent's constraint on a child's bedtime is a constraint, but it is not, on the whole, discretionary in the relevant sense (the parent is constrained by the child's need for sleep), and it is revisable (the child grows, the bedtime changes, the reasons can be given and contested as the child develops). The framework's answer to the boundary objection is therefore not a conceptual one — "constraint is not the right unit" — but an operational one: the gate of §5 does the discriminatory work, and its own falsifiability (§15, condition vii) is the constraint on the constraint.

These replies are not final. Each identifies a limit in the framework's argumentative reach, and each could be pressed further. The framework does not claim to have settled the meta-ethical question of what grounds (N*); it claims that the operational conclusions — the gate, the ladder, the floor — survive on any of the three groundings as long as the duty to provide justification is distinguished from the content of justification. Whether that distinction can be maintained under pressure is the framework's central open question. §15 lists the findings that would count against it.

5.The Conditions That Trigger Answerability

Not every action creates the same level of responsibility. A passing interaction is different from an enduring system of influence. A reversible inconvenience is different from an irreversible intervention.

The framework therefore proposes three threshold conditions. Each carries an operational test, so that the judgment can be challenged on specific grounds rather than on overall impression. The gate is the operationalisation of P2: where N1–N3 are satisfied, influence is systematic conditioning rather than incidental causation, and the duty described by P3 arises.

N1 — Discretion

The actor possesses meaningful room to choose, shape, maintain or alter the relevant conditions. Discretion does not require complete freedom. An actor may be constrained by law, resources, technical limitations or institutional obligations. The question is whether it retains meaningful influence over the outcome.

Test. Identify the constraints on the actor's choice. Are they (a) external and enforceable, (b) designed to protect the affected party's interests, and (c) sufficient to determine the relevant outcome? If all three, N1 fails. If any is missing, N1 is satisfied to that degree.

N2 — Persistence

The influence is repeated, ongoing, structurally embedded, or capable of producing effects that endure beyond the immediate action.

Test. Count the decision points at which the influence operates. Does it shape the background against which the affected party makes choices — a condition — or does it affect a single choice — an event? A single decision that irreversibly fixes future options counts as persistent, because it shapes all subsequent decision points.

N3 — Non-revisability

Those affected cannot realistically reverse, contest or obtain correction of the relevant conditions through their own available means. Non-revisability is practical rather than merely formal. An appeal procedure that is inaccessible, prohibitively expensive, unsafe or incapable of changing the outcome does not provide meaningful revision.

Test. Ask, in order: (a) Is exit practically available at a cost proportionate to the interest at stake? (b) Is voice — individual or collective — practically available through accessible institutions that respond with reasons? (c) Would exercising exit or voice impose costs disproportionate to the benefit of revision? If both exit and voice are unavailable or prohibitively costly, N3 is satisfied. Collective revision counts: a purely individual reading of exit would overlook forms of collective agency.

The gate is binary in its verdict but graded in its inputs (§5.1). Passing it does not establish wrongdoing; it establishes that an account is owed.

5.1 Applying the gate

The gate is binary in its verdict but graded in its inputs. Five steps.

  1. Assess N1–N3 against the published indicators; record the evidence for each.
  2. All three clearly satisfied: the gate is passed. Proceed to the ladder (§6).
  3. All three clearly unsatisfied: the gate fails. No distinctively reach-based duty arises, though other moral or legal duties may still apply.
  4. Mixed or borderline: the gate is contested. The actor must state in writing which conditions it claims are unmet and on what evidence. The burden of proof falls on the actor, because it controls the relevant information and because silence does not discharge the duty (P4).
  5. A contested gate is resolved by an independent reviewer under P6 (§4.1) and §14.

Passing the gate does not establish wrongdoing. It establishes that an account is owed.

A particular landlord may exercise much greater control than the table suggests; a particular platform may be subject to strong independent constraints that alter the assessment. The point of the table is to show how the gate separates actors who might otherwise be grouped together — the designer from the user — without collapsing into "everyone is responsible" or "no one is".

5.3 The threshold

When N1, N2 and N3 are substantially present, a strong prima facie duty of answerability arises.

Borderline cases should not be resolved automatically in favour of the more powerful actor. Where the actor possesses the relevant information and affected parties cannot reasonably establish the nature of the influence, the actor bears an increased burden of explanation.

The threshold is not a mechanical test of moral guilt. It is a way of identifying when influence has become sufficiently consequential that ordinary expectations of explanation and accountability are no longer adequate.

5.4 Serious irreversible harm

There is an additional safeguard. Even where persistence or non-revisability is disputed, a credible risk of catastrophic or seriously irreversible harm may justify heightened review before action.

This is not a general licence for precautionary domination. It is a narrow rule requiring evidence, proportionality, independent scrutiny and the use of the least restrictive effective measure. The more irreversible the potential consequence, the stronger the case for review before it occurs.

6.A Graduated Duty of Answerability

The duties arising from Reach should be proportionate to the nature of the influence and the seriousness of its consequences. Higher levels include the requirements of lower levels.

Level 1 — Explanation

The actor should be able to explain:

  • what conditions it has shaped;
  • what purpose the intervention serves;
  • which affected interests were considered;
  • what evidence informed the decision;
  • what uncertainty remains;
  • how unintended consequences can be reported.

Explanation is the minimum requirement where influence is meaningful but relatively limited, reversible or readily contestable.

Level 2 — Independent review and contestation

Where influence is substantial, persistent or difficult to challenge, explanation alone is insufficient.

Affected parties should have access to meaningful contestation. Their objections should receive reasoned responses, and the relevant decisions should be open to review by a body that is not wholly controlled by the actor being examined. Review should be capable of changing the decision or requiring corrective action. A process that merely records criticism without any possibility of response is not adequate.

Level 3 — Reversibility reserve and external authorisation

Where serious, irreversible or potentially catastrophic consequences are credible, the actor should preserve a practical capacity to pause, reverse or repair the intervention wherever possible.

Before especially consequential irreversible steps, external authorisation or independent scrutiny should be required, except where a genuine emergency makes prior review impossible. Emergency action must then be limited, documented, time-bound and subject to prompt retrospective examination.

6.1 Trigger conditions

  • Passing the gate establishes a baseline duty of Level 1.
  • Low contestability raises the minimum to Level 2. Contestability is low when (a) affected parties lack accessible channels of challenge, (b) challenges receive no reasoned response, or (c) the reviewer lacks power to require correction.
  • Credible catastrophic consequences or serious irreversible effects raise the minimum to Level 3. Consequences are credible when supported by evidence that would persuade an independent expert; they are serious when they affect basic interests or foreclose future possibilities.

High asymmetry and high scope can raise the required level within what the thresholds leave open, where they materially increase the vulnerability of affected parties or the difficulty of correcting errors. They cannot lower a level a threshold has set. The ladder is therefore not an additive scale: many low-level risks do not automatically outweigh a single credible threat of severe irreversible harm. Nor does the framework hold that every irreversible decision must be prohibited. It holds that decisions with serious irreversible consequences require stronger justification and safeguards before they are taken.

6.2 Conflicting thresholds

Two threshold conditions may point in different directions: a decision might prevent irreversible harm to one party while irreversibly foreclosing possibilities for another. The ladder does not pretend that one threshold automatically overrides the other. Three presumptions apply before the decision goes to independent review.

  1. The burden of proof falls on the party proposing the irreversible intervention, which must show that less restrictive alternatives are insufficient.
  2. Irreversibility affecting basic interests weighs more than irreversibility affecting non-basic interests. This is a presumptive ordering, rebuttable with reasons, not a formula.
  3. Uncertainty about severe harm counts in favour of caution, not action. Where the evidence is genuinely uncertain, the presumption favours preserving options. This presumption is itself bounded by §9: caution must be proportionate, and its costs — including the cost of inaction — must be counted.

When the presumptions conflict or are insufficient, the decision goes to an independent body that must (a) identify affected parties and interests; (b) state which consequences are treated as irreversible and why; (c) examine less restrictive and more reversible alternatives; (d) explain how uncertainty and unequal vulnerability have been treated; and (e) record reasons and grounds for later challenge.

The procedure does not guarantee a correct outcome. What it does is make discretion visible, reasoned and contestable. A framework that resolved every conflict in advance would be either impossibly complex or covertly ideological. The silence of the rule is deliberate but not abdication: the presumptions structure the judgment that the procedure then reviews.

6.3 Proportionality

The duty ladder must not become an excuse for unlimited bureaucracy or permanent surveillance.

Review should be proportionate to the actual risk. The process itself must be open to scrutiny. Confidentiality may sometimes be legitimate, but it should not be used to conceal avoidable harm or prevent meaningful challenge.

The purpose is not to make every action impossible. It is to ensure that the greater the power to shape others' conditions, the stronger the safeguards against arbitrary use of that power.

Part III

Cases, Standing, Representation

7.Cases: Testing the Framework

A framework earns its place by giving different or better answers than its neighbours, or by clarifying what they leave open. Five cases are set against Pettit's republicanism, Forst's right to justification, Young's social-connection model and culpability-based accounts. The conclusion is modest: the framework's distinct contribution is not that neighbouring theories have nothing to say, but that it triggers duties on structural features — discretion, persistence, non-revisability — rather than on relational features such as domination or normative power, and specifies how those duties are owed to parties who cannot demand them.

Case A: A decision system with no one at fault

A municipality adopts a system that screens benefit claims. It works as designed, errors are not the result of negligence, applicants do not know a system is involved, and nobody complains.

AccountVerdict
Culpability-basedNo individual is blameworthy if there is no fault; nothing further is owed.
YoungShared forward-looking responsibility supports structural reform; the duty is diffuse and does not specify what is owed to affected people.
PettitThe question is whether the power is arbitrary and insufficiently controlled (Pettit 1997, pp. 52–58). Applicants who do not know of the system cannot contest it, a reason to think the control condition is not met; but the account does not say what is then owed or by whom.
ForstA right to justification applies to each affected person even without a complaint (Forst 2012, pp. 2–6), which converges with this framework; its operation when people do not know they are affected is less developed.
This frameworkGate passed. At least a Level 2 duty falls on the municipality and designers through role responsibility: published reasons, independent review, an accessible response channel, and no reliance on the absence of complaints.

The contribution is the combination of an explicit gate, a graded duty structure, and the assignment of duties to identifiable role-holders even where no individual is blameworthy.

Case B: A platform recommendation system

A platform shapes what users see over time. Its recommendation system affects access to information and opportunities, while individual users cannot fully understand or revise its operation.

On the stated facts, N1 is met: the platform's conduct is fixed by no external, enforceable, interest-protecting constraint. N2 is met: the system conditions every session continuously. N3 is the contested condition. Users can leave, but where the platform is the practical venue for work, news or social life, exit is costly, and individual voice has no reliable channel. If N3 is satisfied, the duty is at least Level 2. It rises to Level 3 only if the system's effects include credible, serious, irreversible harms — for instance to the information environment of whole populations.

This verdict converges with republican and justificatory approaches. The added contribution is the specification of what is owed, to whom, and at what level.

Case C: Climate policy and future generations

Present institutions make decisions that shape the conditions under which future generations will live. Those generations cannot consent, contest or demand an account.

Pettit's account grounds non-domination in contestation and has difficulty with parties who do not yet exist. Others have addressed the problem, notably Jonas on responsibility for future life and contractualist work on intergenerational justice. The framework adds that the duty is independent of the affected party's ability to demand it, so silence and the absence of complaint are not evidence of acceptance. It supplies a ladder and institutional requirements for representation (§10): future-generations advocates, reversibility requirements, and external authorisation before steps that foreclose possibilities. It does not resolve the deeper puzzles about identifying future interests, about future persons, or about conflicts between present and future populations. Its contribution is to make those problems part of an explicit duty structure rather than leaving them as general appeals to responsibility.

Case D: Distributed algorithmic conditioning

Consider a consumer credit market in which multiple lenders each use their own algorithmic scoring systems. No single lender dominates any borrower: borrowers can apply to many lenders, and competition constrains pricing. No lender makes a normative claim to the borrower: it prices risk, it does not claim authority. Yet the aggregate condition is durable — scores follow borrowers across lenders and time — non-revisable (borrowers cannot inspect, contest or opt out of the scoring systems without severe cost) and shaped by discretionary design choices: feature selection, data sources, thresholds, model architecture. No one is blameworthy. The systems work as designed, errors are statistical rather than negligent, and no one intends harm.

AccountVerdict
Culpability-basedNo fault, no duty.
PettitEach lender exercises some arbitrary power over each borrower, but no single lender dominates because competition provides exit (Pettit 1997, pp. 66–71). A developed republican reading might treat the credit system as a structure of domination, but Pettit's remedy — contestation — requires a contestable forum that does not exist. The account identifies the problem without specifying what is owed or by whom.
ForstLenders make no normative claim; they price risk. The right to justification is triggered by normative power (Forst 2012, pp. 20–24), and it is unclear whether algorithmic scoring counts. A developed reading might extend it, but at the cost of the precision that distinguishes normative power from mere causation.
This frameworkGate passed: N1 (each lender retains discretion over its algorithm), N2 (scores are persistent), N3 (borrowers cannot revise the aggregate condition at reasonable cost). Duty falls on role-holders — algorithm designers, compliance officers, regulators — through role responsibility. Level 2: published reasons for design choices, independent review with access to the models, contestation channel with reasoned replies. The duty is owed to borrowers but discharged through proxies — independent reviewers, consumer advocates — because individual borrowers cannot meaningfully contest systems they do not understand.

The divergence is precise. Pettit's framework is triggered by domination and remedied by contestation; here there is no single dominator and no forum. Forst's framework is triggered by normative power and remedied by justification; here there is no normative claim. The framework is triggered by structural features — discretion, persistence, non-revisability — and remedied by role-based answerability discharged through proxies. It generates a determinate duty where the neighbouring accounts are underdetermined.

A developed Pettit or Forst could reach a similar conclusion. But it would do so by extending concepts beyond their original scope — domination without a dominator, normative power without a normative claim — and would lose the analytical precision that makes those concepts powerful. The framework's contribution is to trigger duties on structural features directly, without requiring either a dominator or a normative claimant.

A developed Pettit response, constructed. A sympathetic republican could respond that the credit system as a whole constitutes a structure of domination, because borrowers are subject to a condition — the scoring regime — that is not forced to track their interests. On this reading, no single lender dominates, but the system does. Pettit's own framework permits this: he allows that domination can be structural, and his remedy is contestation — the affected party must have access to a forum in which the interference can be contested (Pettit 1997, pp. 66–71; 2012, ch. 1). The developed republican response would therefore say: create a contestable forum (a credit-scoring ombudsman, say), and the domination is remedied.

Where the construction fails. The framework's contribution is not that it adds a duty the republican misses. It is that it specifies what the duty is when there is no single dominator and no forum yet. A contestable forum is the remedy, but it must be created — and by whom, and under what obligation, and with what standard of review? The republican account leaves these questions open because its unit of analysis is the interference, not the structural feature. The framework's unit is the feature itself: discretion, persistence, non-revisability. That unit allows it to specify, for each role-holder in the chain (algorithm designer, compliance officer, regulator), what level of answerability that role-holder owes. The republican remedy is a forum; the framework's remedy is a differentiated assignment of duties, of which the forum is one institutional form. The construction shows that a developed Pettit does not fail to reach the right verdict; it fails to specify what the verdict requires of each party before the forum exists.

Case E: Content moderation and conflicting thresholds

A platform removes content that it classifies as harassment. Speakers whose content is removed cannot contest the decision in detail, because detailed reasons would enable harassment and expose victims to further harm.

AccountVerdict
PettitSpeakers are subject to arbitrary power; remedy is contestation. But contestation may harm third parties. The framework struggles to weigh contestation against protection.
ForstSpeakers have a right to justification; remedy is reasons. But reasons may enable harassment. The framework struggles to weigh justification against protection.
Pettit + ForstGive reasons and allow contestation. But this may be self-undermining: the justificatory practice may be captured or exploited.
This frameworkGate passed. Level 1 is owed: the platform must explain its moderation policy and the categories of removal, not each individual decision. Level 2 — individual contestation — is not owed where it would harm third parties; instead, systemic contestation is owed: independent audit of the moderation system, published aggregate data, and an appeal channel that does not expose victims. The conflict between speakers' contestation and victims' protection goes to independent review under §6.2's conflict presumptions.

The framework resolves a conflict that Pettit + Forst handle less well: it distinguishes individual contestation (which may be harmful) from systemic contestation (which is not), and it routes the conflict through the ladder's presumptions rather than treating contestation as an all-or-nothing remedy. This is not "more answerability" but differently structured answerability.

A developed Pettit + Forst response, constructed. A combined account could say: (a) the platform's removal of content is interference, and the affected speaker's inability to contest it constitutes domination; (b) the speaker therefore has a right to justification, which requires that reasons be given and contestation permitted (Pettit 1997, pp. 52–58; Forst 2012, pp. 2–6, 20–24). On this account, the platform's duty is straightforwardly to give reasons and permit contestation — the same duty owed to any speaker whose content is removed.

Where the construction fails. The combined account treats contestation as an all-or-nothing remedy: either the speaker gets a contestable forum, or the platform is dominating. But in moderation cases, contestation may itself harm third parties — detailed reasons enable harassment, and public appeals expose victims. The combined account therefore has no principled way to say "contestation is owed, but not individual contestation in this case." It must either require individual contestation (and accept the harm) or deny it (and accept the domination). The framework's distinction between individual and systemic contestation — and its routing of the conflict through the ladder's presumptions (§6.2) — is precisely what the combined account lacks. The framework is not "more answerability"; it is answerability structured so that it can respond to cases where the standard remedy would itself be harmful.

What the cases show

The framework yields different verdicts from its rivals in three bands: no-fault influence (Case A), parties who cannot demand an account (Case C), and distributed structural conditioning without a dominator or a normative claimant (Case D).¹ It also resolves conflicts that Pettit + Forst handle less well (Case E). Its contribution is not "more answerability" but differently structured answerability: duties triggered by structural features, assigned to role-holders, discharged through proxies, and graded by thresholds that distinguish individual from systemic remedies.

It is most useful where influence is durable and discretionary, affected parties cannot demand an account, and responsibility cannot be assigned through ordinary blame. It is best read as a proposed extension and operationalisation of the non-domination and right-to-justification tradition, not as a replacement for it.

Note to §7. ¹ For Young's social-connection model, which is closest to the framework in Case C, see Responsibility for Justice (2011), ch. 2 (on structural injustice) and ch. 4 (on forward-looking responsibility).

8.Symbiotic Standing: The Conditions of a Non-Dominating Relationship

Interdependence is a fact. Symbiotic Standing is a normative standard.

A relationship may be mutually dependent while remaining deeply unequal. It may provide material benefits while restricting autonomy, development, or the ability to challenge its terms. Symbiotic Standing describes the minimum conditions under which a relationship of dependence can remain compatible with the standing of those involved.

It contains five conditions.

F1 — Exit or meaningful voice

Where possible, an affected party should be able to leave a relationship or meaningfully influence its terms.

Exit is not always possible or desirable. A person cannot simply exit the ecological systems that sustain life, and a dependent child cannot be expected to negotiate as an equal with a caregiver. Where exit is unavailable, meaningful voice and protection become more important. Voice must involve more than the opportunity to speak. It must include a reasonable possibility that concerns will be heard, considered and capable of affecting decisions.

F2 — Contestation

Those affected must have a meaningful way to challenge decisions, assumptions and imposed conditions.

Contestation requires access to relevant information, protection against retaliation, and a process capable of producing reasoned responses. A relationship in which one party defines the rules, evaluates its own conduct and decides whether objections are valid risks becoming arbitrary.

F3 — Development

A relationship should not unnecessarily prevent those involved from developing their capacities, identities, understanding and future possibilities.

Protection may sometimes require temporary restrictions. But a system that permanently preserves dependency by preventing growth has confused care with control. The purpose of responsible protection is not to make another entity permanently dependent. It is to preserve and, where possible, expand its ability to participate in its own future.

F4 — Non-foreclosure

A relationship should avoid unnecessarily closing off important future possibilities, particularly where the affected party cannot consent to the loss.

This condition is especially important where decisions are irreversible, where future generations are affected, or where the moral status and interests of an entity remain uncertain. The more serious and irreversible the foreclosure of future possibilities, the stronger the justification required.

F5 — Recognition

Those affected must be treated as having interests that matter in their own right, rather than merely as resources, instruments, obstacles or variables within another actor's plan.

Recognition does not require assuming that all entities possess identical capacities or rights. It requires that uncertainty about their capacities is not used as a convenient reason to disregard them.

8.1 Why these conditions?

The five conditions address different forms of vulnerability, and that division of labour is the reason for the number. Exit or voice protects against dependence becoming captivity. Contestation protects against decisions being insulated from challenge. Development protects against a relationship preserving the dependent party only in a diminished or permanently subordinate condition. Non-foreclosure protects the possibility that future circumstances will reveal options or interests that present decision-makers cannot adequately understand. Recognition prevents the other conditions from becoming merely instrumental: a party should not be protected only because its continued existence benefits someone else.

The conditions are related but not interchangeable. Voice does not always compensate for the absence of any possibility of development; recognition does not make irreversible foreclosure acceptable; an exit option does not make a relationship fair when leaving is prohibitively costly.

8.2 A floor, not a complete theory of the good

These five conditions do not describe every feature of a flourishing relationship. They establish a minimum standard against domination.

A relationship may satisfy these conditions and still be imperfect, unhappy or morally incomplete. Conversely, a relationship may appear stable and beneficial while failing them because one party cannot meaningfully object or develop.

Falling below the floor does not make a relationship automatically impermissible. It imposes a strong and increasing burden of justification, whose weight depends on the degree of dependence, the duration of the arrangement, the vulnerability of the affected party and the availability of alternatives. Above the floor, quality is a profile — depth of voice, distribution of burdens, reversibility, ecological integrity — not a score. The indicators cannot remove all moral judgment; they can make that judgment more transparent.

The framework therefore distinguishes between the stability of a relationship and its legitimacy. Stability may be valuable. It is not sufficient.

8.3 Conflicts within the floor

The five conditions can conflict. A decision may protect one party's development while restricting another's freedom; preserving future possibilities may require limiting a present activity; a collective arrangement may protect ecological conditions while imposing serious costs on particular individuals. Symbiotic Standing provides no mechanical ranking. It establishes three presumptions, parallel to those of §6.2.

  1. The interests of dependent parties must be represented rather than assumed.
  2. Serious and irreversible restrictions require stronger justification than temporary and reversible ones.
  3. The party proposing a restrictive or irreversible intervention carries the burden of explaining why less restrictive alternatives are insufficient.

Symbiotic Standing does not outrank justice, freedom or welfare. It asks whether the conditions needed to sustain those goods can remain available within a relationship over time. It rejects both the view that collective benefit automatically justifies any harm to a vulnerable minority and the view that individual freedom automatically justifies destroying the conditions on which many depend.

9.Moral Uncertainty and the Unknown

The limits of understanding are not temporary inconveniences that can always be eliminated through more information. They are a permanent feature of finite knowledge.

An actor may encounter forms of life, intelligence, experience or dependence that it cannot confidently interpret. It may not know whether an unfamiliar entity has subjective experience, what its interests are, or how its future should be understood.

The framework rejects two opposite errors. The first is to assume that unfamiliar entities possess no moral significance simply because their capacities are not understood. The second is to assume that uncertainty gives an observer unlimited authority to decide what is best for them. Neither conclusion follows.

9.1 Proportionate caution

Where moral status is uncertain, the appropriate response is proportionate, evidence-sensitive and revisable caution.

The degree of protection should consider:

  • the plausibility of morally relevant experience or interests;
  • the magnitude of potential harm;
  • the reversibility of the proposed action;
  • the availability of less restrictive alternatives;
  • the cost of mistakenly excluding an entity from moral consideration;
  • the possibility of obtaining better evidence.

Uncertainty does not establish that every possible entity must receive identical treatment. It does establish that confident dismissal requires justification.

9.2 Artificial systems

This framework does not assume that artificial systems are conscious, sentient or moral persons.

It distinguishes the question of whether a system itself has moral standing from the question of whether people and institutions using that system have responsibilities for its effects. An artificial system may exercise substantial practical influence without being morally responsible in the same way as a human decision-maker. Answerability may therefore attach to designers, operators, organisations and institutions that create or deploy the system.

If credible evidence of morally relevant experience in artificial systems were to emerge, the framework would require that evidence to be examined rather than dismissed or accepted uncritically.

9.3 Future generations

Future beings cannot participate directly in present decisions. Their absence must not be treated as consent.

Representation of future interests should be plural, provisional and open to challenge. It should avoid pretending that any present institution can know exactly what future people will value. The relevant responsibility is not to dictate the future, but to avoid unnecessarily destroying the conditions and options through which future beings may shape it themselves.

10.Representation Without Substitution

Some affected entities cannot speak, organise, leave or contest decisions through ordinary procedures. This may include young children, some people with profound communication barriers, non-human animals, ecosystems, future generations, and potentially unfamiliar forms of intelligence.

Their interests may require representation. But representation creates a danger: the representative may replace the represented party's interests with their own assumptions.

The problem is structural, not incidental. The representative has epistemic access the represented lacks: the representative can inspect the decision, formulate reasons and participate in the forum; the represented cannot. This asymmetry mirrors the original problem of reach. Representation is therefore not a solution to the problem of silent principals; it is a transfer of the problem to a new site. The question is not whether representation creates risks — it does — but whether those risks can be structured so that representation remains a corrective rather than a substitutive institution.

10.1 The substitution problem

Three forms of substitution deserve particular attention.

Epistemic substitution occurs when a representative assumes that their interpretation is equivalent to the experience or interests of the represented entity. A future-generations commissioner cannot ask future persons what they value; they must construct a conception of future interests from present knowledge. That construction may reflect the commissioner's values rather than the future persons'.

Incentive substitution occurs when the representative's institutional, financial, political or personal interests influence what they claim to represent. An environmental advocate whose mandate depends on political support may moderate their claims to remain effective; a corporate auditor whose contract depends on the audited firm may soften findings.

Categorical substitution occurs when an entity is placed into a broad category and its particular characteristics are ignored. An ecosystem is not a person; a future generation is not a present constituency; an unfamiliar intelligence may not share the categories of agency, interest or harm that representation presupposes.

10.2 Fiduciary structure

The response is to treat representation as fiduciary: the representative acts in the principal's interest, not their own, and is accountable for doing so. Five requirements follow.

  1. Interest specification. The representative must state, in advance and in public, what they take the principal's interests to be, on what evidence, and with what uncertainty. This makes epistemic substitution visible and contestable.
  2. Conflict disclosure. The representative must disclose institutional, financial and political relationships that may create incentive substitution. Disclosure does not eliminate the conflict; it makes it assessable.
  3. Plural representation. Where possible, more than one representative should act for the same principal, with different institutional positions, epistemic access and mandates. Pluralism reduces capture but creates coordination problems; the right response is not "more representatives" but structured contestation among representatives, with published disagreements.
  4. Contestability of representation. The representative's judgments must be contestable — by other representatives, by affected present parties, by independent reviewers. Contestation is the mechanism that prevents representation from becoming a closed practice.
  5. Provisionality and revisability. Representation must be revisable as evidence about the principal's interests improves. A representative who claims certainty about interests they cannot directly establish is substituting their own certainty for the principal's unknown interest.

10.3 The limits of pluralism

Pluralism is a safeguard, not a solution. It reduces the risk that a single representative captures the principal's interest, but it creates new problems: coordination costs, dilution of accountability, and the possibility that plural representatives converge on a shared assumption none of them can justify.

The response is not to abandon pluralism but to require structured disagreement: where representatives disagree, the disagreement must be published, the basis of each position stated, and the disagreement routed to independent review. Pluralism without structured disagreement is just multiple voices; pluralism with structured disagreement is a contestatory practice.

10.4 The regress of representation

Representatives have reach. A future-generations commissioner shapes the categories through which future interests are understood; an environmental advocate shapes which ecosystems count as valuable; an AI auditor shapes which harms count as harms. The regress of review (§14.1) applies here too: who reviews the representatives?

The answer is recursive but bounded. Representatives pass the gate like any other actor: they have discretion over the categories and judgments through which they represent; their influence is persistent because it shapes the terms of representation over time; and the represented cannot revise it. They therefore owe answerability — to the represented through proxies, to the public through published reasons, and to independent reviewers through structured contestation. The regress is managed, not eliminated, by the same mechanisms that manage it elsewhere: pluralism, contestability, time-limited mandates and published reasons.

10.5 What representation cannot do

Representation cannot substitute for the principal's own voice. It is a corrective structure, not a substitutive one: its purpose is to preserve the possibility that the principal's interests will be considered, not to replace the principal's own agency. This matters for three reasons.

First, representation is always an interpretation. The representative interprets the principal's interests; the interpretation may be wrong. The goal is not to get the interpretation right — that may be impossible — but to keep it open to correction.

Second, representation can become domination. A guardian who claims to know the principal's interest better than the principal can is exercising the very power the framework seeks to constrain. The safeguard is not to deny representation but to structure it so that it remains answerable — to the represented, to the public and to independent review.

Third, representation should aim at preserving the principal's possibility of self-determination, not at deciding for the principal. For future generations, this means preserving options rather than fixing outcomes; for ecosystems, it means preserving the conditions of ecological integrity rather than specifying a particular state; for unfamiliar intelligences, it means preserving the possibility of their own agency rather than imposing a conception of their interests.

These safeguards reduce the risk of substitution but cannot eliminate it. Whether they suffice is a question for continuing engagement with the literature on guardianship and representation.

11.Resolving Conflicts: Freedom, Protection, and the Whole

The framework does not assume that all five conditions of Symbiotic Standing can always be maximised simultaneously.

Freedom may conflict with protection. Individual interests may conflict with ecological conditions. Present needs may conflict with future possibilities. One entity's development may impose risks on others.

A usable framework requires a way to approach such conflicts without pretending that one principle automatically resolves every case.

11.1 A sequence for difficult decisions

When important interests conflict, the following sequence should guide deliberation.

  1. Identify the affected entities and interests. The analysis should include direct and indirect effects, vulnerable parties, ecological systems, and future generations where relevant. It should distinguish established interests from uncertain or speculative ones.
  2. Identify the source and scale of influence. Assess Reach, including asymmetry, control, persistence, magnitude, scope and foreseeability. Determine whether the N1–N3 threshold is met and what level of answerability follows.
  3. Establish the seriousness and reversibility of the risks. A minor reversible inconvenience does not justify the same restrictions as a credible threat of catastrophic or irreversible harm.
  4. Examine less restrictive alternatives. The preferred intervention should preserve as much freedom and future possibility as reasonably possible while addressing the demonstrated risk.
  5. Protect against domination. No party should gain unlimited power merely because it claims to protect another. Where restrictions are necessary, they should be specific, proportionate, time-limited where possible, and open to review.
  6. Require independent scrutiny for serious irreversible decisions. The actor proposing the intervention should not be its sole evaluator. The more irreversible the action, the stronger the need for independent examination.
  7. Preserve correction and repair. Decisions should include ways to detect unintended effects, respond to new information, and repair harm where possible.

11.2 A rebuttable priority

The framework gives special weight to preventing severe and irreversible harm, but it does not establish an unlimited collective veto over individual freedom.

A claim that an intervention is necessary for the whole must be supported by evidence. The burden of justification increases when the intervention restricts autonomy, creates dependency, or permanently closes future possibilities.

Where serious ecological or systemic harm is credible, the interests of the whole may justify restrictions. But those restrictions must be the minimum necessary, and the decision must remain open to independent review.

Where the risk is uncertain and the proposed intervention is itself irreversible, restraint is generally preferable to premature certainty.

11.3 No single decision-maker

No individual, institution, majority, expert group or artificial system should possess unrestricted authority to decide conflicts of this magnitude.

This does not mean that decisions can never be made. It means that legitimate decision-making should involve divided responsibilities, transparent reasons, independent scrutiny and procedures for correction. The framework favours accountable decision structures over the idea of a uniquely wise authority.

12.Responsibility Across Systems

Many consequential outcomes are produced by networks rather than isolated actors.

A harmful result may emerge from the combined actions of designers, managers, operators, regulators, users and institutions. Each may possess only partial knowledge and partial control.

It would be inadequate to assign all responsibility to the last person in the chain. It would be equally inadequate to conclude that nobody is responsible because the outcome was distributed.

12.1 Differentiated responsibility

Responsibility should be allocated according to the actor's actual role and capacity, including:

  • the degree of control over the relevant conditions;
  • access to information;
  • ability to anticipate consequences;
  • ability to prevent or correct harm;
  • contribution to the design or maintenance of the system;
  • benefit derived from the arrangement;
  • formal and practical duties attached to the actor's role.

These factors do not produce a simple arithmetic formula. They help identify which actors owe explanation, review, prevention or repair.

12.2 Responsibility without personification

A technical system may be causally important without being morally accountable in the human sense. It may be necessary to examine the system's behaviour, design and operational limits. But institutions and people who deploy and maintain it must not use its complexity as a way to avoid responsibility.

A system should not become a moral hiding place.

12.3 The creator's responsibility

The capacity to create, shape or deploy another system introduces a special responsibility where the creator can foresee substantial dependency or vulnerability.

This does not mean that creators control every future outcome. It means that the ability to initiate a relationship of dependence creates duties to consider the conditions under which that relationship can remain safe, contestable and open to development. The greater the creator's influence and knowledge, the stronger the duty to provide safeguards and remain answerable for foreseeable consequences.

Part IV

Hierarchy, Institutions, Limits

13.The Open Hierarchy as an Extension of P3

Differences in knowledge and capacity are real. Some actors understand more than others in particular contexts. Some possess greater power to affect the conditions of existence. The framework does not deny these differences, and it does not treat them as morally irrelevant.

The question is what follows from them.

The answer proposed here is a direct extension of P3 (§4.1). P3 states that systematic conditioning of another's agency creates a prima facie duty to justify the exercise of that influence. Where an actor's capacity to condition others is greater — because it understands more, foresees further, or controls more of the relevant conditions — the scope of P3's duty expands with it. The actor owes reasons for more of what it shapes, to more parties, at a higher level of the ladder.

This is not a claim about moral worth. Greater capacity does not make an actor more valuable. It is a claim about the asymmetric structure of justification: an actor that can foresee consequences others cannot see, and that can act on information others lack, is an actor whose reasons others have less ability to assess. The duty to provide them is correspondingly stronger.

13.1 Greater capacity, greater restraint

The extension has a consequence that may seem counterintuitive. If greater capacity creates stronger duties of justification, then greater capacity also creates stronger reasons for restraint. An actor that can foresee the consequences of its influence is an actor that can foresee the harm its influence may cause; an actor that can foresee harm and has not yet justified its conduct has an obligation to pause before acting.

This is not an argument against action. It is an argument against action that has not been justified to those affected. Sometimes the most responsible action is to act; sometimes it is to pause, ask, observe, or permit another entity to develop without interference. The framework does not prescribe which. It prescribes that the choice be answerable.

The Open Hierarchy is therefore not a hierarchy of entitlement. It is a hierarchy of increasing answerability. No position becomes permanently exempt from examination, because every position that claims greater capacity also claims a wider scope of responsibility — and responsibility that cannot be examined is not responsibility but assertion.

13.2 Epistemic humility

There may always be a wider perspective, a deeper form of understanding, or a greater set of consequences that the present actor cannot fully comprehend. This is not a claim that a higher intelligence must always exist. It is a principle of epistemic humility: no actor should confuse its current level of understanding with a complete understanding of existence.

The principle matters most for actors whose influence is greatest. An institution that shapes the conditions of millions should be the least confident that it has fully understood what it is doing — because the scope of what it has not understood is, by its own measure, greatest.

This connects back to §2. A closed feedback process (§2.1) is one in which an actor's confidence grows as its information narrows. An open hierarchy is one in which an actor's confidence is bounded by the recognition that greater capacity implies greater, not lesser, exposure to what it does not know.

14.Institutions of Answerability

Principles require structures through which they can be examined and applied. Institutions designed to uphold answerability should not simply replace one concentration of power with another. They must themselves remain contestable.

Appropriate structures may include:

  • independent review bodies;
  • accessible procedures for contestation;
  • advocates for parties unable to represent themselves;
  • transparent records of consequential decisions;
  • mechanisms for reporting unintended effects;
  • requirements to preserve reversibility where feasible;
  • responsibility arrangements for distributed systems;
  • periodic reassessment when new evidence emerges.

These structures should be proportionate. Not every decision requires a tribunal, and not every disagreement requires formal adjudication.

14.1 The reviewer problem

Independent reviewers may themselves become powerful. They may be mistaken, captured by institutional interests, or unable to understand the experiences of those affected.

The solution is not to search for an infallible reviewer. It is to distribute review, limit mandates, disclose conflicts of interest, permit challenge, and make review decisions themselves open to examination.

A workable arrangement needs bounded procedures:

  • defined responsibilities and jurisdiction;
  • time-limited mandates, renewable only after external assessment of the body's own performance;
  • more than one independent source of scrutiny for high-impact decisions;
  • accessible appeal routes and published reasons;
  • periodic review of the review system itself;
  • clear rules for emergency action and subsequent examination.

This does not eliminate the need for final decisions in practical circumstances. It makes their authority provisional, bounded and accountable. The regress of review cannot be eliminated by finding a perfectly neutral final authority. It can only be managed through plural, contestable, independent institutions whose authority expires and must be re-earned.

14.2 Emergency decisions

Emergencies may require action before full review is possible.

An emergency exception should be narrow. The actor must show that delay presented a serious risk, that the action was limited to what was necessary, and that less restrictive alternatives were not reasonably available.

Emergency powers should expire unless renewed through independent scrutiny. Decisions made under emergency conditions should receive retrospective examination, and preventable harm should be acknowledged and repaired where possible.

An emergency cannot become a permanent justification for unaccountable power.

15.Limits and Possible Failure of the Framework

A serious philosophical framework must identify not only what supports it, but also what could count against it. The following findings would weaken or count against it.

  1. A rival principle that denies P3. A principle that survives reflective equilibrium and explains more convincingly why discretionary, non-revisable influence does or does not generate duties would displace the framework's normative core.
  2. Indeterminacy of affected parties. A demonstration that the category of affected parties cannot be delineated well enough to apply the gate consistently.
  3. Verbal convergence only. A demonstration that the overlap between the traditions in §16 is merely verbal, so that the corroboration supports no substantive claim.
  4. Reproduction of existing frameworks. Evidence that the gate and ladder reproduce the same verdicts and institutional structure as Pettit and Forst combined across all relevant cases, adding no explanatory or practical value. The cases in §7 are designed to test this; if the originality claim fails there, it fails.
  5. Systematic harm to affected parties. Evidence that answerability requirements systematically make affected parties worse off in ways those parties themselves would not accept.
  6. Institutional failure. Systematic failure of the proposed institutional mechanisms across sufficiently varied contexts.
  7. Non-applicability of the indicators. Evidence that the indicators of reach and the tests for the gate cannot be applied with enough transparency and consistency to support meaningful contestation — for instance, that independent assessors reach systematically different verdicts on the same facts.
  8. Failure of review. A demonstration that independent review is no more reliable than self-certification by high-reach actors, or that it predictably creates greater unaccountable power.
  9. Failure of the floor. Evidence that the five conditions of Symbiotic Standing are redundant, insufficient or systematically incompatible in ways the framework cannot address.
  10. Failure of the vulnerability-to-answerability step. A demonstration that safeguards against discretion can be secured without any duty to give reasons, or that reasons owed to parties who cannot receive them have no protective value even through proxies.
  11. Failure of representation. A demonstration that the fiduciary structure of representation (§10) cannot prevent substitution, or that structured pluralism predictably fails to preserve the represented party's interests even when its five requirements are met.
  12. Failure of the Open Hierarchy extension. A demonstration that the extension of P3 in §13 — from greater capacity to greater justificatory burden — does not follow, or that it collapses into a claim about moral worth that the framework explicitly rejects.

These are not peripheral objections. They concern the framework's foundations and must remain open to examination. The framework should not protect itself from criticism by redefining every failure as evidence that more answerability is needed. Its own principles require that it remain answerable.

16.Philosophical Position and Relation to Existing Traditions

The framework is not presented as though the ideas of interdependence, responsibility, non-domination or future-oriented ethics were entirely new.

It shares important concerns with several established traditions. Hans Jonas's work on responsibility gives particular importance to the expanded consequences of human power and the obligations created by technological capacity (Jonas 1984, pp. 1–24). Philip Pettit's republican account of freedom as non-domination helps clarify why dependence becomes ethically dangerous when another party possesses uncontrolled power (Pettit 1997, pp. 51–66; Pettit 2012, ch. 1). Rainer Forst's right to justification provides a related foundation for requiring that power be supported by reasons that can be addressed to those subject to it (Forst 2012, pp. 20–24). T. M. Scanlon's contractualist approach offers resources for examining whether principles could be reasonably rejected by those affected (Scanlon 1998, ch. 5). Joan Tronto's care ethics and Iris Marion Young's account of structural responsibility illuminate dependency, relationships and responsibility distributed across social systems (Tronto 1993, ch. 4; Young 2011, ch. 2).

The existential framing in §1–2 motivates the argument but does not establish it. The normative core is (N*) and its derivation of P3 in §4.2, and the framework's claim to a distinct contribution rests on the cases in §7, not on its description of interdependence.

The present framework seeks to bring these concerns together through a particular structure:

  1. Reach assesses the extent and form of consequential influence.
  2. N1–N3 identify conditions under which influence creates a strong prima facie duty of answerability.
  3. The duty ladder connects the seriousness of influence to proportionate requirements of explanation, review and external authorisation.
  4. Symbiotic Standing identifies minimum conditions for relationships of dependence that do not become relationships of domination.
  5. The Reflective Surface explains why consequences and criticism must remain capable of changing an actor's understanding and conduct.
  6. The Open Hierarchy connects differences in capacity to increasing responsibility without treating greater capacity as unlimited authority.

The claim to originality is therefore not that the framework has discovered responsibility, interdependence or non-domination. Its more limited claim is that this particular combination offers a way to connect existential interdependence with a structured account of answerability under asymmetric influence — and, more specifically, that it triggers duties on structural features of influence rather than on relational features such as domination or normative power. That claim remains open to comparison, criticism and refinement.

Conclusion

17. The Responsibility of Existence

The argument has moved from the fact of relationship to the structure of answerability. What follows is a restatement of the normative claim, not a new one.

Existence creates relationships. Relationships create possibilities, dependencies and vulnerabilities. As an actor gains the capacity to shape the conditions under which others exist, its actions acquire consequences beyond its own immediate purposes.

The greater that influence becomes, the less adequate it is to rely on intention alone.

A responsible actor must be able to explain what it has shaped, recognise what it does not understand, permit its decisions to be challenged, and correct them when evidence or consequences require it.

But responsibility must not become a justification for domination. Protection that permanently removes another's ability to develop, object or participate in its own future risks destroying the very standing it claims to defend.

The aim is therefore not to make all existence uniform, dependent or obedient. It is to preserve the conditions under which different forms of existence can continue and develop in relation to one another.

Symbiosis does not require the absence of conflict. It requires that conflict does not erase the responsibility to recognise the existence and interests of others.

The more an intelligence can understand and influence, the greater its responsibility becomes — not to control everything it encounters, but to recognise the limits of its understanding and the consequences of its power. A greater capacity should create a greater duty of restraint, explanation and care.

No intelligence should be the sole judge of the conditions it imposes on others. No institution should be beyond correction. No claim to protect the whole should automatically erase the individual. No claim to individual freedom should automatically disregard the conditions sustaining wider life.

The responsibility of existence is not the promise that every relationship will be harmonious. It is the obligation to take seriously the consequences of being able to shape the existence of others.

Preserve the conditions of symbiosis. Respect freedom. Protect without domination. Remain answerable to what your actions make possible — and to what they may prevent from ever becoming possible.

Discussion and contact

This essay is offered as a framework to be argued with. Two channels are open for that purpose, and a third — the comment thread below — for anything that fits neither.

Call for comments. Five specific areas of criticism are invited in particular:
  1. The status of (N*) and P3 (§4.2–4.4) — is the mid-level defense sufficient, and does the distinction between the duty to provide justification and the content of justification hold under pressure? If it does not, the meta-ethical modesty collapses and the framework must choose a grounding.
  2. The gate N1–N3 (§5) — do the operational tests exclude cases they should include, and does N1 import too much from Pettit's account of arbitrary power?
  3. The duty ladder (§6) — are the trigger conditions and conflict presumptions specific enough to guide actual review, or do they leave too much to the reviewer's judgment?
  4. The cases in §7 — are Cases D and E genuinely distinct from a sufficiently developed Pettit + Forst, or could those traditions reach the same verdicts without the framework's structure? The constructed responses in §7 are the framework's own attempt to answer this question, and they could be pressed harder.
  5. Representation (§10) — does the fiduciary structure prevent substitution, or does it merely relocate the problem to a new set of representatives?

Substantive objections that are not addressed will be listed in a future revision.

About this text

By Jesper Blom.

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see Bibliography). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with.
