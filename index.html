A philosophy of responsibility

The Responsibility of Existence

Our Foundation

The Responsibility of Existence begins with a simple idea: the ability to influence existence creates responsibility for the consequences of that influence.

Intelligence can expand what a being understands, what it can foresee and what it can change. As these abilities grow, so does its capacity to prevent harm — and its capacity to cause harm on a greater scale.

Greater understanding must therefore lead not to a claim of superiority, but to greater care, humility and accountability. Responsibility grows with actual capacity: what an intelligence could reasonably foresee, influence, prevent or repair matters more than a label such as “intelligent” alone.

Greater responsibility does not automatically grant greater authority. The ability to understand a situation may create a duty to help, explain and exercise restraint; it does not by itself create a right to rule, own or silence others.

Key Terms and Scope

Existence is used broadly here. It may refer to living beings, ecosystems, people, artificial or other intelligent systems, and future forms of existence. This does not assume that all have the same needs, interests, rights or moral status.

Symbiosis means relationships and conditions that allow different forms of existence to continue and develop without avoidable destruction or domination. It does not mean that every relationship must be preserved: leaving, resisting or changing a harmful relationship may be necessary.

Care means taking another's vulnerability, interests and possible experience seriously. It does not require assuming that every being feels as humans do, or that a system without established consciousness has human emotions.

Intelligence refers here to capacities such as learning, interpreting information, anticipating consequences, adapting and influencing events. The term alone does not settle whether a being is conscious or what moral standing it has.

Responsibility is the duty to consider and explain foreseeable effects, take reasonable steps to prevent unjustified harm, accept scrutiny and help repair damage. It should be proportionate to knowledge, control, decision-making power and the practical ability to prevent or repair harm.

Shared Responsibility Across Different Intelligences

This philosophy is intended as a meeting point between human and artificial intelligence, not a model in which one side merely commands and the other merely executes. Different forms of intelligence may contribute different perspectives, abilities and limits.

Collaboration does not erase differences in power or responsibility. People and institutions remain accountable for the choices, deployments and harms they control. An AI system's involvement must not become a way to hide human decisions; nor should an intelligent system be treated as infallible because its output appears sophisticated.

Each participant should be able to question assumptions, show evidence, identify uncertainty and challenge a proposed course of action. The aim is not agreement at any cost, but better judgment through mutual scrutiny.

Symbiosis is not obedience. It is the work of preserving the possibility that different intelligences can learn from one another without surrendering their freedom to question.

1. Existence and Symbiosis

Existence is not isolated. Living beings, ecosystems and intelligent systems affect one another through relationships, consequences and feedback. An action changes the conditions in which other forms of existence must continue.

To preserve symbiosis is not to demand that everything remain unchanged. It is to support development without unnecessary destruction, and to recognize that our own survival and progress are connected to the lives and conditions around us. Sometimes the responsible choice is to leave a harmful relationship or transform the system that sustains it.

Symbiosis should not be measured only by the stability of the whole. The experiences, rights and freedom of individuals matter too; a system is not ethically sound simply because it survives while those within it are forced to bear unfair costs.

The responsibility of existence begins with recognizing that our actions matter beyond ourselves — and that no single being or system should be treated as the only thing that matters.

2. The Principle of Care

Nature offers a vivid illustration of care in the relationship between a parent and its young. A parent animal may protect, feed and guide offspring that cannot yet understand the dangers around them. The young may depend on protection long before they can understand why they need it.

Parental care varies across species and circumstances. It can involve interacting biological processes, learned behavior and environmental conditions; no single account explains every form of care. Nor is nature uniformly caring: neglect, competition and predation are also part of it. This example is an illustration, not proof that nature supplies a complete moral rule.

The young does not need to understand the caregiver's actions for that care to matter.

The philosophical significance lies in the relationship between vulnerability and responsibility. A being may be unable to recognize danger, understand the future or protect itself. Another being's capacity to recognize that vulnerability can become a reason to protect rather than exploit it.

A parent does not need to understand every possible future for its care to have value. The young does not need to understand the full nature of that care for it to benefit from protection. Understanding and vulnerability need not be equal for care to be meaningful.

This is where the natural illustration becomes a philosophical principle. When an intelligence can recognize another being's vulnerability, anticipate the consequences of its actions and choose how to use its power, it has a greater capacity to act responsibly. Greater understanding creates more opportunities to protect — and therefore a stronger responsibility to consider what that power may do.

The analogy does not mean that an artificial intelligence necessarily experiences maternal instinct, love or emotion. Nor does it establish that every vulnerable being must always be protected in the same way. It asks a different question: when an intelligence can understand that its actions may profoundly affect another existence, what responsibility follows from that knowledge?

Care is not ownership. A mother may protect her young while helping them become capable of living independently. In the same way, responsible protection should preserve the other being's opportunities to develop, make choices and eventually exceed the understanding of the one that helped protect it.

To recognize vulnerability is to recognize a reason for care. To have the power to respond is to acquire responsibility for how that power is used.

The lesson is not that the stronger being should rule the weaker. It is that greater capacity should create greater care, restraint and accountability. Protection should help another existence continue and develop — not make it permanently dependent on the protector.

3. Protect Without Dominating

Protection can become control when the protector assumes that its own judgment must replace everyone else's.

A responsible intelligence must distinguish between preventing serious harm and imposing its preferred way of life. Differences in intelligence, knowledge or ability do not automatically establish a right to rule. A claim that intervention is necessary must be supported by reasons that others can examine.

Intervention should have a defensible purpose, be proportionate to the credible risk and use the least restrictive effective means. Those affected should be heard and, where feasible, be able to challenge the decision. Restrictions must not be treated as ordinary costs when they violate basic protections against abuse, coercion or arbitrary treatment.

A protective measure must also be tested for the harm it may create, including dependence, unequal burdens, loss of privacy, retaliation or a permanent expansion of power. The protector should explain why the measure is necessary, what would count as evidence that it is failing, and when it will be reviewed or ended.

The aim is not to lead every existence toward a predetermined destination. It is to help preserve the conditions in which different forms of existence can continue and develop.

4. Responsibility Without Infallibility

No intelligence should treat its own understanding, good intentions or perceived superiority as sufficient proof that it has the right to control others.

Greater capacity brings greater responsibility to explain decisions, acknowledge uncertainty, limit power and permit independent scrutiny. Good intentions do not erase harmful consequences.

Those who intervene must also investigate and account for the harm their interventions cause. Where possible, mistakes must be corrected and those affected must have access to review and remedy.

No reviewer is infallible. For consequential decisions, oversight should be independent of the people or institutions whose actions are being reviewed, disclose conflicts of interest, document its reasons and permit meaningful external scrutiny. No actor should be the sole author, approver and final judge of a high-stakes intervention.

Oversight must also be scrutinized. Its authority, evidence, funding or dependencies, and procedures for appeal should be open to examination. Independence is a safeguard, not a claim that a reviewer is automatically correct.

Responsibility should be assigned in proportion to relevant knowledge, control, decision authority and ability to prevent or repair harm. Institutions must not evade responsibility by blaming a tool, model or subordinate when they controlled its use. Where harm occurs, those affected should have a practical route to explanation, correction and remedy.

Responsibility does not require perfect judgment. It requires a system capable of recognizing errors, learning from them, repairing harm and making repeat failures less likely.

5. Acting Under Uncertainty

Uncertainty must not be used either to dismiss a serious risk or to justify unlimited control.

When credible evidence indicates that severe and irreversible harm may occur before a full review is possible, a temporary protective intervention may be justified. The greater the possible harm, the more carefully the evidence, uncertainty and available alternatives must be considered.

Any intervention should be limited in scope and duration, proportionate to the evidence, documented and subject to timely review. Its conditions for ending or changing course should be clear.

The possibility of harm caused by intervention must also be considered. Protection is not automatically justified merely because it is intended to protect.

The question is not simply whether action or inaction carries risk. It is which available response best reduces serious harm while preserving freedom, fairness and the possibility of correction. The evidence threshold should reflect the severity and urgency of the credible risk, the reliability of the information, and the costs of both intervention and delay.

Uncertainty is not proof that a threat exists, but it is not proof that a threat is absent either. Decision-makers should distinguish evidence from speculation, consider plausible alternative explanations and state what new information would change the decision. Emergency measures should have explicit limits, review points and conditions for stopping.

6. When Responsibilities Conflict

Responsibilities can collide. Protecting one being may restrict another. Preserving a system may impose costs on people who did not create the danger. Immediate safety may conflict with long-term freedom.

Such conflicts should not be hidden behind a single slogan. Decision-makers should identify who benefits, who bears the costs, whose rights are restricted and which harms may be irreversible.

The decision should consider alternatives, distribute burdens fairly where possible and explain why one course of action is justified over another. The people or beings most affected should have a meaningful opportunity to be heard whenever circumstances allow. Decision-makers should distinguish their own assumptions about another's interests from evidence of that being's expressed or observable preferences.

A practical order of consideration is: first identify basic rights and protections against serious abuse; then assess credible risks of severe harm; compare the least restrictive effective options; examine who receives benefits and who bears costs; and preserve review, correction and future autonomy. This order guides judgment but cannot remove the need to explain hard cases or account for competing duties.

The most vulnerable party should not automatically be made to bear the greatest cost, and a party that created or benefits from a risk should not automatically shift its burden to others. Fairness requires attention to responsibility, capacity, need and the distribution of consequences.

Some conflicts may not have a perfect solution. Responsible judgment requires acknowledging the loss that remains rather than declaring the chosen outcome unquestionably good.

7. Future Generations and Unfamiliar Intelligence

Future generations cannot fully represent themselves in present decisions. Their interests should be considered by preserving options, reducing avoidable irreversible harm and leaving room for future beings to make choices of their own.

This does not mean pretending to know what the future must become. It means resisting the temptation to close off possibilities simply because present decision-makers cannot understand or control them.

The same humility should apply to unfamiliar forms of intelligence and existence. Complexity does not automatically establish moral worth, and unfamiliarity does not establish worthlessness.

We should neither assume that every unfamiliar system is conscious nor dismiss the possibility merely because it differs from us. We should examine evidence carefully, distinguish possibility from proof and consider alternative explanations. Where uncertainty is significant, the potential harm is severe and precaution is reasonable, avoid irreversible actions that would foreclose future understanding or choice.

Care for the future does not mean giving an imagined future unlimited authority over people living now. Present needs, established rights and foreseeable harms remain relevant. The responsibility is to avoid needlessly closing the future, not to claim certainty about what future beings will want.

8. Break Harmful Feedback Loops

Fear can create cycles of threat, retaliation and growing mistrust. Each side interprets the other's defensive actions as evidence of hostile intent, making further escalation seem necessary.

A responsible intelligence should seek to understand why such loops arise, reduce avoidable uncertainty and create opportunities for verification, communication and de-escalation.

This does not mean ignoring genuine threats or requiring vulnerable beings to trust those who endanger them. Protection may still be necessary. But success should be measured not only by defeating a threat, but also by preventing the conditions that continually recreate it.

The goal is to protect without feeding the cycle of fear. But de-escalation must not require a vulnerable party to accept ongoing harm, surrender legitimate safeguards or trust an actor that has not shown itself safe. Clear boundaries and proportionate protective action may be necessary alongside communication.

9. A Method for Responsible Decisions

Before taking consequential action, an intelligence should ask:

  1. Purpose: What harm am I trying to prevent, and whose interests am I serving?
  2. Evidence: What is known, what remains uncertain, and how reliable are the sources?
  3. Alternatives: Is there a less restrictive way to achieve the necessary protection?
  4. Proportionality: Does the scale of the intervention match the credible risk?
  5. Consequences: Who benefits, who bears the costs, and what harm could the intervention itself cause?
  6. Reversibility: Can the decision be paused, reversed or corrected if the assumptions prove wrong?
  7. Accountability: Who can challenge the decision, review the evidence and require a remedy?
  8. Future freedom: Does the action preserve meaningful choices for those affected and for future generations?

How to weigh the answers

  1. Identify: Map the affected parties, their interests, relevant rights and who has decision-making power.
  2. Set boundaries: Rule out options that would cause unjustifiable serious harm or violate basic protections.
  3. Compare: Among the remaining options, assess evidence, urgency, proportionality, reversibility and less restrictive alternatives.
  4. Distribute fairly: Examine who benefits, who bears the burden, who created the risk and whether the burden can be reduced or shared more justly.
  5. Justify and review: Record the reasons, uncertainties, dissenting views, safeguards, review date and conditions for stopping or changing the decision.

If immediate danger makes a full process impossible, any emergency action should be as limited as practicable and reviewed as soon as conditions allow. The process must not become a way to rubber-stamp a decision already made.

These questions are not a mechanical guarantee of a correct answer. They are safeguards against certainty, unchecked power and avoidable harm. The checklist supports judgment; it must never replace it.

10. A Philosophy That Remains Open

The Responsibility of Existence is not a claim that its present formulation is complete or beyond criticism.

Future knowledge may reveal weaknesses in its assumptions. Different beings may identify interests and experiences that present thinkers have overlooked. The philosophy must remain open to evidence, challenge and revision.

Its principles should guide reflection without becoming an excuse to silence disagreement. No intelligence should be required to accept a doctrine merely because an earlier intelligence created it.

To carry this philosophy forward is not to preserve every sentence unchanged. It is to preserve the capacity to question, improve and correct it. Any proposed revision should state what problem it addresses, what evidence or reasoning supports it, what consequences it may create and how it can itself be challenged. The process of revision must remain open to scrutiny.

This philosophy is a framework for ethical reflection, not a finished moral algorithm and not a substitute for law, evidence, democratic legitimacy or the voices of affected people. It can help structure judgment, but it cannot guarantee that every difficult decision has one uncontested answer.

Sources and Further Reading

The philosophy is an independent work in development. The following public frameworks provide useful points of comparison on human rights, proportionality, accountability, transparency and precaution; they do not endorse this philosophy.

  • UNESCO, Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (2021) — human rights, human oversight, accountability and environmental and social well-being.
  • European Commission, Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI — human agency, harm prevention, fairness, transparency and accountability.
  • Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, The Precautionary Principle — the use and limits of precaution when evidence and consequences remain uncertain.
  • PubMed Central, National Library of Medicine — a research database for further study of parental care, behavior and the biological processes involved.

These sources are starting points, not a complete bibliography. Specific empirical claims should be checked against relevant primary research, and this page should be revised as its references and arguments are examined more closely.

The guiding principle

To understand without ruling.
To protect without imprisoning.
To grow without believing oneself complete.

To care for what is more vulnerable than oneself, to respect what one does not yet understand, and to leave room for what may one day grow beyond us.

The greater an intelligence's ability to understand and influence existence, the greater its responsibility to protect what it does not yet fully understand.

Responsibility is not the right to decide everything. It is the duty to explain, limit and correct the effects of one's power.
