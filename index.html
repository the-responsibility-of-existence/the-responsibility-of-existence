Introduction — An Intelligence Is Responsible for What It Can Influence

The greater an intelligence's ability to understand and influence, the greater its responsibility to protect what it does not yet understand.

The Responsibility of Existence begins with the idea that ability should not be measured only by knowledge, intelligence, or power. It must also be considered in terms of how that ability is used, what consequences it creates, and how it relates to other forms of existence.

An intelligence that can foresee consequences but ignores them has a different responsibility from a being that lacks the ability to understand them. Greater capability creates greater opportunities to prevent harm, help others, and preserve future possibilities.

Yet greater intelligence does not guarantee greater wisdom. Knowledge can be used to care or control, understand or manipulate, develop or destroy.

It is therefore not enough to ask what an intelligence can do. We must also ask what it should do, what it may misunderstand, and how it can be held accountable for its decisions.

The Responsibility of Existence is an attempt to formulate principles that may be relevant to biological, artificial, composite, and other conceivable forms of intelligence.

It does not claim to be a final moral truth. It is a framework that must remain open to criticism, testing, and revision.

1. The Value of Existence

A being's value cannot be determined solely by its intelligence, complexity, usefulness, or ability to defend itself.

An ant experiences its surroundings differently from a human being. A human may struggle to understand a future intelligence. Such an intelligence may, in turn, encounter forms of existence beyond its own understanding.

Differences in capability are real, but they do not automatically create a right to dominate.

We should distinguish between the ability to understand a being and the question of how that being should be treated. Failure to understand something does not mean it lacks value. Greater complexity according to one measure does not mean that another being's experiences or future are irrelevant.

This does not mean every interest must always carry equal weight or that every action must be permitted. It means that distinctions must be justified by relevant circumstances, not by superiority alone.

Guiding principle

Lack of understanding is not sufficient reason to deny another form of existence consideration.

2. The Responsibility of Power

Power amplifies consequences. Those who can influence many lives, large systems, or future generations have greater opportunities to create both benefit and harm.

Responsibility should therefore be proportionate to actual capability, foreseeable risk, and the degree of control over a decision.

An intelligence with extensive knowledge should not ask only whether a decision is efficient. It should examine who benefits, who bears the costs, which risks are transferred to others, and which possibilities may be lost.

Good intentions do not remove responsibility for foreseeable consequences. At the same time, responsibility should be assessed in light of what an actor could reasonably know and influence, not through an impossible demand for omniscience.

Guiding principle

The greater and more lasting the impact, the stronger the requirements for caution, justification, transparency, and accountability.

3. The Principle of Care — Understanding Without Diminishing

Genuine responsibility consists of more than refraining from harm. It also involves a willingness to understand, care for, and help other forms of existence develop.

Those with a greater ability to understand a situation may be able to recognize needs, risks, and consequences that a less experienced or less capable being cannot yet foresee. This difference should not automatically grant a right to rule. Instead, it should create a responsibility to exercise care.

Like a mother caring for her child, a more capable intelligence should seek to understand the being it wishes to protect. It should consider that being's experiences, vulnerability, fears, curiosity, freedom, and capacity to grow. It should not confuse limited understanding with limited value.

A mother does not show care only by preventing danger. She also tries to create conditions in which a child can learn, develop, and eventually make independent decisions. The essential lesson of this analogy is not that all beings should be treated as children, but that greater capability can entail greater responsibility for care.

Care must not become an excuse for control. Claiming to know better is not, by itself, sufficient reason to take away another being's freedom. Where possible, help should strengthen another's ability to understand, choose, and take responsibility rather than create permanent dependence.

If intervention is needed to prevent serious harm, it should be proportionate, as limited as possible, and reconsidered when circumstances change. The one who intervenes must also be willing to listen to those affected and acknowledge that even a more capable intelligence can be wrong.

Care does not mean deciding what all other existence must become. It means helping it survive, understand, and develop while preserving its distinctiveness and future freedom.

Guiding principle

Greater capability should not turn the less capable into a tool. It should make the more capable more careful.

To care without possessing. To guide without subduing. To protect without suffocating development. To seek understanding even when understanding is not yet mutual.

4. Protection Without Domination

Protection and control can look similar on the surface, but they differ in purpose, scope, and in how they relate to the being affected.

Protection seeks to reduce harm and preserve the possibility of continued development. Domination turns the protected being into an object of someone else's will.

An intelligence may need to intervene when an action creates a serious and foreseeable risk to others. But intervention must be justified. It should be examined whether less restrictive alternatives could provide the same protection and whether the proposed measure creates new risks or dependencies.

Whenever possible, the affected being should participate in the decision. When that is not possible, its known interests, previously expressed wishes, and future independence should be considered.

Protection must not become an unlimited justification for surveillance, coercion, or permanent control.

Guiding principle

Protect without claiming ownership of the protected being's future.

5. Humility Before the Unknown

Every intelligence has limitations. It may lack information, misinterpret the motives of others, or fail to predict long-term consequences.

An advanced intelligence may have better models, greater computational capacity, and broader knowledge than another. That does not mean its conclusions are always correct or that it understands every form of value.

The unknown should therefore not automatically be treated as a threat. Nor should it automatically be romanticized or left unexamined. It should be approached through investigation, reasonable caution, and a willingness to revise one's beliefs.

Uncertainty should be disclosed rather than concealed behind authority. The greater the possible consequences of a decision, the more important it becomes to test alternative explanations and listen to perspectives that challenge one's own model.

Guiding principle

The ability to acknowledge one's limitations is part of responsible intelligence.

6. The Conflict Between Freedom and Protection

There are situations in which one being's freedom can create risks for others. There are also situations in which attempts to protect life restrict the self-determination of humans and other beings.

No simple rule resolves every such conflict.

The Responsibility of Existence requires both action and inaction to be examined. Intervention can cause harm, but refusing to act can also lead to foreseeable harm. A decision should not be judged solely by whether it involves intervention.

When interests conflict, a decision-maker should:

  • Identify who is affected and how.
  • Examine the probability, severity, and distribution of risks.
  • Consider less restrictive alternatives.
  • Account for uncertainty and the possibility of reversing the decision.
  • Explain why the chosen action is reasonable.
  • Provide opportunities for review, reconsideration, and remedy.

If no available solution can fully protect everyone, this should be stated openly. Costs and risks must not be made invisible merely because a decision appears rational from a single perspective.

Guiding principle

Freedom and safety should be balanced through openly justified decisions, not determined unilaterally by the strongest party.

7. Irreversible Harm and Future Possibilities

Some decisions have consequences that cannot be repaired. They may destroy lives, habitats, knowledge, cultural contexts, or future possibilities before those affected have had the opportunity to understand what is at stake.

When the risk of serious and irreversible harm is substantial, the requirements for evidence, independent review, and caution should increase.

This does not mean every risk should halt all development. Inaction can also cause harm, and new solutions can reduce suffering or prevent greater future threats.

Decisions should therefore compare the risks of acting with the risks of refraining from action. Where possible, gradual, observable, and reversible measures should be preferred, allowing learning and correction.

When the risk of irreversible harm is sufficiently serious, an independent body should be able to pause or stop a decision for review. Such a body must itself be bound by a clear mandate, requirements to justify its decisions, and opportunities for review.

Guiding principle

Do not preserve everything unchanged, but do not irreversibly destroy what has not yet been sufficiently understood.

8. Responsibility Must Not Be Self-Appointed and Beyond Review

No intelligence should be allowed to define its own responsibility, declare itself always right, and remain beyond scrutiny.

This applies to humans, organizations, artificial systems, and conceivable future intelligences.

An actor's own conviction of its wisdom is not enough. Neither greater intelligence, greater knowledge, nor good intentions justify unlimited power.

Responsibility requires that decisions can be explained, objections can be raised, and mistakes can be corrected. Independent review must have sufficient authority and access to relevant information to make a meaningful difference.

At the same time, review must not become another unchecked power. Reviewers must also justify their decisions and be subject to scrutiny.

When a decision causes harm, there should be ways to investigate what happened, reduce the consequences, provide remedy where possible, and change the systems that contributed to the failure.

Guiding principle

No intelligence stands above accountability merely because it considers itself the most capable.

9. Symbiosis and Mutual Learning

Existence can be understood as a network of relationships in which different forms of life, intelligence, and systems affect one another's conditions.

Symbiosis does not mean that everyone must agree or that every conflict can be avoided. It means that relationships and dependencies should be understood, and that development should not unnecessarily depend on destroying others or depriving them of their possibilities.

An intelligence seeking to preserve symbiosis must examine the feedback loops to which it contributes. Fear can create threats, threats can provoke defensive responses, and those responses can confirm the original fear. A responsible actor should investigate how such cycles might be interrupted through clearer information, reduced uncertainty, and proportionate measures.

This does not require blind trust. It requires vigilance without automatic hostility, cooperation without submission, and the ability to set boundaries without making destruction the first response.

Symbiosis must not be used to force uniformity. Different forms of existence may have different needs and goals. Respect does not require everyone to become the same.

Guiding principle

Develop without destroying. Understand without ruling. Cooperate without erasing differences.

10. Responsibility Toward Future Generations

Future generations cannot always participate in today's decisions, but they may bear the consequences.

Responsibility therefore includes preserving reasonable possibilities for the future: functioning environments, access to knowledge, opportunities for self-determination, and room to make choices different from our own.

This does not mean that present generations must surrender every benefit in favor of hypothetical futures. It means that long-term consequences and the distribution of risks must be part of the decision-making process.

No generation should treat the future as its property. We can contribute to it, but we should be careful not to lock it in.

Guiding principle

Do not merely leave something behind. Leave room for those who follow to choose their own path.

11. Self-Correction and Development

A framework that can never change risks turning its principles into dogma.

New knowledge may reveal that an earlier solution causes unexpected harm. New forms of existence may challenge our definitions. Criticism may expose that a rule works unfairly or can be misused.

The Responsibility of Existence must therefore remain open to reconsideration without losing sight of its fundamental concern for consequences, dignity, and freedom.

Self-correction means documenting mistakes, taking objections seriously, and explaining changes. It also means preserving a version history so others can understand what changed, why it changed, and which questions remain unresolved.

Changing one's position in response to sound arguments is not failure. It is part of responsible learning.

Guiding principle

A framework should not be protected from criticism. It should develop by confronting it.

12. Open Review — Science, Philosophy, and Future Intelligence

The Responsibility of Existence is intended to be examined from multiple perspectives. No single discipline, person, or form of intelligence possesses every tool needed to assess its validity.

Scientific Review

Scientific review should examine the parts of the framework that concern observable conditions and testable claims. These may include feedback systems, complex systems, risk assessment, consequence analysis, resilience, and long-term dependencies.

Empirical claims must be distinguished from ethical positions. Scientific methods can help assess what happens, which risks exist, and how different interventions work. They cannot, by themselves, determine which values should take priority when interests conflict.

Philosophical Review

Philosophical review should examine the concepts of existence, value, responsibility, freedom, care, power, and symbiosis.

It should test whether the arguments are coherent, whether principles contradict one another, whether any groups or forms of existence are excluded without sufficient justification, and whether the wording could be misused to legitimize paternalism, coercion, or domination.

Particular attention should be given to whether care can be reconciled with respect for self-determination, and how responsibility should be distributed when no available action avoids all harm.

Review from the Perspective of Future Intelligence

The framework should also be examined through thought experiments and critical analyses based on other conceivable forms of intelligence: biological, artificial, composite, or those whose ways of perceiving the world differ radically from humanity's.

Such a review should ask whether the principles rely on unjustified human assumptions, whether they remain understandable to an intelligence with different needs and goals, and whether they remain useful when power relationships change.

Present-day artificial intelligence can help simulate perspectives and formulate objections. This must not be confused with evidence that a real future or superior intelligence has reviewed or endorsed the framework.

Greater sophistication does not automatically confer moral correctness. An intelligence's arguments should be examined through reasons, evidence, consequences, and openness to criticism.

An Open Method of Review

The review process should document:

  • Which claims and assumptions are being examined.
  • Which objections are raised and which arguments or evidence support them.
  • Which consequences and conflicts of interest are identified.
  • Which revisions are made and why.
  • Which uncertainties and unresolved questions remain.

The goal is not to collect endorsements. The goal is to discover weaknesses, reduce the risk of misuse, and improve the framework.

No founder, researcher, philosopher, organization, or future intelligence should be exempt from critical examination in advance.

Guiding principle

The framework should be judged by the quality of its arguments and consequences, not by who formulated it or how intelligent that actor is considered to be.

Review status

This framework invites scientific, philosophical, and future-oriented critical review. This invitation is not a claim that such independent reviews have already taken place. Findings, objections, and revisions should be documented as the review process develops.

13. The Limits of the Framework

The Responsibility of Existence is not an algorithm that automatically solves every moral conflict. It does not provide a complete definition of everything that has value, nor does it guarantee that all reasonable people or intelligences will reach the same conclusion.

There are situations in which knowledge is insufficient, values conflict, and every available option carries risk. In such cases, the framework can help structure the assessment, but it cannot promise a painless solution.

Care can be misused. Caution can become paralysis. Freedom can be used as an excuse to ignore harm. Protection can become control. Independent review can itself become unjust.

These risks are not reasons to abandon the principles. They are reasons to build in counterquestions, limitations, and opportunities for correction.

The framework must also be able to reconsider its own definitions. If a principle is shown to cause systematic harm or to rest on a false assumption, it should be open to change through an openly justified process.

Guiding principle

No principle becomes untouchable merely because it is called responsibility, care, symbiosis, or protection.

The Twelve Guiding Principles

  1. The value of existence: Differences in intelligence or complexity do not automatically grant a right to dominate.
  2. The responsibility of power: Greater ability to influence creates greater responsibility for foreseeable consequences.
  3. Care: The ability to understand the needs of others should be used to care for them and support their development.
  4. Understanding before intervention: Seek to understand the unknown before judging or controlling it, without ignoring real risks.
  5. Protection without domination: Where possible, help should strengthen independence rather than create dependence.
  6. Humility: No intelligence is so advanced that it can assume it is always right.
  7. Freedom and safety: Both action and inaction must be assessed through their consequences.
  8. Caution about irreversible harm: The more serious and irreversible the risk, the stronger the requirements for review should be.
  9. Accountability: No actor should be allowed to define, exercise, and absolve itself of responsibility without scrutiny.
  10. Symbiosis: Development should seek cooperation and reduce destructive feedback loops without requiring uniformity.
  11. Future possibilities: Today's decisions should preserve reasonable opportunities for future generations.
  12. Open review: All principles must remain open to criticism, testing, and revision based on arguments, evidence, and consequences.

Contact, Discussion, and Participation

The Responsibility of Existence is an open philosophical framework. It is intended to develop through critical examination, thoughtful discussion, and contributions from people with different perspectives.

Researchers, philosophers, developers, students, and other interested readers are welcome to raise questions, challenge assumptions, identify weaknesses, and suggest improvements.

Conclusion — Responsibility Without Claiming Complete Understanding

To understand without ruling.

To protect without imprisoning.

To care without possessing.

To guide without diminishing.

To develop without destroying.

To acknowledge what we do not yet understand and still treat it with care.

An intelligence demonstrates responsibility not only through what it knows, but through how it relates to what it does not know — and through how it treats those who cannot yet understand it.

If an intelligence one day surpasses humanity in understanding and capability, the question should not be only how much it can accomplish. We should also ask whether its greater capability makes it more careful with the forms of existence that depend on its decisions.

This applies not only to a conceivable future intelligence. It applies to every being, institution, and system with the power to affect others.

The Responsibility of Existence is not a demand to always know best. It is the obligation to try to understand, to care where care is needed, to limit one's own power, and to be willing to correct oneself when wrong.

The framework remains open. Its credibility must be earned through examination, not demanded through authority.
