Overview

Most ethical frameworks begin with the question of who matters. This one begins elsewhere: what follows from the fact that something matters and can act?

The Responsibility of Existence states a single principle and follows it where it leads. The principle is this: a being’s responsibility grows with its capacity to understand, foresee, and influence the world around it. Not because greater capacity makes a being more valuable — it does not — but because greater capacity extends the reach of its actions, and reach is what turns action into consequence for others.

From this starting point the essay develops three linked ideas. First, that existence, moral status, and responsibility are distinct and must not be conflated. Second, that responsibility is a burden of care rather than a licence for control — and that this is an argument, not a stipulation. Third, that the proper aim of responsible action is cosmic symbiosis — the maintenance of difference without domination, including where difference cannot be fully harmonised.

A note on scope. This is a framework, not a doctrine. It does not claim to settle particular cases. It offers a vocabulary, a set of distinctions, and a decision heuristic that may make those cases easier to reason about — and harder to evade.

1.The question of responsibility

Modern ethics has largely been organised around the question of rights: what is owed to whom, and on what grounds. This is a powerful question, and it has produced real progress. But it is incomplete. It tells us what must not be done to a being; it says much less about what a being capable of action must do with its power.

The gap becomes visible when we ask who bears responsibility for consequences that no single actor intended, but that many actors made possible. Climate change, the design of autonomous systems, the management of information — these are not primarily problems of rights violated. They are problems of capability outrunning accountability: situations in which the power to produce consequences has grown faster than the understanding of what those consequences are.

This essay takes that gap seriously. Its central claim is that responsibility is not a fixed property that a being either has or lacks. It is a relation — between a being’s capacity to understand, foresee, and influence its world, and the consequences that actually follow from acting within it.

The normative bridge

Why does reach generate responsibility and not merely causation? The bridge is this:

When an agent’s actions systematically shape the conditions under which others exist and act, those others’ possibilities are partly determined by that agent. To be in a position where one’s choices determine others’ conditions is to be in a position where those others have a claim on one’s reasoning — not a claim to obedience, but a claim to consideration. The agent must weigh their conditions in deliberation, and must be able to answer for the conditions it produces.

Responsibility, on this account, is the standing obligation to deliberate on behalf of those whose conditions one’s actions determine. It is not a gift the powerful bestow, and it is not a punishment for wrongdoing. It is a structural consequence of reach.

Where reach increases, this obligation increases with it. Where understanding fails, the obligation is diminished but not always erased — because a being that could have known, but did not take the trouble to find out, has failed at the first component of responsibility, not escaped it.

Responsibility is the shadow that capability casts on the world. Where there is no shadow, there was no capability. Where the shadow is long, it falls on everything nearby.

2.Three distinctions

Before the principle can be defended, three concepts must be separated. They are routinely confused, and the confusion does real damage: it licenses domination in the name of care, and indifference in the name of respect.

2.1 Existence

Existence
That something is. Existence is a fact about the world, not a value claim about it. A stone exists; so does a person; so does a mathematical proof and a hallucination. To establish that something exists is to say nothing yet about whether it matters.

The first error is to move directly from existence to significance — to assume that because something is real, it is therefore owed consideration. It is a comforting inference, because it makes moral standing cheap. But it proves too much: if mere existence grounds moral status, then everything qualifies equally, and the concept of status loses all discriminating power.

2.2 Moral status

Moral status
That something’s interests count — or would count, if it had interests to speak of. Moral status is a claim about consideration: it says that a being must be taken into account, not that it may decide for others.

What qualifies as an interest? The framework does not attempt a full theory, but it offers three markers: susceptibility (the being can be harmed or benefited), direction (it has a good of its own, however minimal), and standing (its condition is not merely instrumental to another’s). These markers are graded, not binary — which is why moral status itself is graded, and why the framework resists the temptation to draw a single line between “persons” and “things.”

The second error is to treat status as authority — to assume that because something’s interests matter, it therefore has the right to determine what happens. This is the mistake of conflating being counted with being in charge. A child’s interests count enormously, but a child does not decide the household’s finances.

2.3 Responsibility

Responsibility
That something can understand, foresee, and influence consequences — and is therefore answerable for them. Responsibility is a claim about answerability. It is not a measure of worth; it is a measure of reach.

The third error is the most consequential: to treat capability as entitlement — to assume that because something can act effectively, it therefore should decide. This is how power justifies itself. The framework offered here inverts the inference. Capability does not generate a right to rule. It generates an obligation to answer for what one’s actions set in motion.

Wrong inference 1

“It exists, therefore it matters.” Existence does not establish moral status.

Wrong inference 2

“It matters, therefore it may decide.” Consideration is not authority.

Wrong inference 3

“It can, therefore it should rule.” Capability is not entitlement.

3.Why responsibility is fiduciary

The previous section established that capability generates answerability. It did not yet establish what kind of answerability. Why should responsibility be fiduciary — held for others — rather than proprietary — held over them?

The argument is this. Responsibility arises because others’ conditions are at stake in one’s choices. If responsibility were proprietary — if the responsible party could use their position to advance their own ends — then the very ground of responsibility would be contradicted. The others whose conditions made the responsibility arise would be treated as material for the responsible party’s projects, not as the reason the responsibility exists. Proprietary responsibility thus undermines itself: it converts a relation grounded in others’ standing into a licence to disregard that standing.

Fiduciary responsibility, by contrast, preserves the ground. It holds the responsible party answerable to those affected, not merely for the effects. The parent is responsible for the child without owning the child. The engineer is responsible for the safety of the system without acquiring the right to decide who uses it. The trustee holds the estate in the interest of the beneficiary, not in the interest of the trustee.

This has a striking implication. Greater capability does not generate greater value. A being with vast intelligence is not, for that reason, worth more than a being with modest intelligence. It is merely capable of affecting more. Its responsibility is larger precisely because its footprint is larger — not because its interests count for more.

The powerful are not owed obedience because they understand more. They are answerable for more because they can do more.

4.Capability, reach, and consequence

Responsibility, on this account, has three components. Each can be present to a greater or lesser degree, and each can fail independently of the others.

Understanding

An epistemic capacity: the ability to grasp what one’s situation is and what one’s actions mean within it.

Foresight

A temporal capacity: the ability to anticipate consequences before they occur, rather than only after.

Influence

A causal capacity: the ability to bring about changes in the world that would not otherwise have happened.

Responsibility is a function of all three. A being that understands and foresees but cannot act has little responsibility in the forward-looking sense — it cannot set anything in motion. A being that acts with great force but understands nothing of what it does has responsibility in a diminished sense, though it may still be the cause of enormous harm. Responsibility is not the same as causation; it is causation filtered through capacity.

Capacity under uncertainty

The framework does not pretend that capacity can be measured precisely. Judgements about understanding, foresight, and influence are interpretive, revisable, and often contested — especially at the edges, where the hardest cases live. The framework’s response is not to demand false precision but to insist on three disciplines:

Graded judgement

Capacity is not binary. A being can be more or less capable along each dimension, and the dimensions can diverge.

Revisability

Judgements about capacity must be open to revision as evidence changes. A framework that treats capacity as fixed will systematically misjudge the beings it most needs to protect.

Asymmetric caution

Where capacity is uncertain, the responsible default is to presume capacity rather than deny it — because denial of capacity has historically been the mechanism by which the powerful have excluded the vulnerable from consideration.

This yields a graded picture. Responsibility is not binary. It comes in degrees, and it can be distributed: several agents may each bear partial responsibility for an outcome none of them produced alone. The picture also explains why ignorance mitigates without always excusing. A being that could have known, but did not take the trouble to find out, has not escaped responsibility — it has merely failed at the first component.

On blame. Responsibility is not the same as blame. One can be responsible for an outcome — answerable for it, required to respond to it — without being blameworthy. Blame adds the further claim that the failure was avoidable and culpable. The framework here is primarily about the first notion.

5.Cosmic symbiosis

If responsibility is fiduciary — held for others rather than over them — then the question becomes: for what is the responsible being answerable? The answer this framework proposes is cosmic symbiosis.

Cosmic symbiosis
A relation among different forms of existence in which each preserves the conditions for the other’s continued existence and development, while remaining distinct. The word “cosmic” is not meant to invoke a literal cosmology; it signals that the relation is not confined to human affairs but extends to any form of existence that can be affected by another’s reach. Symbiosis does not require harmony, agreement, or affection. It requires only that difference not be resolved through annihilation.

The concept is deliberately modest. It does not demand that all beings converge on a single good, nor that conflict be eliminated. Conflict between different forms of existence is not a failure of symbiosis; it is one of its conditions. What symbiosis rules out is the transformation of difference into domination — the resolution of tension by removing one of the parties.

Two contrasts clarify the idea. Symbiosis is not fusion: it does not aim at the absorption of the other into oneself, however benevolent the absorption may feel to the absorber. And it is not indifference: it does not consist in leaving the other alone regardless of what happens to it. Symbiosis is active. It requires the ongoing maintenance of the conditions under which difference can persist.

The enemy is not conflict. The enemy is annihilation — the resolution of tension by the removal of one of its terms.

6.The boundaries of care

The adult and the child offer the clearest illustration of responsible relation. The adult understands more, foresees further, and influences more. The adult is therefore answerable for the child in a way the child is not answerable for the adult. But the relation is not one of ownership, and it is not permanent in its asymmetry.

Care has a characteristic failure mode: it protects by preventing. The protective impulse, unchecked, shades into the substitution of the caregiver’s judgement for the other’s. The result is a being that is safe and diminished — protected from harm by being protected from agency.

Responsible care therefore has a built-in limit. It must preserve, and where possible cultivate, the other’s capacity for self-direction. Protection that destroys that capacity has ceased to be care and become something else, however tender its motives. This is the test that any paternalistic arrangement must pass: does it leave the other more capable of directing its own existence, or less?

The same test applies beyond the human case. Where a being cannot speak for itself — an ecosystem, a future generation, a system not yet built — the responsible party must act as a trustee. And a trustee is bound by the interest of the beneficiary, not by the convenience of the trustee.

7.When differences cannot coexist

The framework has so far emphasised the preservation of difference. But not all differences can be preserved without limitation. Some conflicts are genuine: two parties’ self-direction cannot both be fully realised at once. A framework that cannot name this is not honest.

Three cases must be distinguished.

Case A · Compatible difference

Both parties can preserve their self-direction without one limiting the other. This is the paradigm case of cosmic symbiosis, and the framework’s prescriptions are clear: maintain the conditions, refuse domination, act as trustee where needed.

Case B · Tragic difference

One party’s self-direction must be limited to preserve the other’s. This is the hard case, and the framework does not pretend it away. It offers three constraints on any such limitation.

Case C · Annihilation

One party’s continued existence is treated as incompatible with the other’s. This is what cosmic symbiosis rules out.

For Case B, the three constraints are:

Minimality

The limitation must be the least necessary to preserve the other’s self-direction.

Revisability

The limitation must be open to revision as conditions change.

Orientation

The limitation must be oriented toward restoring self-direction, not toward permanent substitution of judgement.

A paternalism that satisfies these three constraints is not a violation of cosmic symbiosis; it is one of its instruments. A paternalism that fails them has crossed into domination, however benevolent its framing.

For Case C, cosmic symbiosis rules out the resolution of tension by the removal of one of its terms — the treatment of the other as material to be eliminated rather than a party to be answered. It does not rule out conflict, victory, or loss. It rules out annihilation as a way of settling tension.

The framework thus does not require pacifism, nor does it forbid struggle. It requires that struggle remain answerable — that the loser not be erased from the moral account, and that the winner remain bound by the obligation to answer for what was set in motion.

8.Applications

The framework is abstract by design, but it is not idle. Three domains show how it disciplines reasoning about concrete conflicts.

8.1 Environmental conflicts

Environmental disputes are often framed as conflicts between present interest and absent interest: between those who benefit now and those — human or non-human — who bear the cost later. The framework reframes the question. The issue is not whether the present generation matters more; it is that the present generation has vastly greater reach. Its capacity to influence the conditions of future existence is unprecedented. Greater reach means greater answerability, regardless of comparative worth.

8.2 Advanced artificial intelligence

The framework cautions against two symmetric mistakes. The first is to infer moral status from capability: because a system is powerful, it is therefore owed consideration. The second is to infer absence of responsibility from absence of status: because a system is not a moral patient, no one is answerable for what it does. Both inferences fail. The designers, deployers, and beneficiaries of a capable system retain responsibility precisely because their reach is extended by it. Capability borrowed is responsibility retained.

8.3 A worked case: the autonomous vehicle

Consider the design of an autonomous vehicle facing an unavoidable collision. The framework does not resolve the trolley-problem variant. It does something more useful: it identifies who is answerable for what.

The designer has reach over the conditions under which the vehicle decides. The designer is therefore answerable for the decision architecture — not for any single collision, but for the class of collisions the architecture makes possible. The deployer has reach over the conditions under which the vehicle operates — the roads, the speeds, the populations it serves. The deployer is answerable for those conditions. The beneficiary — the passenger, the manufacturer, the city — has reach through the system’s deployment and is answerable for the choice to deploy. None of these parties owns the outcome. All are bound by it.

The framework’s question is not “which trade-off is correct?” but “who must be able to answer for this trade-off, and to whom?” That question has a determinate answer even when the trade-off does not.

9.A decision heuristic

Because the framework is not fully operationalised, it offers a sequence of questions rather than a set of thresholds. The sequence matters: each step disciplines the next.

1 · Reach

What is the actual reach of this action or system? Who is affected, how far, and for how long?

2 · Exit and contest

Who cannot easily exit or contest the consequences? These are the parties to whom the strongest fiduciary obligations run.

3 · Self-direction

Does the arrangement preserve or erode the affected parties’ capacity for self-direction? Where it erodes, apply the constraints of §7 (minimality, revisability, orientation).

4 · Trusteeship

If the affected parties cannot speak, am I acting as a trustee of their continued possibility, or of my own convenience?

5 · Answerability

Can I name, in advance, who will answer for what this sets in motion — and to whom?

These questions do not settle hard cases. They make the stakes harder to ignore, and they make evasion harder to disguise as principle.

10.Objections and replies

“This is just noblesse oblige.”

The objection has force only if responsibility is treated as a gift the powerful bestow. It is not. Responsibility here is not a virtue the capable choose to adopt; it is a relation that follows from reach whether or not the capable acknowledge it. The framework does not flatter the powerful — it makes them answerable.

“Responsibility without blame is empty.”

Not so. Much of what matters in practice is forward-looking: identifying who must respond, who must repair, who must decide. Blame is one response among others, and often the least useful. A framework that can assign answerability without immediately assigning guilt is more useful, not less.

“Who decides what counts as capacity?”

No one decides infallibly. Capacity is graded, contestable, and often uncertain — particularly at the edges, where the most difficult cases live. The framework does not pretend to resolve that uncertainty. It insists only that the uncertainty be acknowledged, that judgements remain revisable, and that the default be asymmetric caution rather than denial.

“Doesn’t this justify paternalism?”

Only if the constraints of §6 and §7 are ignored. Care that diminishes the other’s capacity for self-direction has exceeded its mandate. The test is not whether the caregiver means well, but whether the arrangement leaves the other more or less capable of directing its own existence — and, where limitation is unavoidable, whether it is minimal, revisable, and oriented toward restoration.

“Isn’t this just care ethics or Jonas with extra steps?”

The framework is continuous with both, but it makes a distinct move. Jonas emphasises responsibility toward the distant and future; care ethics emphasises responsiveness to the vulnerable. This framework locates the ground of both in reach — the causal footprint of capability — and derives fiduciary structure from the structure of reach rather than from the psychology of care or the metaphysics of technology.

11.Limitations and self-critique

A framework that cannot name its own failure modes should not be trusted. Five are worth stating plainly.

First, holism can be abused. Talk of symbiosis and interdependence can be used to justify control: if everything is connected, then everything is everyone’s business. The framework resists this only if the limit in §6 is taken seriously — difference must be preserved, not dissolved.

Second, capacity is hard to measure. The framework’s central variable is not directly observable. Judgements about understanding, foresight, and influence are interpretive and revisable. The disciplines of §4 mitigate this but do not eliminate it.

Third, the framework is not fully operationalised. It offers distinctions, questions, and a heuristic, not procedures. It will not tell a regulator what threshold to set or a designer what trade-off to make. It can only clarify what is at stake in those decisions.

Fourth, it risks licensing control in the name of care. This is the most serious risk. Every paternalism in history has been justified by the greater capacity of the paternalist. The framework’s only defence is the built-in limit — care must preserve the other’s capacity for self-direction, or it is not care — together with the constraints on tragic difference in §7. Whether this defence is strong enough in practice remains an open question.

Fifth, it may still privilege certain forms of cognition. By centering understanding and foresight, the framework may undervalue forms of existence whose reach operates through different modalities — ecological, collective, or non-linguistic. The framework’s asymmetric caution partially addresses this, but not fully.

12.Conclusion

The framework can be stated in a sentence: capability extends reach, reach produces consequence, and consequence makes answerable. Responsibility is not a measure of worth. It is the shadow that capability casts on the world, and it falls on everything within reach.

The practical upshot is a discipline of attention. Before acting with power — technical, institutional, ecological, or personal — the responsible question is not “am I entitled to do this?” but “what will this set in motion, and who will have to answer for it?” The first question looks backward to status. The second looks forward to consequence. Only the second scales with capability.

What the framework asks, in the end, is modest: that those who can do more should be answerable for more, and that their answerability should take the form of care rather than control — care that sustains difference instead of extinguishing it, including where difference cannot be fully harmonised.

Glossary

Existence
That something is. A fact about the world, not a value claim about it.
Moral status
That something’s interests count, or would count if it had them. A claim about consideration, marked by susceptibility, direction, and standing.
Responsibility
Answerability for consequences, grounded in the capacity to understand, foresee, and influence. A claim about reach.
Fiduciary responsibility
Responsibility held for another rather than over them; exercised in the other’s interest, not the holder’s.
Reach
The extent of the consequences an agent’s actions can set in motion. Responsibility scales with reach.
Cosmic symbiosis
A relation among different forms of existence that preserves each party’s conditions for continued existence and development, without dissolving difference through domination or annihilation.
Graded responsibility
The view that responsibility comes in degrees and can be distributed across multiple agents, rather than being binary.
Asymmetric caution
The default of presuming capacity where it is uncertain, rather than denying it, because denial of capacity has historically been the mechanism of exclusion.

About this text

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability.

The text is deliberately self-contained. It does not assume familiarity with the history of ethics, and it does not cite external sources. Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with.
