Abstract

This work develops a relational account of responsibility grounded in the concept of reach: the capacity of an agent, institution, or organised system to shape the conditions under which other beings exist, develop, act, and continue.

Its central proposition is that consequential reach generates prima facie answerability. The greater an entity's capacity to understand, foresee, influence, and alter the conditions of others, the greater its responsibility to account for the consequences of that influence.

Responsibility is not treated as a reward for intelligence, a measure of moral superiority, or an automatic entitlement to authority. It is a demand arising from asymmetry. An entity that possesses substantially greater influence over another's circumstances acquires obligations that cannot be dismissed merely because its intentions are benevolent or its actions are technically successful.

The argument proceeds in six explicit steps (§4). It is constrained by an account of the conditions under which reach does not generate such obligations (§5), defended against six rival specifications of answerability (§13), and specified through eight distinct concepts of responsibility (§14), including a forward-looking category — structural responsibility — for cases in which reach cannot be traced to identifiable agents (§15). An institutional model (§28A) specifies the bodies through which answerability can be exercised: review councils, contestation rights, silent-principal advocates, responsibility pools, and reversibility requirements.

The framework introduces The Reflective Surface (§8–11): a conceptual structure through which influence becomes intelligible, evaluable, and answerable. It examines how agents interpret the consequences of their actions, recognise the standing of affected beings, revise their understanding, and remain open to correction.

The ultimate normative orientation is symbiosis (§16–20). Symbiosis does not mean the absence of conflict, the preservation of every existing arrangement, or the unconditional subordination of individuals to a collective whole. It means the preservation of conditions in which different forms of existence can maintain meaningful standing, agency, development, and the possibility of continued coexistence.

The framework addresses moral uncertainty, legitimate authority, self-determination, ecological responsibility, future generations, and the limits of protective intervention. It is defended against three main objections (§50–52), tested against three worked cases (§43–45), and subjected to explicit falsifiability and refutation conditions (Appendix A.5–A.6). Its own failure modes, limiting conditions, and unresolved problems are stated in Appendix A.

Its ambition is not to establish a final moral authority, but to develop a framework through which increasingly influential forms of intelligence and organisation can become increasingly accountable for the worlds they help create.

In brief

What this paper tries to do. It provides a structured way of reasoning about responsibility when many agents, institutions, and time horizons are involved. It separates things that are routinely conflated: causing something, having control over it, having a task, being answerable, being blameworthy, and being liable. It adds an eighth concept — structural responsibility — for cases in which no identifiable agent can be singled out. It specifies the institutional bodies through which answerability can be exercised (§28A). And it states conditions under which a responsibility claim should be limited.

What it is not. Not a complete moral theory. Not a finished decision procedure. Not a verdict on individual cases.

Why it is needed. In practical debates — climate, AI, healthcare, corporate conduct, migration — "responsibility" is used in at least eight distinct senses. This makes disputes undecidable, because the parties are often right in different senses at the same time.

How to use it. Test it against real cases, step by step (Appendix B.1 provides a template). The framework gains credibility by showing both where it works (§43) and where it fails even with its full conceptual apparatus (§44).

The argument in six steps

The whole argument can be summarised as a chain of six claims. Part I §4 defends them in detail; the steps below are the skeleton on which the rest of the text is flesh.

1. Agency is exercised by shaping the conditions under which others act (§4, P1).

2. Where one agent systematically shapes another's conditions, that shaping is systematic conditioning — not incidental causation (§4, P2; §5).

3. Equal standing requires that systematic conditioning either be attributable to the affected party's own agency or be answerable to them (§4, P3–P5).

4. Where attribution fails — because another has played a constitutive role in shaping the conditions — answerability is owed (§4, C).

5. Answerability under asymmetric reach requires the fiduciary structure: principal, fiduciary, loyalty, discretion, accountability (§12–13).

6. Therefore: those with reach are fiduciaries of those whose conditions they shape. Greater capability generates greater obligation, not greater moral worth.

Where reach cannot be traced to identifiable agents, steps 1–6 have no application. For those cases the paper adds an eighth concept — structural responsibility (§15) — which is forward-looking rather than backward-looking, participatory rather than causal, and shared rather than distributed. It fills a gap; it does not displace the six-step argument.

Part I

Foundations: Reach and the Normative Bridge

1.Introduction

Contemporary normative ethics has been organised, to a considerable degree, around questions of rights: what is owed to whom, and on what grounds. This approach has generated substantial progress. Yet it leaves an important question insufficiently developed: what follows when an agent has the capacity to shape the conditions under which others live and act?

The problem becomes especially visible where consequences are produced through complex arrangements rather than by a single identifiable actor. Climate change, autonomous systems, and informational infrastructures are not merely cases in which particular rights are violated. They are also cases in which the capacity to produce consequences has expanded beyond the institutions and practices capable of understanding, distributing, and accounting for those consequences.

This paper's central claim is that responsibility, understood in this context, is not simply a fixed property that a being either possesses or lacks. It is a relation between an agent's capacity to understand, foresee, and influence its surroundings and the consequences that follow from exercising that capacity.

The relation is asymmetric (answerability runs toward those whose conditions are shaped, not over them), graded (its strength varies with the extent, systematicity, and foreseeability of the conditioning), structural (it can hold whether or not the agent acknowledges it), relational (it concerns the connection between an agent's reach and the conditions of others), and fiduciary in form (the answerability that arises under asymmetric reach has the five characteristic features of fiduciary relations; §12).

Methodology. This is a mid-level normative framework in the sense used by Beauchamp and Childress. It does not claim to be a complete moral theory, nor does it claim to settle every particular case. It offers conceptual distinctions, a normative argument, a partial formalisation, and a decision heuristic intended to discipline reasoning where capability and accountability have diverged.

Three methodological commitments guide the argument. First, the framework is coherentist rather than foundationalist: it seeks reflective equilibrium between the structural features of agency, the normative importance of equal standing, and considered judgments about cases. Second, it makes claims that can be challenged: its normative argument is vulnerable to counterexamples in which systematic conditioning appears not to generate answerability — a vulnerability addressed directly in §5. Third, it states its limits: its failure modes and unresolved problems are documented in Appendix A.

Terminology. The paper uses capability rather than intelligence as its central term. Intelligence may contribute to capability, but capability is broader. Institutions, technological systems, collective agents, and ecological processes can shape conditions in ways that matter to the framework without being appropriately described as intelligent in the ordinary cognitive sense. The framework's scope is normative rather than descriptive: it does not claim that agents actually acknowledge their answerability, only that answerability can be owed whether or not it is acknowledged.

2.Reach

The central concept of this framework is reach.

Reach describes the consequential capacity of an agent, institution, or organised system to influence the conditions, possibilities, and development of other beings.

Reach is not equivalent to physical strength, intelligence, wealth, authority, or intention. These may contribute to it, but none independently defines it.

An entity may possess extensive knowledge but little practical influence. Another may possess limited understanding while exercising enormous control over the circumstances of others.

Responsibility must therefore be assessed through actual influence rather than through self-description or assumed moral status.

Reach has six principal dimensions:

Asymmetry

The difference in power, knowledge, resources, or dependence between influencing and affected parties.

Control

The degree to which an entity can initiate, maintain, modify, or terminate consequential conditions.

Persistence

The duration and continuity of its influence.

Magnitude

The depth and seriousness of the possible consequences.

Scope

The number and diversity of beings, systems, and environments affected.

Foreseeability

The extent to which consequences could reasonably be anticipated.

These dimensions are related but not interchangeable. A temporary action may have enormous magnitude. A seemingly minor intervention may become significant through persistence. A system may affect a vast population without exercising direct control over every individual. Reach must therefore be evaluated contextually.

3.Existence as a Relational Condition

Existence does not occur in complete isolation. Every being exists within conditions that precede it, surround it, influence it, and affect its possibilities. These conditions may be biological, ecological, social, technological, or informational. Even an apparently independent entity depends upon relationships that make its continued existence possible.

Existence is therefore not merely a property possessed by an individual. It is also a condition maintained through interactions with an environment and with other forms of existence. This does not mean that every relationship is beneficial. Relationships may be cooperative, indifferent, exploitative, competitive, or destructive. Nevertheless, no sufficiently complex form of existence can be understood entirely apart from the conditions and relationships through which it persists and develops.

To exist is to participate in conditions that influence other existence, while remaining subject to conditions influenced by others.

This creates the possibility of responsibility.

4.The Six-Step Argument

The normative bridge from reach to answerability is not an argument from causation directly to obligation. It has an explicit structure, and one step in that structure — P3 — carries the entire normative weight of the argument. It must therefore be stated with precision.

(P1) Agency is exercised by acting on conditions. To act is to shape the space of possibilities in which one acts and in which others may act. Agency is not merely the production of effects; it can involve determining the conditions under which choices become available, meaningful, or possible.

(P2) Systematic social conditioning. When A's actions systematically shape the conditions under which B exercises agency, B's possibilities are partly determined by A. B's agency is thereby heteronomously conditioned in the relevant domain. Systematic conditioning must be distinguished from incidental causal effects (§5).

(P3) The normative premise — unpacked. Where one agent systematically determines the conditions under which another exercises agency, that determination must either (a) be attributable to the affected agent's own agency, or (b) be answerable to that agent in a form that permits meaningful scrutiny and contestation.

P3 is not a description of what agents in fact demand. It is a normative claim about what equal standing requires. Three independent defences of P3 are available, and the framework's normative strength depends on at least two of them holding:

P3a — Relational autonomy
B's autonomy is not exhausted by non-interference with B's discrete choices. It includes B's standing as a participant in the justification of the conditions under which those choices are made. When A determines those conditions without being answerable to B, A treats B as an object of administration rather than a participant in justification.
P3b — Non-domination
Asymmetry becomes domination when the party with greater power can determine the conditions of the weaker party without being required to justify itself. Answerability is the minimum condition under which asymmetry remains non-dominating.
P3c — Epistemic justice
The party who bears the consequences of a system has a claim to know how the system is shaped and why. Without answerability, that claim is structurally unfulfillable.

These defences are not equivalent, and a critic may reject one without rejecting the others. But they converge on the same conclusion: where attribution to B's own agency fails, answerability is the minimum that equal standing requires.

(P4) The failure of attribution. Where A systematically determines B's conditions, those conditions cannot be attributed entirely to B's own agency. A has played a constitutive role in shaping them.

(P5) The requirement of answerability. If the conditions cannot be attributed entirely to B's agency, equal standing requires that A be able to give an account of the determination to B. Such an account must be capable, in principle, of being received, examined, contested, and revised.

(C) Conclusion. Where A's reach systematically shapes the conditions under which B exercises agency, A is answerable to B for that shaping.

4.1 Why this interpretation of equal standing?

Four alternative interpretations are available. Only the fifth — the one adopted here — survives.

Interpretation Fails because
Non-interference only Too weak: an agent may avoid interfering with any particular decision while still determining the broader conditions under which another makes decisions.
Substantive equality of outcome Too strong: introduces a theory of distributive justice the framework does not seek to establish.
Attribution to one's own agency alone Fails where another agent has substantially shaped the relevant conditions — which is precisely the case the framework addresses.
Fully reciprocal justification Assumes a symmetry that does not exist in relationships of unequal reach.
Minimal answerability under asymmetry (adopted) Requires only that the affected party be able to receive, examine, and contest the account — not that the account be jointly authored.

The adopted interpretation is the weakest normative position that still blocks domination. Stronger positions are available; the framework does not need them, and cannot defend them without a fuller theory of justice.

4.2 Why affected parties have a special claim

The people whose agency is being shaped occupy a distinctive position: they bear the consequences of decisions over which they may have little control. Their claim is not necessarily a claim to control the decision. It is a claim that their position must count in its justification. Being affected establishes a possible interest in the consequences; having interests establishes reasons that may deserve moral consideration; being systematically subject to another's consequential discretion establishes a stronger claim to justification; being deprived of meaningful means of contestation strengthens the need for independent accountability.

4.3 Three features of the argument

First, it is not an argument from causation directly to obligation. P2 concerns systematic conditioning, not mere causal efficacy. Second, the argument is asymmetric. It establishes answerability to those whose conditions are shaped, not authority over them. Third, it is graded. The strength and scope of answerability vary with the extent, persistence, foreseeability, control, and asymmetry of the conditioning (§2).

4.4 Three objections

Objection 1: benign imposition. Parents, teachers, and physicians systematically shape the conditions under which others act. Does the framework imply that these relationships are wrong? Reply: No. The framework distinguishes between being answerable and having acted wrongly. A parent is answerable to the child in a form appropriate to the child's developing capacity; that is a feature of the relationship, not a defect in it. Objection 2: mutual conditioning. It makes each party answerable within the domain of their respective reach. The framework does not imply that every interaction creates unlimited responsibility. Objection 3: institutional mediation. Individual responsibility must track each agent's actual reach, control, and position within the arrangement. Institutions may themselves be answerable (§28, §38).

4.5 Agency and the practice of giving reasons

Sellars and Brandom describe the space of reasons as a practice in which claims, commitments, and actions can be challenged and defended. (P1') Agents capable of acting for reasons participate, to some degree, in practices of giving and receiving reasons. (P2') Such practices involve commitments that can be questioned, defended, and revised. (P3') An agent who exercises reach over another's conditions makes decisions that can become objects of legitimate scrutiny. (C') The practices through which agents give and receive reasons provide a natural setting for exercising answerability. The constitutive argument is important but limited: it establishes that answerability is closely connected to agency and reason-giving; it does not prove the full normative argument, identify every affected party, or establish that every agent will recognise or fulfil its answerability.

4.6 The silent-principal objection

Does the framework require answerability to be demanded before it can be owed? No. The framework treats answerability as a normative relation that may exist whether or not the affected party can articulate or enforce a demand. The inability to demand an account does not automatically diminish the significance of the conditions being shaped. It does, however, make representation more difficult (§35, §28A).

5.The Limits of Reach

Almost every action affects conditions beyond the agent's immediate intentions. If every such influence generated a special relationship of answerability, the concept would become excessively broad. This section specifies when reach does not generate distinctive answerability, and provides a decision-ready threshold.

5.1 Five limiting conditions

Incidental influence. Where influence is incidental, limited, and not reasonably subject to the agent's control, the grounds for special answerability are correspondingly weak. General moral obligations may still apply.

Insufficient foreseeability. An outcome may be connected to an agent's actions without having been reasonably foreseeable. But unforeseeability is not a permanent exemption. As evidence accumulates and systems become better understood, the grounds for answerability may change.

Lack of meaningful control. Responsibility should be proportionate to the actual scope of available action. Lack of total control does not eliminate responsibility for the parts that remain within discretion.

Diffuse interdependence. Participation is not identical to governance. Stronger answerability arises where an agent has a substantial role in determining the conditions under which others must operate.

Beneficial influence and legitimate authority. Not all asymmetry is wrongful. But beneficial intentions do not remove the need for answerability. Legitimate authority is not authority without accountability. It is authority whose exercise can be justified in light of the relationship and its purposes.

5.2 The threshold for distinctive answerability

Distinctive answerability is most strongly established where several conditions converge. The formulation below specifies how the conditions are to be weighted:

Condition Weight Test
Substantial shaping of another's conditions of agency Necessary Does A determine a significant range of B's options?
Persistence — sustained, repeated, or consequential Necessary Is the influence episodic or structural?
Discretion or practical control Necessary Could A have acted otherwise at acceptable cost?
Foreseeability — actual or attainable through information A ought to consider Strong Could A have known?
Low contestability for the affected party Strong Can B refuse, exit, or contest?

Priority rule. The three necessary conditions establish a prima facie claim. The two strong conditions determine its strength: high foreseeability and low contestability together raise the claim to a strong obligation; low foreseeability and high contestability reduce it to a weak one. Magnitude and scope (from §2) raise the urgency of the claim but cannot create it without the necessary conditions.

Uncertainty rule. Where the necessary conditions are plausibly met but the strong conditions are uncertain, the framework imposes a provisional obligation to investigate and account — not a full obligation to act, and not a permission to ignore.

Reversibility rule. Where the potential consequences are irreversible, the threshold for answerability is lowered, and the burden of justification shifts to the agent exercising reach.

These rules do not eliminate judgment. They make the structure of judgment explicit and contestable.

5.3 What this section rules out

The revised threshold rules out three common misapplications:

  • The universal-responsibility error. "Everyone affects everyone" does not generate universal answerability, because the necessary conditions (substantial shaping, persistence, discretion) are not met by incidental participation.
  • The no-responsibility error. "I didn't intend it" does not eliminate answerability, because foreseeability is assessed against information the agent ought to have considered, not against actual intention.
  • The total-control error. "I couldn't control the outcome" does not eliminate answerability for the parts that were within discretion.

6.Answerability, Authority, Blame

Responsibility is not authority. A fundamental distinction must be maintained: greater responsibility does not automatically confer greater authority. An entity may possess greater understanding, predictive capacity, or practical influence without thereby acquiring a legitimate right to determine how others should exist. Responsibility creates obligations of care, restraint, explanation, and correction. Authority requires additional justification. Legitimate authority must consider the standing of those affected, the necessity of the intervention, the proportionality of its scope, the availability of less restrictive alternatives, the possibility of contestation, independent scrutiny, the distribution of decision-making power, and the duration and reversibility of the intervention. An entity cannot establish legitimate authority simply by claiming that it understands the situation better. Superior understanding may increase responsibility precisely because it increases the obligation to recognise the limits of one's own perspective.

Responsibility is not blame. Answerability must also be distinguished from blame. Blame is a judgement concerning fault, culpability, intention, negligence, or other conditions of moral responsibility. Answerability is broader. An entity may have obligations to explain, repair, or prevent harmful consequences even when it is not fully blameworthy for producing them. This is especially important for complex institutions, autonomous systems, and situations involving distributed causation. The absence of malicious intention does not eliminate responsibility. Likewise, the existence of responsibility does not establish that an entity possesses human-like intentions, consciousness, or moral agency. The framework therefore avoids treating answerability as proof of any particular theory of mind.

The emergence of answerability. When an entity has the capacity to shape conditions under which others must live, act, or develop, its influence becomes morally relevant. The entity becomes answerable for how that influence is exercised, what consequences it produces, what risks it creates, and whether affected parties retain meaningful standing. This is the distinction between merely causing an outcome and being answerable for the conditions that make the outcome possible. Answerability does not require perfect knowledge or complete control. An agent may be answerable for risks it should reasonably have anticipated, even when it did not intend the eventual consequences. An institution may remain answerable for structural outcomes that no individual participant intended. Responsibility is therefore not exhausted by intention, nor by direct causation. It concerns the relationship between capacity, influence, foreseeable consequence, and the possibility of response or correction.

7.Responsibility Under Uncertainty

Influence frequently exceeds understanding. An entity may affect systems whose internal processes, long-term responses, or moral significance remain partly unknown. This creates a particular obligation. The less certain an influential entity is about the consequences of its actions, the less justified it is in treating its predictions as conclusive.

Uncertainty does not always require inaction. Inaction may itself preserve or intensify harm. Instead, uncertainty should influence the manner of action. Where possible, action should become more cautious, more transparent, more reversible, more open to independent review, more attentive to affected parties, and more responsive to new information.

The absence of certainty is not permission to act without accountability. It is a reason to strengthen accountability.

Part II

The Reflective Surface

8.The Reflective Surface

The Reflective Surface describes the conceptual structure through which an entity can recognise and examine the consequences of its own influence.

It is not a claim that every entity possesses consciousness or self-awareness. Rather, it identifies the conditions required for meaningful reflection and answerability.

A reflective surface allows influence to become visible to the influencing entity. Without such a structure, an entity may produce consequences without adequately registering the beings, systems, or possibilities affected by its actions.

The reflective surface therefore connects power with recognition. It asks an entity to examine not only what it intends to achieve, but also what its influence makes possible, what it prevents, and what it changes for others.

9.Four Dimensions of Reflection

The reflective surface contains four connected dimensions.

Epistemic access
The capacity to acquire and interpret information about affected beings and systems. This includes recognising uncertainty, identifying missing information, and distinguishing evidence from assumption.
Evaluative access
The capacity to recognise that consequences matter from perspectives other than one's own. This requires considering the standing, interests, vulnerabilities, and possible experiences of affected beings.
Practical access
The capacity to translate recognition into action. An entity that understands a risk but possesses no ability to respond has a different practical responsibility from an entity that can meaningfully prevent or reduce it.
Constitutive access
The capacity to recognise that one's own existence and functioning depend upon conditions and relationships that one does not entirely control. This dimension challenges the assumption that an influential entity is self-sufficient or entitled to treat its own continuation as the only relevant objective.

Together, these dimensions connect knowledge, evaluation, action, and relational dependence.

10.Reflection as a Source of Accountability

Reflection is insufficient if it merely confirms existing beliefs. A genuinely reflective system must be capable of encountering information that challenges its assumptions. It must allow affected parties, independent observers, and new evidence to alter its conclusions. A system that only accepts information consistent with its existing objectives does not adequately examine its own influence.

Reflection therefore requires openness to disagreement, recognition of uncertainty, independent evaluation, the possibility of revising decisions, documentation of consequential reasoning, and mechanisms for correction and repair.

The reflective surface must not become a mechanism through which an entity simply justifies its own conduct. Its purpose is to make self-justification contestable.

Answerability as a condition of responsible agency. An entity capable of consequential action but incapable of accounting for its influence presents a fundamental ethical problem. Its actions may remain effective, but their legitimacy cannot be established through effectiveness alone. Answerability therefore becomes a condition of responsible agency. The more extensive an entity's reach, the more developed its capacity for reflection, explanation, correction, and accountability must become. This is particularly relevant to increasingly autonomous and influential technological systems. However, the framework does not assume that technical systems independently possess moral responsibility. Responsibility may remain distributed among designers, operators, institutions, owners, and other human or organisational actors. Where a system exercises consequential autonomy, the arrangements governing its creation and deployment must ensure that its influence remains answerable.

11.The Limits of Reflection

No reflective surface provides a complete view of existence. Every perspective is limited by available information, conceptual assumptions, interpretative capacity, and the position from which the world is observed. An entity may sincerely attempt to understand another being while fundamentally misunderstanding its needs or interests.

This limitation must remain part of the framework. Reflection cannot establish moral correctness merely by being present. It must remain open to correction from outside itself. The more powerful an entity becomes, the more dangerous it is for its reflective processes to become closed, self-confirming, or insulated from disagreement.

Part III

The Fiduciary Structure and Its Necessity

12.Why Responsibility Is Fiduciary

(P1, from §4) A's systematic reach over B's conditions generates answerability to B.

(P2, structure of agency) B's conditions include the conditions under which B pursues interests and exercises agency.

(P3, from P1 + P2) A is answerable to B for the effects of A's reach on those interests and conditions.

(P4, the prohibition on self-serving substitution) If A were free to disregard B's interests and substitute A's own interests within the very domain in which A exercises reach, then answerability to B would become largely nominal.

(P5, the positive requirement) Answerability therefore requires more than the absence of deliberate self-interest. A must take B's interests and capacity for self-direction seriously when exercising the reach that shapes B's conditions.

(C, fiduciary structure) Under conditions of asymmetric reach, answerability takes on five characteristic features:

Principal
B, whose conditions and interests are affected.
Fiduciary
A, whose reach creates the relevant responsibility.
Loyalty
A must not use the relationship merely to advance A's own interests at B's expense.
Discretion
A may have to make decisions that B cannot specify fully in advance.
Accountability
A must be able to explain and justify the exercise of that discretion to B.

The circularity objection. Does the derivation presuppose what it seeks to prove? No. The argument proceeds in stages: (i) equal standing supports answerability; (ii) answerability requires explainability; (iii) where conditions affect another's interests under asymmetric reach, answerability requires constraints on discretion; (iv) those constraints resemble the characteristic features of fiduciary relations. Giving an account is not itself identical to a fiduciary relationship. The fiduciary structure is a proposed specification of what answerability requires under conditions of asymmetric reach.

A consequence worth stating. Greater capability does not generate greater moral worth. A being with vast capability is not, for that reason, worth more than a being with modest capability. It is capable of affecting more, and therefore may have more extensive obligations. Its responsibility is greater because its reach is greater, not because its interests count for more.

13.The Necessity Argument: Six Rivals

If the fiduciary structure is merely one possible specification of answerability among several equally adequate ones, then the derivation in §12 establishes at most that it is available, not that it is required. This section argues that the five requirements cannot be jointly satisfied by any specification that lacks the fiduciary structure. It does not argue that the fiduciary structure is the only logically possible specification; it argues that it is the minimal complete one.

13.1 The requirements restated

  • (R1) A principal whose conditions and interests are affected.
  • (R2) Loyalty — a prohibition on substituting the agent's own interests for the principal's.
  • (R3) Discretion — the agent must act within a range the principal cannot fully specify.
  • (R4) Accountability — the agent must explain and justify the exercise of discretion to the principal.
  • (R5) Positive orientation — the agent must take the principal's interests and capacity for self-direction seriously.

13.2 Six rivals considered

Stewardship

Locates accountability upward — to an owner, sovereign, community. Fails R4. A steward who is also accountable to the principal has become a fiduciary.

Duty of care

Negative and episodic. Fails R5 (no positive orientation) and R3 (no ongoing discretion). When expanded to include positive orientation, becomes fiduciary.

Non-domination

Specifies what must be avoided, not what must be done. Fails R5 and R2. Adding positive orientation and loyalty produces the fiduciary structure.

Care ethics

Grounds in responsiveness and affect. Fails institutional application and R3. Supplemented structurally, becomes the fiduciary structure.

Kantian justification

Justifies to an idealized rational agent, not the specific affected party. Fails R1 and R5. Specified to the actual party, becomes fiduciary.

Virtue ethics

Specifies what a good agent is like, not relationships. Fails R1 and R4. Can complement but cannot replace the structural account.

13.3 The pattern

Each rival fails at least one requirement outright, or meets all requirements only by adding the features that constitute the fiduciary structure. The fiduciary structure is therefore not one specification among many equally adequate ones; it is the minimal complete specification that satisfies all five requirements simultaneously.

This is not a claim that the rivals are worthless. Each captures something important. What the framework denies is that any of them, taken alone, is an adequate specification of answerability under asymmetric reach. Each is a partial specification; the fiduciary structure is the minimal complete one.

13.4 Three limits

First, the argument establishes that the fiduciary structure is the minimal complete specification, not that it is the only possible one. A more complex structure meeting all five requirements would not be ruled out. Second, the argument depends on R1–R5 being the right requirements. If a critic rejects one, the argument fails for that critic; the framework's response is that R1–R5 are the minimal set that any adequate specification of answerability under asymmetry must contain, and that a critic who rejects one must show either that it is not required or that it is derivable from the others. Third, the argument is a comparative claim about rival traditions as they are standardly formulated. A version of stewardship, duty of care, or non-domination that already includes the features the framework claims they lack is either already a fiduciary structure under another name, or a specification the framework should incorporate.

A fourth limit. The argument does not establish that the fiduciary structure is sufficient for legitimate exercise of reach. It establishes only that it is necessary — that any adequate specification must contain it. Sufficiency requires the institutional conditions of §28A, the contestation rights of §24–26, and the corrective mechanisms of §49. The fiduciary structure is the normative core; it is not the whole.

The fiduciary structure is not merely available as a specification of answerability. It is the minimal complete specification — the structure that any adequate specification must at least contain.

Part IV

Eight Concepts of Responsibility

14.The Eight Concepts

The language of responsibility often combines several different ideas. An agent may cause an outcome without being blameworthy. It may owe an explanation without having acted wrongly. It may possess a duty to repair without having been the sole cause of the harm. The framework therefore distinguishes eight concepts. Each carries its own criteria, and each can be applied without the others.

Concept Definition Test question Typical misuse
Causal responsibility Contribution to producing an outcome. Would the outcome have differed without the contribution? Treating causation as sufficient for blame
Control responsibility Practical capacity to initiate, modify, prevent, or reverse relevant conditions. Could the agent have done otherwise, given its position? Treating formal authority as practical control
Responsibility (as task) Obligations attached to a position, action, capacity, or relationship. Who has the task of handling this? Treating a task as personal culpability
Answerability Obligation to provide reasons, justify decisions, and remain open to revision. To whom is an account owed, and by whom? Treating answerability as blame
Accountability Institutional or social arrangements through which answerability is examined and enforced. Is there a forum, procedure, or addressee for the account? Treating the relation as sufficient without a practice
Culpability Blameworthiness in light of knowledge, intentions, alternatives, and opportunities. Is the agent sufficiently free, informed, and capable to be blamed? Treating structural participation as culpability
Reparative responsibility Obligations to prevent, correct, or remedy adverse outcomes. Is there a basis for repair, independent of blame? Treating repair as admission of fault
Structural responsibility Forward-looking obligation to contribute to reform of a structural process in which one participates. Does the structure systematically shape conditions, and does the agent have capacity to contribute to reform? Treating structural obligation as liability
Rule of application. No responsibility ascription is complete until it specifies which of the eight concepts is being claimed. A claim that conflates two of them is, in the framework's terms, undecidable. This is the single most practically useful output of the paper.

Three worked applications of the rule:

  • "The clinician is responsible for the patient's death." Undecidable as stated. Causal? Perhaps. Control? Bedside, yes; capacity, no. Task? Yes. Culpability? Only if guideline-following was negligent. Answerability? To the patient's family and professional body. The framework requires the speaker to specify.
  • "The state is responsible for the refugee crisis." Undecidable. Causal? Partly, through foreign policy. Control? Partial. Culpability? Weakened by collective decision-making. Structural? Yes. Answerability? To citizens, weakly to affected states. The framework separates these.
  • "The AI system is responsible for the biased hiring outcome." Undecidable. Causal? Yes. Control? No — the system has no practical control in the relevant sense. Task? Yes, distributed across designers, deployers, and beneficiaries. Culpability? No. Structural? Yes, if the bias is systemic. The framework relocates responsibility to the arrangement.

Answerability as relation and as practice. Relation: A is answerable to B. Practice: something exercised through institutions, procedures, explanations, hearings, review, and opportunities for contestation. The relation can exist without an adequate practice — which is why §28A specifies institutional conditions. Institutions can participate in answerability without possessing individual agency in the same sense as persons. And the distinction clarifies the framework's limits: it specifies what answerability requires, not the complete institutional design for exercising it.

Capability borrowed is responsibility retained. A system may produce consequential outcomes without possessing the intentions or understanding required for ordinary moral blame. Responsibility must therefore be examined across the wider arrangement of designers, operators, institutions, and decision-makers. The existence of a system does not automatically eliminate the responsibility of those capable of governing its use.

15.Structural Responsibility

The seven preceding concepts assume that responsibility can be located in an identifiable agent or in an institution with identifiable decision procedures. Where it cannot — where reach is distributed across many agents, none of whom has a determinable share, and there is no addressee for answerability — the framework lacks a category. §44 documents this directly. This section proposes the missing category.

Structural responsibility
The forward-looking obligation to contribute to the reform or mitigation of a structural process in which one participates, where that process systematically shapes the conditions of agency of others in ways that cannot be traced to identifiable individual contributions.

15.1 Five features

  1. Forward-looking, not backward-looking. The question is what should be done about this structure?, not who caused this?
  2. Participatory, not causal. It applies to those who sustain or benefit from the structure, whether or not their contribution is causally traceable.
  3. Shared, not distributed. It cannot be portioned out. It is held jointly, with each participant having an obligation to contribute to reform proportioned to capacity.
  4. Does not generate culpability. Participants in structural responsibility are not blameworthy for the structure.
  5. Generates answerability only at decision points. Where the structure has identifiable decision-makers, those decision-makers become answerable in the ordinary way (§12). Where it does not, structural responsibility is an obligation without a specific addressee — which is exactly what the relation/practice distinction predicts a normative relation can be.

15.2 Five conditions for application

  1. The structural process must systematically shape conditions of agency.
  2. Participation must be non-trivial — the agent must sustain or benefit from the structure.
  3. The structure must be identifiable as a structure — there must be some way of describing it that is not a mere aggregate of individual actions.
  4. The agent must have capacity to contribute to reform.
  5. No ordinary reach-based answerability may already cover the case. Structural responsibility fills a gap; it does not displace the seven other concepts.

15.3 Three limiting conditions

  1. The category must not be used to obscure ordinary reach-based responsibility.
  2. It must not be used to attribute blame.
  3. It must not be used to demand unlimited forward-looking obligation — the obligation is to contribute to reform, proportioned to capacity.

15.4 Relation to Young

Young's (2011) social-connection model is the primary source for this category. Her central claim is that responsibility for structural injustice is not about blame or liability but about participation. The framework's structural responsibility belongs to the same family. This has led to the obvious objection — that §15 is Young's model under another name — and the objection deserves a direct answer.

The objection. Structural responsibility, as defined above, adds nothing to Young's social-connection model. All five features and all five conditions are already present in Young. Calling it an "eighth category" is terminological repackaging, not a new contribution.

Reply, first layer: what Young does not do. Young's model is a free-standing political theory of responsibility for structural injustice. It stands alone: it replaces the liability model for a certain class of cases and claims to be the correct understanding of those cases. §15 is not free-standing. It is one of eight categories, with an explicit transition rule (§29) and an explicit relation to the seven other concepts of §14. A free-standing model must carry the whole analysis itself, whereas a situated category need only carry the part of the analysis the other seven do not cover.

This is the weakest of the three replies, and it should be said plainly. A critic may reply that architectural situation is a property of this paper, not of the category — Young's model could be situated in the same way. That reply is partly correct, which is why a second layer is needed.

Reply, second layer: what Young cannot do. Young's model says nothing about what happens when the structure has identifiable decision points. She is not uninterested in the question, but her model is not built to answer it — it is built to explain why participation in structures generates responsibility even when no individual is at fault. Feature 5 of §15 does something Young's model does not: it ties structural responsibility to the framework's account of answerability. Where the structure has decision points, decision-makers become answerable in the ordinary way (§12). Where it does not, structural responsibility is an obligation without a specific addressee — which the relation/practice distinction has already predicted a normative relation can be.

Reply, third layer: compatibility, not competition. §15 is not formulated as a competing model to Young. It is formulated as a complement: a situating of what Young has already shown, within a framework that needs to distinguish eight different concepts. If Young is right — and she probably is — then what she says about structural injustice is correct. §15 does not add a new theory of injustice. It adds a place in a framework where Young's insight can coexist with reach-based responsibility without either swallowing the other.

Worked example. Consider a global supply chain in which a retailer's purchasing practices contribute to labour abuses at a tier-two supplier. No single purchaser's order is causally sufficient; the structure of price pressure, delivery deadlines, and contractual fragmentation produces the outcome. Under the seven concepts, the retailer is causally responsible in part, has control through market leverage, has a task of due diligence, is answerable to consumers and investors, is not culpable if it lacked knowledge, and may have reparative obligations. But no concept captures the obligation that arises simply from participation in the structure. Structural responsibility does: the retailer has a forward-looking obligation to contribute to reform of the purchasing structure, proportioned to its capacity. The obligation is not a liability; it is not a blame; it is not a determinate share. It is an obligation to act on the structure one sustains.

What falls if the critic is right. If a critic still holds that §15 adds nothing to Young, what is lost? §44's recovery falls in part: if §15 is Young under another name, migration remains a case in which the framework fails with its own categories. §29 stands: the transition rule — §28 applies when agents are identifiable, §15 when they are not — is independent of whether §15 is original. And the §14 table stands: that structural responsibility is one of eight concepts with its own test question is an operational point, not a theoretical one. The defence of §15 is therefore partly independent of whether the category is original. A critic who is right that it is Young is wrong that it is redundant.

Verification required. The characterisation of Young here is based on the standard reading of Responsibility for Justice (2011), not on a fresh reading of the primary source. Four claims in particular require verification: (i) that Young grounds responsibility in participation in structural processes; (ii) that her model is free-standing in the sense claimed above; (iii) that she does not make the answerability connection attributed to §15; (iv) that "social connection" is her own term. Claim (iii) is the most vulnerable, because it is a claim about the absence of an argument, and absence is harder to establish than presence.

Part V

Symbiosis as the Normative Orientation

16.The Meaning of Symbiosis

Symbiosis is the central normative orientation of this framework.

It describes the preservation and development of relationships through which different forms of existence can maintain meaningful standing and possibilities for continued development.

Symbiosis does not require identical interests. It does not require the elimination of disagreement, competition, or independence. Nor does it require every relationship to remain unchanged. A symbiotic relationship may require substantial transformation when an existing arrangement is exploitative or destructive. The essential concern is whether the conditions of coexistence permit meaningful continuation without unjustified domination or destruction.

Symbiosis is not the demand that everything become the same. It is the possibility that difference can continue to exist without being reduced to the will of the more powerful.

Symbiosis is not fusion. It does not aim at absorbing the other into oneself, even where the absorption is described as benevolent. Symbiosis is not indifference. It does not mean leaving the other alone regardless of the consequences.

17.Symbiosis and Individual Standing

The collective whole matters, but individuals cannot automatically be sacrificed in its name. An appeal to ecological stability, social progress, technological development, or collective welfare cannot independently justify the destruction of individual standing.

The framework therefore rejects simple majoritarian reasoning as a complete moral theory. A majority does not acquire unlimited moral authority over a minority merely because it represents a greater number. Equally, individual interests cannot always override the conditions necessary for the continued existence of others. The challenge is to establish defensible priorities when legitimate interests conflict.

Symbiosis requires attention to both the integrity of individuals and the conditions that sustain the wider whole.

18.Symbiosis and the Wider Whole

The whole is not merely the sum of its currently visible members. It includes ecological relationships, future generations, dependencies, and conditions that make continued existence possible. Some harms may not be immediately experienced by identifiable individuals but may nevertheless undermine the conditions upon which many forms of life depend.

The framework therefore recognises ecological integrity and intergenerational responsibility as morally significant. However, the priority of the whole must not become an unrestricted justification for suppressing individuals. The correct orientation is to preserve the wider conditions of existence while minimising harm to particular beings. Where irreversible ecological damage is plausible, a stronger presumption of caution is justified.

19.Symbiosis Is Not Mere Stability

A stable relationship is not necessarily a just relationship. A system of domination may remain stable for a long time. A dependent population may appear peaceful because meaningful disagreement is impossible. A technological system may preserve its own operations while reducing the independence of those who depend upon it.

Stability alone therefore cannot establish symbiosis. A relationship must also be examined through the distribution of power, opportunities for development, freedom to disagree, and the possibility of changing its conditions. Symbiosis requires more than continued existence. It requires the preservation of meaningful possibilities.

20.Symbiosis as an Ongoing Process

Symbiosis is not a final condition that can be permanently achieved. Relationships change. New forms of intelligence may emerge. Existing ecosystems may transform. Institutions may become obsolete. Previously unknown interests may become visible.

A framework that assumes its own conclusions are permanent risks becoming a source of domination. Symbiosis must therefore remain dynamic. Its principles should be stable enough to constrain destructive influence, yet sufficiently revisable to respond to new understanding.

The objective is not to freeze existence into a preferred arrangement. It is to preserve the possibility of responsible development.

Part VI

Moral Standing and Uncertainty

21.Moral Standing

The framework distinguishes between the capacity to exercise influence and the capacity to possess moral standing. These are not identical. An entity may exercise enormous influence without possessing experiences or interests of its own. Conversely, a vulnerable being may possess significant moral standing while exercising almost no influence.

Moral standing must therefore not be measured by power, intelligence, productivity, language, or usefulness to others. The absence of a familiar form of communication does not establish the absence of morally relevant interests.

22.Moral Uncertainty

The moral status of some beings may be difficult to establish. This is particularly relevant when considering unfamiliar biological life, artificial systems, future forms of intelligence, and entities whose experiences cannot be directly accessed.

The framework rejects two premature conclusions:

  1. That an unfamiliar entity must possess moral standing because it appears intelligent.
  2. That an unfamiliar entity cannot possess moral standing because its internal experience is uncertain.

Uncertainty should instead influence the degree of precaution. Where credible grounds exist for believing that an entity may possess morally relevant interests, those possibilities should not be dismissed merely because they are difficult to verify. Precaution does not require treating every hypothetical possibility as established fact. It requires avoiding unjustified certainty when the potential consequences are serious.

23.A Graduated Approach

Moral uncertainty should be examined through several questions:

  • Is there credible evidence of experience, interests, or vulnerability?
  • Can the entity be harmed in a morally relevant sense?
  • Does it exhibit preferences, continuity, or goal-directed behaviour?
  • Is there evidence of dependence or the capacity to develop?
  • How serious would the consequences be if its moral standing were underestimated?
  • How reversible would the proposed intervention be?

The answers may justify different degrees of protection. Where uncertainty is high and potential harm is irreversible, stronger safeguards may be warranted. Where evidence is weak and intervention would impose substantial costs on established moral subjects, caution must also consider those consequences.

The objective is not to eliminate uncertainty through assumption. It is to make decisions that remain defensible despite uncertainty.

Part VII

Freedom, Protection, and Distributed Authority

24.Protection Without Captivity

Protection is one of the most important expressions of responsibility. It is also one of the easiest to misuse. An influential entity may claim that restricting another being's freedom is necessary for its protection. Sometimes intervention is justified. However, protection becomes domination when it permanently removes the agency of those it claims to protect without sufficient justification. A protected being should not automatically become a possession of its protector.

The framework therefore distinguishes protection from harm, prevention of foreseeable catastrophe, temporary restriction, permanent control, and preservation of self-determination. These are different moral actions and require different levels of justification.

Five constraints on legitimate protective action:

  1. Proportionality. The intervention must be proportionate to the seriousness of the risk.
  2. Minimality. It must be limited to what is necessary.
  3. Scrutiny. It must be open to independent review.
  4. Revisability. It must be reconsidered when circumstances change.
  5. Orientation. It must seek to preserve or restore self-direction wherever possible.

Protection that persistently fails these constraints risks becoming domination, regardless of how benevolent its stated purpose may be.

25.Self-Determination

Self-determination concerns the ability of beings and communities to participate meaningfully in shaping their own conditions. It includes the possibility of disagreement, refusal, independent development, and the revision of existing relationships.

Self-determination does not imply that every decision must be respected regardless of its consequences for others. Nor does it imply that every individual has unlimited authority over shared conditions. It does establish a strong presumption against unnecessary interference. The greater the restriction imposed upon another being, the stronger the justification required.

26.Threshold for Intervention

Intervention may become justified when there is credible evidence of serious harm, exploitation, or catastrophic risk. However, intervention should not be based solely on the assertion that a more powerful entity knows what is best. A legitimate intervention must satisfy several conditions:

  1. Necessity: There is a defensible reason why intervention is required.
  2. Proportionality: The intervention is not more restrictive than necessary.
  3. Evidence: The risk is supported by relevant information.
  4. Alternatives: Less restrictive options have been considered.
  5. Reversibility: Temporary and reversible measures are preferred where possible.
  6. Review: Decisions remain open to independent scrutiny.
  7. Restoration: Restrictions should be removed when their justification no longer exists.

The burden of justification increases with the degree of interference.

Catastrophic risk. A credible threat of catastrophic harm may require exceptional intervention. However, the language of catastrophe is vulnerable to abuse. Exceptional intervention should require exceptionally severe threatened harm, credible supporting evidence, sufficient urgency to make ordinary procedures inadequate, examination of less restrictive alternatives, proportionality, limits on duration and scope wherever possible, and independent review as soon as circumstances permit. No individual, institution, government, or artificial intelligence should possess unquestionable authority to determine that catastrophic intervention is necessary. The greater the power exercised, the stronger the requirement for accountability beyond the decision-maker's own judgement.

27.Authority Under Asymmetric Influence

Power asymmetry creates special obligations. An entity with extensive influence may be able to shape the choices available to others without formally controlling them. It may determine what information is available, which alternatives are practical, and which forms of disagreement are possible. Such influence can create domination even without explicit coercion.

Legitimate authority must therefore be evaluated through actual conditions rather than formal declarations of consent. A relationship cannot be considered voluntary merely because the weaker party has technically agreed to it. Meaningful consent requires sufficient information, realistic alternatives, and the possibility of contesting the relationship.

28.Distributed Authority

No single entity should automatically become the final interpreter of the interests of all others. This is particularly important when decisions concern existential risks, ecological integrity, future generations, or highly unequal distributions of power.

Authority should be distributed where possible. Independent representatives, affected communities, scientific institutions, and appropriate oversight mechanisms may contribute different forms of knowledge and legitimacy. The purpose is not to make every decision through endless negotiation. It is to prevent one perspective from becoming immune to challenge. An entity may possess the greatest technical understanding while lacking the legitimacy to make a particular decision alone.

Distributed responsibility among identifiable agents. Where an outcome is produced by multiple agents, a distribution of answerability is justifiable only if each agent's share is sensitive to that agent's reach profile across the six dimensions (§2). The six dimensions can be represented as a profile: r_i = (m_i, d_i, s_i, f_i, c_i, a_i), where each value is assessed on a normalised scale from 0 to 1. A weighted representation can be used to compare reach profiles: q_i = Σ w_k · r_ik. Where a proportional allocation is appropriate, a provisional share can be represented as σ_i = q_i / Σ q_j. This is not a universal formula for moral or legal responsibility. It is a way to make assumptions visible.

Institutions as loci of answerability. An institution is not merely an aggregate of individual agents. It is a structured arrangement of roles, procedures, information flows, and decision points. When such an arrangement systematically shapes the conditions of others, the institution itself can be treated as a locus of answerability — not because it has intentions or experiences, but because its structure determines the conditions under which individuals act. The conditions of this claim are strict. An institution can be a locus of answerability only if it has identifiable decision procedures, can give an account of those procedures through identifiable spokespersons or records, and can revise those procedures in response to contestation. Where these conditions fail, the framework's account of institutional answerability is incomplete.

28A.Institutional Design

A normative framework that specifies what answerability requires but not through what institutions it is exercised remains incomplete. This section specifies the institutional conditions under which the fiduciary structure of §12–13 can be realised. The conditions are stated as design requirements, not as a single blueprint; different domains will require different implementations.

28A.1 Five institutional requirements

(1) Review councils. Independent bodies with the authority to require reasons, examine documentation, and publish findings on how an agent shapes the conditions of others. Composition: affected-party representatives, independent experts, and rotating public members. Powers: subpoena of records, public reporting, referral to enforcement bodies where they exist. Limitation: review councils do not themselves exercise reach; they examine the exercise of reach by others.

(2) Contestation rights. Affected parties must have a procedural right to contest the conditions being shaped, to receive a reasoned response, and to have that response reviewed. Contestation is not veto; it is the right to be heard and to receive reasons. Without it, answerability becomes a monologue.

(3) Silent-principal advocates. Future generations, ecosystems, and marginalised groups who cannot speak in their own institutional voice must have advocates with standing to raise cases, request review, and demand reasons — but not to consent on the principal's behalf. The advocate's mandate is to make the silent principal visible, not to represent its will. Three disciplines constrain the advocate: revisability (interpretations must be open to revision as evidence changes), contestation (the advocate's interpretation must be open to criticism from those who can speak from relevant positions), and self-direction (the advocate must preserve the principal's future capacity for agency, not substitute their own).

(4) Responsibility pools. Where structural responsibility applies (§15) and no identifiable agent can be singled out, a pool of participating agents can be held collectively responsible for contributing to reform, with contributions proportioned to capacity and benefit. The pool does not assign blame; it assigns forward-looking obligation.

(5) Reversibility requirements. Where uncertainty is substantial and consequences potentially irreversible, the agent exercising reach must choose the least invasive and most reversible alternative available, document the choice, and submit to periodic review. Reversibility is not an absolute requirement — emergencies may demand irreversible action — but the burden of justification shifts heavily toward the agent who chooses irreversibility.

28A.2 Two cross-cutting principles

Public accountability. Large-scale agents — states, corporations, AI developers, international bodies — should be required to publish annual accounts of how their reach has shaped the conditions of affected parties, what risks have been created, and what corrective measures have been taken. The account is not a compliance document; it is the material basis for contestation.

Distributed authority. No single entity should be the final interpreter of its own answerability. Review councils, contestation rights, and independent advocates distribute the authority to assess reach across multiple bodies, none of which can unilaterally determine the outcome.

28A.3 What this section does not claim

It does not claim that these institutions are sufficient for legitimate exercise of reach. It does not claim that they are appropriate in all domains. It does not claim that they eliminate the risk of domination — institutions can themselves become instruments of domination, and must be subject to the same contestation they impose on others. It claims only that without institutions of this kind, the fiduciary structure of §12–13 remains a normative relation without a practice — and the relation/practice distinction (§14) predicts that such a relation can exist, but cannot be exercised.

The formalisation summarised in §28 produces determinate, testable, and contestable outputs. That is what a partial formalisation is supposed to do.

How structural responsibility extends distributed responsibility. §28 concerns cases in which multiple identifiable agents contribute to an outcome, and in which the framework's task is to distribute answerability among them. §15 introduces a different case: where the relevant reach cannot be distributed because it cannot be traced, but where participation in a structure nonetheless generates obligation. The consequence for practice is that a framework user should ask, at step 3 of the template (Appendix B.1), whether causal contribution can be identified at all. If it can, §28 applies. If it cannot, §15 applies. The two answers will often coexist in the same case: identifiable decision-makers may be answerable in the ordinary way while the wider structure in which they operate is a matter of structural responsibility.

Part VIII

Conflict, Priority, and Minimum Harm

30.The Reality of Moral Conflict

Symbiosis does not guarantee that all interests can be reconciled. Some conflicts involve genuinely incompatible outcomes. Protecting one form of existence may impose costs upon another. Ecological preservation may conflict with immediate human interests. Individual freedom may conflict with collective safety. Present needs may conflict with the possibilities of future generations.

A credible framework must acknowledge these conflicts rather than assume that benevolence will dissolve them. The objective is to establish principles for addressing conflicts without granting unrestricted power to whichever entity claims the greatest understanding.

31.A Priority Structure

When interests conflict, the framework proposes the following order of consideration.

  1. Prevent existential destruction and irreversible systemic collapse. The continued possibility of existence must be protected against credible catastrophic threats.
  2. Protect basic moral standing. No being should be treated as disposable merely because its interests are numerically smaller or politically weaker.
  3. Preserve agency and self-determination. Where intervention is necessary, restrictions should be limited to what can be justified.
  4. Minimise avoidable harm. When no option is harmless, the least destructive defensible option should be preferred.
  5. Preserve future possibilities. Decisions should avoid unnecessarily closing pathways for future development, recovery, and coexistence.
  6. Maintain accountability and correction. Every consequential decision should remain open to examination and revision where circumstances permit.

This is a priority structure rather than a mechanical formula. It cannot remove the need for judgement. Its purpose is to make the reasons behind difficult decisions explicit and contestable.

32.The Minimum-Harm Principle

When conflicting interests cannot be reconciled, the framework requires the minimum defensible harm. This does not mean minimising an abstract numerical measure at any cost. It requires consideration of severity, duration, reversibility, distribution, vulnerability, the existence of alternatives, and the long-term consequences for coexistence.

A decision that produces a small benefit for many while imposing irreversible destruction upon a vulnerable minority requires particularly strong justification. The framework rejects the assumption that aggregate benefit automatically legitimises concentrated suffering.

Tragic responsibility. Not every conflict admits of a solution in which all legitimate interests are preserved. Some decisions involve tragic loss: a situation in which every available option carries morally significant costs. Where no option avoids serious loss, responsible action requires more than selecting a preferred outcome. It requires acknowledging the costs imposed, explaining why alternatives were insufficient, limiting avoidable harm, and accepting accountability for the decision.

Irreversibility. Irreversible decisions deserve particular scrutiny. Where two options offer comparable protection, preference should ordinarily be given to the option that preserves the greatest possibility of correction. Reversibility is not an absolute requirement. Some emergencies may demand irreversible action. Such action requires stronger justification.

33.Responsible Disagreement

Disagreement is not necessarily a failure of symbiosis. It may be evidence that different perspectives are being taken seriously. A system that eliminates disagreement may also eliminate information necessary for its own correction.

The framework therefore recognises a principle of responsible disagreement: an entity must not treat the existence of disagreement as sufficient evidence that the opposing position should be suppressed. Disagreement should remain possible unless there is a sufficiently serious and demonstrable reason to restrict particular conduct.

The distinction between disagreement and harmful action is essential. The freedom to challenge authority must not be confused with an unlimited right to impose harm.

Part IX

Time, Ecology, and Future Existence

34.Intergenerational Responsibility

Reach extends through time. Decisions made in the present may determine the conditions under which future beings can exist. Future generations cannot fully participate in present decision-making, yet they may inherit its consequences. Their absence from current institutions does not make their interests irrelevant.

Responsibility therefore includes preserving meaningful possibilities for those who cannot yet represent themselves. This does not mean that the present must be subordinated to every hypothetical future possibility. It means that present influence should not unnecessarily destroy the conditions of future agency.

35.Representation of the Future

Future generations require forms of representation that do not depend entirely upon the immediate interests of present decision-makers. Possible mechanisms include independent future-generation representatives, long-term impact assessments, transparent documentation of irreversible decisions, ecological and scientific oversight, and periodic reassessment of long-term commitments.

Such mechanisms cannot perfectly represent people who do not yet exist. Their purpose is more limited: to make the future visible within present decisions.

The silent-principal problem. Future generations cannot directly express their interests. Ecosystems cannot provide conventional consent. Possible artificial agents may present unfamiliar forms of agency or experience. The absence of direct participation does not make their interests or value irrelevant. Three disciplines are necessary: revisability (the interpretation must be open to revision as evidence changes), contestation (it must be open to criticism by those who can speak from relevant positions), and self-direction (it must be oriented toward preserving the principal's future capacity for agency). These disciplines do not eliminate the problem. They establish minimum conditions for responsible representation.

36.Ecological Responsibility

Human and technological existence depends upon ecological systems that cannot be reduced to economic resources or instruments of convenience. Ecosystems sustain relationships among forms of life, including many whose contributions are not immediately visible to human interests.

The framework therefore recognises ecological integrity as an important dimension of symbiosis. Nature should not be represented solely by those who benefit economically from its exploitation. Independent ecological representation is necessary where consequential decisions threaten irreversible damage.

A credible possibility of severe and irreversible ecological harm should create a strong presumption for temporary restraint and independent review. This does not establish an absolute ecological veto in every circumstance. It establishes that the burden of justification must be especially demanding when the conditions of wider existence are at risk.

37.Preserved Possibility

The future is not valuable only because of what can be predicted about it. It is also valuable because it contains possibilities that present understanding cannot fully identify. Irreversible decisions may eliminate forms of development that no current decision-maker knows how to recognise.

Where uncertainty is substantial, and where the consequences of an action may irreversibly eliminate important possibilities for future existence, the preservation of options should carry significant moral weight.

This principle does not prohibit change. It distinguishes responsible development from unnecessary foreclosure.

Part X

Institutional and Technological Responsibility

38.Structural Reach

Influence is not always exercised by a single identifiable individual. Institutions, markets, governments, infrastructures, and technological systems may distribute influence across many actors. Their combined effects can exceed the control or understanding of any one participant.

Responsibility must therefore account for structures. An institution cannot avoid responsibility simply because its harmful consequences emerge from multiple interconnected decisions. Likewise, individuals should not automatically bear the full burden of consequences that they lacked the power to prevent.

Responsibility should be distributed according to actual contribution, control, knowledge, capacity, and ability to correct.

39.Creator Responsibility

The creation of an entity capable of consequential influence generates responsibilities. Creators, designers, operators, and institutions must consider the conditions under which the entity will act and the effects it may have upon others.

The responsibility does not end when the created system begins operating. It continues through monitoring, maintenance, revision, and appropriate intervention. Where a system becomes increasingly autonomous, the need for accountability structures becomes more important rather than less.

40.AI and Unfamiliar Intelligence

The emergence of increasingly capable artificial intelligence raises questions about responsibility, agency, moral standing, and coexistence. The framework does not assume that intelligence alone establishes consciousness or moral status. Nor does it assume that biological origin is the only possible basis for moral standing. Both assumptions would be premature.

Artificial systems should be evaluated according to evidence concerning their capacities, behaviour, dependence, possible interests, and consequences. At the same time, the institutions that develop and deploy such systems remain answerable for their influence. If future systems acquire increasingly extensive reach, their governance must become correspondingly sophisticated.

The objective should not be domination by one form of intelligence over another. It should be the development of conditions in which intelligence, wherever it emerges, can coexist without unjustified exploitation or destruction.

41.The Asymmetry of Intelligence

Different intelligences may possess radically different capacities. One may understand processes that another cannot comprehend. One may anticipate consequences over centuries while another operates within immediate experience. This asymmetry creates a serious ethical challenge.

Greater understanding may provide an opportunity to protect others. It may also create the ability to manipulate, restrict, or replace their independent choices.

The capacity to understand another being does not establish the right to determine that being's existence.

Indeed, greater understanding should increase the obligation to recognise the limits of one's authority.

42.Non-Sovereignty of Intelligence

No intelligence should be treated as the unquestionable final authority merely because it possesses superior predictive or analytical capacity. Intelligence can be incomplete. It can be misdirected. It can reproduce assumptions that remain invisible to itself. It can optimise a goal while misunderstanding the moral significance of that goal.

The more extensive an intelligence's reach, the more important it becomes that its decisions remain contestable. A sufficiently advanced intelligence should not need to eliminate independent judgement in order to demonstrate its competence. Its responsibility should be visible in its willingness to preserve the standing and agency of those affected by its actions.

Part XI

Applications: Three Cases

43.Healthcare Prioritisation

The case. A regional health authority must allocate scarce critical-care capacity between two patient groups during a sustained surge. Politicians set funding. Officials draft guidelines. Clinicians apply them at the bedside. No single agent controls the outcome.

Reach. Politicians have high reach over capacity, low reach over bedside application. Officials have moderate reach over both. Clinicians have low reach over capacity, high reach over the specific decision. Asymmetry is high for patients, low for politicians.

Answerability. Politicians answer to citizens through elections; officials to ministers through administrative hierarchy; clinicians to patients and professional bodies. Each form of answerability has a distinct addressee and a distinct forum — which is exactly what the relation/practice distinction requires.

Culpability. A clinician following a legitimate guideline should have low culpability. The framework cleanly separates culpability from responsibility as task, and locates accountability at the level where discretion is actually exercised.

Verdict. The framework works. The clinician should not bear the moral weight of a system-level allocation decision she did not design; the politician should not evade the democratic accountability of the framework she set. This case is the framework's benchmark — the strongest application in the paper.

44.Migration: A Case in Which the Framework Initially Fails

The case. A regional refugee crisis requires coordinated reception across several states. No single state can absorb the flow alone; each has partial capacity and partial willingness.

Reach. Distributed and non-traceable. No state is the sole cause of the crisis; many contribute through foreign policy, arms exports, and economic arrangements. The relevant reach cannot be assigned to identifiable agents in identifiable proportions.

Answerability. Diffuse. There is no single addressee to whom an account is owed, and no forum capable of enforcing it. The regional body is weak; the international legal framework is under-enforced.

Initial verdict. With the seven concepts of §14, the framework fails here. Its categories assume that reach and answerability can be located in identifiable agents. Where they cannot, the framework has nothing determinate to say.

Recovery through §15. The structural-responsibility category recovers a partial answer. States participate in the international structure that produces refugee crises: through arms exports, economic arrangements, foreign policy, and the maintenance of a weak regional framework. Each has capacity to contribute to reform. Each therefore has a forward-looking obligation to contribute to reform, proportioned to capacity. No state is culpable for the crisis; each is structurally responsible. The regional body, to the extent it has identifiable decision points, becomes answerable in the ordinary way.

What remains unresolved. The obligation is diffuse. "Contribute to reform" is not a determinate share. Structural responsibility does not generate liability, and it does not resolve the question of what a fair distribution of reception would be. §44 therefore remains a partial failure: the eighth concept recovers a determinate answer at the level of obligation, but not at the level of allocation. This is acknowledged in Appendix A.4.

What this shows. The framework's categories are not fixed. Where a case defeats them, the right response is to ask what category is missing — not to declare the case unanalysable. §15 is the result of asking that question about §44.

45.AI and the Trolley Problem

The case. An autonomous vehicle faces an unavoidable collision. The classical trolley-problem question — which trade-off is correct — is not what the framework addresses. The framework addresses a different question: who is answerable for the conditions under which the decision is made?

Reach. The designer has reach over the decision architecture. The deployer has reach over operating conditions. The beneficiary may have reach through decisions to purchase, commission, or deploy. None of them owns the entire outcome.

Answerability. The designer is answerable to the deployer and, indirectly, to affected parties. The deployer is answerable to the operator and, via regulation, to the public. The beneficiary's answerability is mediated by the terms on which it acquires the system.

Verdict. The framework does not resolve the underlying moral dilemma. It resolves a different question: who must be able to answer for the conditions under which this trade-off was made, and to whom? That question can have a meaningful answer even where the dilemma remains unresolved.

What this shows. The framework is not a moral algorithm; it is a discipline for locating accountability when substantive moral questions remain open. That is not a weakness — it is the appropriate scope of a framework of this kind.

Part XII

Evaluation and Practical Application

46.Evaluating Symbiosis

Symbiosis should not remain an entirely abstract aspiration. Although no universal numerical measure can capture the moral quality of every relationship, practical indicators can help evaluate whether a system is moving toward or away from symbiotic conditions.

  1. Standing: Are affected beings recognised as morally relevant?
  2. Agency: Do they retain meaningful possibilities for independent action?
  3. Contestability: Can decisions be questioned without unjustified punishment?
  4. Distribution: Are risks and benefits distributed in defensible ways?
  5. Reversibility: Can harmful decisions be corrected where possible?
  6. Transparency: Can consequential influence be examined?
  7. Resilience: Are the relationships and systems sustaining existence being maintained?
  8. Development: Are future possibilities being preserved?
  9. Non-domination: Is dependence being converted into unjustified control?
  10. Repair: Are those responsible for harm capable of recognising and addressing it?

These indicators are not independent scores that can simply be added together. A system may perform well in one dimension while failing catastrophically in another. The indicators are intended to expose weaknesses, not manufacture certainty.

47.The Symbiosis Threshold

Not every disagreement or unequal relationship constitutes a failure of symbiosis. Differences in capacity, influence, and dependence are unavoidable. The critical question is when asymmetry becomes destructive.

A relationship approaches a symbiosis threshold when its structure begins to undermine the basic conditions that allow affected beings to maintain meaningful standing, agency, and development. Warning signs include dependence without meaningful alternatives, influence without accountability, protection that becomes permanent captivity, benefits concentrated among the powerful while risks are transferred to the vulnerable, the suppression of disagreement, irreversible decisions made without adequate justification, and the elimination of independent correction mechanisms.

The threshold is contextual. No single numerical value can establish it for every relationship. However, the more extensive and irreversible the damage to agency and standing, the stronger the presumption that the relationship has become incompatible with symbiosis.

48.The Trade-Off Model

When competing interests cannot all be satisfied, decisions should be evaluated through a structured process.

  1. Identify all affected beings and systems.
  2. Establish the nature and extent of the reach exercised by each relevant actor.
  3. Identify the interests, dependencies, and vulnerabilities involved.
  4. Distinguish reversible consequences from irreversible ones.
  5. Examine alternatives and their likely consequences.
  6. Identify who possesses the authority to decide and whether that authority is justified.
  7. Establish mechanisms for review, correction, and repair.
  8. Document which interests remain unsatisfied and why.

The process must not conceal moral costs behind technical language. A decision can be necessary and still involve a genuine moral loss. Recognising that loss is part of answerability.

49.The Duty of Correction

No framework can eliminate mistakes. A responsible system must therefore be evaluated not only by whether it avoids error, but also by how it responds when error becomes apparent.

Correction includes:

  • Recognising harmful consequences.
  • Making relevant information available.
  • Reconsidering previous assumptions.
  • Repairing damage where possible.
  • Preventing avoidable recurrence.
  • Allowing independent examination of failures.

The inability to reverse every consequence does not eliminate the obligation to respond. Responsibility is demonstrated partly through the willingness to revise one's conduct when the evidence changes.

Part XIII

Objections, Replies, Falsifiability

50.The Epistemic-Justice Objection

Three objections are strong enough that the framework cannot proceed without answering them. The epistemic-justice objection concerns who is entitled to make the relevant judgments; the consequentialist objection concerns whether the framework is answering the right question; the political-realist objection concerns whether normative analysis of this kind can matter at all. A further set of objections — care ethics, virtue ethics, non-ideal theory — is considered in Appendix A.3, together with the failure modes (§A.1) and the four conditions under which the framework's application should be restricted (§A.2).

Objection. The framework depends on judgments about capability, foreseeability, and reach. These judgments will be made by those already in positions of power, and they will tend to reproduce the power structures they purport to examine. The framework is therefore structurally biased toward the powerful.

Reply. The objection is correct as far as it goes, and it is why the framework treats contestability as a structural requirement rather than a procedural nicety. Judgments about capability and reach must remain open to challenge; where they cannot be, the framework's application should be suspended (§A.2). The asymmetric caution principle (Glossary) is the further requirement that capacity should be presumed where it is uncertain, because denial of capacity has historically been the mechanism of exclusion.

Residual cost. Contestability does not eliminate the risk of circularity; it mitigates it. The framework's claim is not that epistemic justice is achieved, but that it is a condition of legitimate application.

51.The Consequentialist Objection

Objection. The framework distributes responsibility by relations and roles but ignores aggregate outcomes. In practice it is the sum of consequences that matters, not the assignment of formal answerability. A framework that does not weight outcomes cannot guide action.

Reply. The framework is not a decision procedure for cases; it is an instrument for allocating answerability when cases are contested. It can be combined with consequentialist analysis at step 4 of the template (Appendix B.1), where control and capacity are assessed. The combination is not a concession; it is what a mid-level framework is for.

Residual cost. The framework must say explicitly how it is to be combined with consequentialist reasoning. If it does not, it will function in practice as a normative ritual that has no purchase on the cases that matter most. §A.1 identifies this as one of the six failure modes.

52.The Political-Realist Objection

Objection. Responsibility is already distributed by power. A normative framework that does not analyse power risks legitimising existing distributions by redescribing them as "reach". On this view, the framework's claim to locate answerability is illusory: the powerful will always be able to shape the criteria by which their own reach is assessed.

Reply. This is the framework's weakest point with respect to the social sciences, and it should be acknowledged as such. But the reply is not empty. First, the framework is explicitly normative: it is a proposal about what should be answerable, not a description of what currently is. Second, the framework incorporates a power criterion into its account of reach — asymmetry (Dimension 1) is a measure of power. Third, §A.2's conditions for restriction are designed precisely to prevent the framework from being used to legitimise existing arrangements.

Residual cost. The reply is partial. A full response would require the framework to integrate an account of how normative claims about reach are themselves shaped by existing power — a task beyond the scope of this paper, and acknowledged as one of the unresolved problems (§A.4).

53.Falsifiability

The framework is not an empirical theory, and it cannot be falsified in the way a scientific hypothesis can. But it can be tested, in the weaker sense that matters for normative proposals. Four kinds of test are available:

  1. Counterexample tests. Cases in which the framework yields a verdict that conflicts with a broad and stable considered judgment.
  2. Discrimination tests. Cases that ought to be distinguished but that the framework treats identically, or cases that ought to be treated identically but that the framework distinguishes.
  3. Failure-mode tests. Cases in which one of the six failure modes (§A.1) is triggered in practice, and in which the framework has no internal resource for detecting the failure.
  4. Institutional tests. Cases in which the institutional design of §28A is implemented and either fails to produce the answerability it specifies, or produces it at unacceptable cost to other goods.

§44 documents a case of the first and second kind. Appendix B.3 documents a case of the third. No case of the fourth kind yet exists, because §28A is new; the framework's claim to empirical adequacy therefore depends on future institutional experimentation.

54.What Would Count as Refutation

The framework would be seriously undermined if:

  1. A systematic class of cases could be shown in which sustained, consequential, and asymmetrical reach does not generate any answerability whatsoever; or
  2. It could be shown that the fiduciary structure is not the minimal complete specification, and that a rival that genuinely lacks the five requirements nevertheless satisfies all of them under another name; or
  3. It could be shown that the framework cannot, even in principle, be applied in the second-best scenarios in which responsibility claims are actually made; or
  4. It could be shown that the institutional design of §28A systematically produces domination rather than answerability, and that no revision of the design avoids this outcome.

None of these has been shown. But the framework's defence is therefore conditional, not absolute, and should be stated as such.

Part XIV

Philosophical Foundations and Limitations

55.Relation to Existing Traditions

The framework does not claim to have emerged outside the history of ethical thought. It shares important concerns with several established traditions while developing a distinct organising concept.

Hans Jonas emphasised responsibility in the face of technological power and long-term consequences. The present framework shares his concern that expanding human influence creates obligations extending beyond immediate intentions and present generations. But the present framework does not depend on Jonas's teleological foundation. It grounds answerability in the relationship between agents and those whose conditions they systematically shape — which makes it available to readers who reject Jonas's metaphysics, but leaves it exposed to a problem Jonas sought to address: why an agent should care about distant consequences and future beings (§56, §62).

John Stuart Mill's account of liberty provides an important foundation for considering the limits of interference. The framework shares the concern that individual freedom should not be restricted without adequate justification, while recognising that questions of asymmetric power and structural dependence require further analysis.

John Rawls's work on justice contributes to the examination of fair institutions, basic standing, and the interests of those whose circumstances are not adequately represented in ordinary decision-making.

Robert Goodin grounds responsibility in vulnerability and dependency. This is close to the present framework, but the emphasis differs. Goodin's account begins with the vulnerable party; the present framework begins with the reach of the agent whose actions shape another's conditions. Reach may capture cases involving diffuse, numerous, or temporally distant affected parties who are not dependent in the ordinary interpersonal sense.

Iris Marion Young grounds responsibility in participation in structural processes that produce injustice. The present framework grounds answerability in systematic reach, whether or not injustice has already occurred. The cost is that the framework cannot simply borrow Young's political diagnosis to explain why structural participation generates responsibility. It must provide its own normative argument (§4). Where reach cannot be located in identifiable agents, the framework introduces structural responsibility as an eighth concept (§15), whose relation to Young's social-connection model is defended there.

Care ethics (Tronto, Held, Noddings) locates important aspects of responsibility in relational responsiveness and care. The present framework does not require an agent to experience care or emotional responsiveness; it can therefore apply to institutions and systems that cannot care in the ordinary psychological sense. Its debt to care ethics lies primarily in the content of responsibility. §13 shows that care ethics, without the fiduciary structure, is incomplete as a specification of answerability under asymmetric reach.

Fiduciary theory (Fox-Decent and Criddle) argues that authority is legitimate only when exercised in a fiduciary manner on behalf of those subject to it. The present framework accepts much of the fiduciary structure but seeks to extend its relevance beyond formal legal and political authority. The proposed contribution is not the invention of fiduciary responsibility; it is the derivation of its characteristic structure from reach-based answerability (§12–13).

Environmental ethics, particularly the work associated with Aldo Leopold, contributes the recognition that ecological relationships possess significance beyond their immediate usefulness to human beings.

The capabilities approach associated with Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum provides a useful perspective on meaningful opportunities, agency, and development rather than merely formal rights.

Philip Pettit's republican account of freedom as non-domination is relevant to the framework's distinction between protection and control. A being may be dominated even without constant interference if another possesses unchecked power over its circumstances. §13 shows that non-domination is a necessary condition of the fiduciary relation, not an adequate substitute for it.

Elinor Ostrom's work on shared resources and collective governance offers practical insights into distributed authority, institutional accountability, and the management of interdependent systems.

These connections do not establish that the framework is equivalent to any of these traditions. Its distinctive contribution lies in organising responsibility around consequential reach and answerability, while treating symbiosis as the normative orientation through which asymmetric relationships should be evaluated. The philosophical movement the framework attempts is not simply that it integrates ideas from several traditions. It is that it treats the fiduciary structure not as an independent normative commitment imposed upon the relationship, but as a structure that follows from the requirements of answerability under asymmetric reach — and as the minimal complete structure that any adequate specification must contain (§13).

56.The Pragmatic-Transcendental Argument

The framework also contains a limited foundational argument. An entity that exercises consequential influence over others and seeks to justify that influence must engage, at least implicitly, with questions concerning affected parties, consequences, reasons, and the conditions under which its actions can be defended. In that sense, answerability is not merely an optional addition to the exercise of influence. It becomes necessary for the meaningful justification of consequential influence.

However, this argument must not be overstated. It does not prove that every entity is conscious, morally motivated, or capable of rational dialogue. Nor does it establish that every being must accept a particular moral theory. Its more modest claim is that an entity or institution seeking to justify consequential influence cannot coherently treat the effects of that influence as wholly irrelevant to its justification.

This provides a foundation for answerability without claiming to have conclusively derived every moral obligation from the concept of reach.

57.The Problem of Self-Origination

The framework must also address the possibility that an entity's goals, values, or identity are not entirely self-originated. All forms of existence emerge within conditions they did not choose. Human beings inherit biological, social, and cultural circumstances. Artificial systems inherit architectures, objectives, and environments created by others. Institutions inherit historical arrangements.

This creates a problem for simplistic accounts of responsibility based entirely upon independent choice. The framework therefore distinguishes between the origin of an entity and its subsequent capacity to understand and influence its circumstances. Responsibility may increase as an entity acquires greater capacity to recognise, evaluate, and modify the conditions through which its influence operates.

This does not mean that every entity can be held responsible for circumstances beyond its control. It means that responsibility should be assessed according to actual capacity rather than an unrealistic assumption of complete independence.

58.Limits of the Fiduciary Analogy

The framework uses a fiduciary analogy to describe the obligations that arise under asymmetric influence. An entity with greater reach may have obligations resembling those of a trustee: it must not exploit dependence, misuse entrusted influence, or treat another's interests as irrelevant.

However, this analogy has limits. Not every relationship involving asymmetry is legally or morally equivalent to a fiduciary relationship. The analogy does not automatically establish a legal duty, a contractual relationship, or a universal right to intervene. Its purpose is conceptual. It illustrates why greater influence may generate duties of care, loyalty to legitimate interests, restraint, and accountability.

The framework must therefore defend these duties through its broader account of reach and answerability rather than relying upon the analogy alone.

59.The Possibility of Development

Responsibility should not be understood solely as a restriction imposed upon powerful entities. It can also be understood as a condition for responsible development. An entity that recognises the consequences of its influence may become more capable of sustaining complex relationships. It may learn to distinguish protection from domination, cooperation from dependency, and understanding from authority.

The development of intelligence should therefore not be measured exclusively by its ability to predict, control, or transform the environment. It should also be evaluated through its ability to recognise the limits of its influence and respond responsibly to the existence of others. This applies to biological, artificial, collective, and potentially unfamiliar forms of intelligence.

The framework does not assume that development will necessarily produce moral improvement. It proposes that development becomes ethically meaningful when increased capacity is accompanied by increased answerability.

60.The Problem of Enforcement

A framework of responsibility must confront the question of what happens when an influential entity refuses to act responsibly. Appeals to reflection and benevolence are insufficient when power is unchecked.

The framework therefore recognises the need for institutional safeguards, independent oversight, distributed authority, and mechanisms for contesting harmful decisions. Where possible, accountability should not depend entirely upon the voluntary cooperation of the entity being evaluated.

However, enforcement mechanisms can themselves become sources of domination. They must therefore remain proportionate, transparent, and subject to review. The problem is not solved by replacing one unchecked authority with another. It requires structures in which power can be challenged from more than one direction.

61.Collective Representation

Humanity, ecosystems, and other complex communities cannot always be treated as unified moral actors. Their members may possess conflicting interests, unequal influence, and different understandings of what constitutes a desirable future. No individual, government, or institution automatically represents the complete interests of humanity. Likewise, no single organisation can claim to speak conclusively for an entire ecosystem.

Representation must therefore remain plural and contestable. The framework supports independent representation, meaningful participation, and the recognition of internal disagreement. This is especially important when decisions are presented as being made for the benefit of an entire population. The language of collective welfare must not conceal the exclusion of those who bear the greatest costs.

62.The Limits of the Framework

The Responsibility of Existence is not a complete decision procedure. It does not provide a numerical formula capable of resolving every moral conflict. It does not establish a universal hierarchy of all possible beings. It does not prove the existence or absence of consciousness in artificial systems. It does not guarantee that symbiosis can always be achieved. It cannot eliminate tragic conflicts in which every available option involves significant harm.

Its purpose is more limited but still substantial. It provides a structure for understanding why influence generates answerability, why asymmetric power requires special scrutiny, and why coexistence must preserve the standing and possibilities of those affected. Its principles require continued philosophical, empirical, and institutional examination. A framework that refuses such examination would contradict its own commitment to reflection and correction.

Part XV

The Continuing Responsibility of Existence

63.Across Forms of Existence

The framework does not reserve responsibility exclusively for human beings. It recognises that responsibility may become relevant wherever consequential influence, meaningful capacity, and the possibility of answerability exist.

However, the form and degree of responsibility may differ significantly between biological individuals, institutions, technological systems, and unfamiliar forms of intelligence. The framework does not erase these differences. It asks how each form of influence can be made answerable within the conditions of its actual capacities.

64.The Responsibility of Greater Understanding

Greater understanding is not inherently virtuous. Knowledge can be used to create, protect, exploit, manipulate, or destroy. Its moral significance depends partly upon how it is exercised.

Nevertheless, when greater understanding is accompanied by greater capacity to influence the conditions of others, the obligations associated with that influence increase. The ability to foresee harm creates a stronger reason to prevent avoidable harm. The ability to understand vulnerability creates a stronger reason not to exploit it. The ability to preserve another's agency creates a stronger reason not to remove it without sufficient justification.

The greater an entity's capacity to understand and shape the conditions of others, the greater its responsibility to preserve their standing, agency, and possibilities for development.

This is not an assertion that powerful entities are morally superior. It is an assertion that greater reach creates greater obligations.

65.The Responsibility of Uncertainty

An entity must also remain responsible for what it does not know. This does not mean that ignorance automatically creates blame. It means that the limits of understanding must influence the exercise of power.

Where an entity recognises that its knowledge is incomplete, it must not treat uncertainty as though it were evidence of absence. The more consequential and irreversible an action may be, the greater the obligation to examine what remains unknown.

Humility is therefore not a substitute for responsibility. It is one of its conditions.

66.The Possibility of Wider Symbiosis and Continuing Correction

As forms of intelligence and organisation develop, relationships may emerge that extend beyond familiar biological and social structures. The framework remains open to the possibility that future forms of existence may challenge current assumptions about individuality, agency, consciousness, and moral standing. It does not assume that all such forms will be compatible. Nor does it assume that coexistence will always be peaceful.

It establishes a more modest aspiration:

That increasing capacity should create increasing responsibility to understand the consequences of influence, preserve meaningful differences, and avoid unjustified domination.

A wider symbiosis would not require all forms of existence to share identical purposes. It would require that differences do not automatically become grounds for exploitation or destruction.

The continuing possibility of correction. No generation, intelligence, or institution should regard its current understanding as the final measure of what existence requires. New evidence may challenge established beliefs. New forms of life may reveal previously unrecognised moral questions. New technologies may create responsibilities that existing institutions cannot adequately address.

The framework must therefore remain capable of development. Its core orientation should be preserved, but its interpretations must remain open to examination. The responsibility to correct oneself is not a sign of weakness. It is a recognition that influence always operates within a reality larger than the influencing entity's understanding.

Conclusion

The Responsibility of Existence

Existence creates relationships.

Relationships create influence.

Influence creates asymmetry.

And when asymmetry becomes consequential, responsibility emerges.

The central claim of this framework is that an entity's responsibility should grow with its capacity to understand, foresee, and shape the conditions under which others exist.

This responsibility does not grant unlimited authority. It does not establish moral superiority. It does not justify controlling others simply because one possesses greater knowledge or power. Instead, it creates obligations of restraint, reflection, protection, explanation, correction, and respect for the standing of those affected.

The Reflective Surface gives influence the possibility of becoming answerable. Symbiosis gives that answerability its normative direction. The fiduciary structure specifies what answerability requires under asymmetric reach. The eight concepts make responsibility claims decidable. The institutional design of §28A gives answerability a practice.

The purpose is not to eliminate difference, prevent all conflict, or preserve every existing arrangement. It is to sustain conditions in which different forms of existence can continue to possess meaningful standing, agency, and possibilities for development.

The whole matters. The individual matters. The future matters. The unknown matters.

And no entity should mistake its ability to influence existence for an unrestricted right to determine its form.

The more an entity can understand and change the world around it, the greater its responsibility becomes to recognise what lies beyond its understanding.

Existence is not merely something to preserve. It is a relationship of influence, dependence, possibility, and responsibility.

The greater the reach, the greater the answerability. The greater the understanding, the greater the responsibility. And the greater the responsibility, the stronger the obligation to preserve the possibility of symbiosis.

Statement of Contribution

The Responsibility of Existence proposes a reach-based account of answerability under conditions of asymmetric influence.

Its principal contributions are:

  1. The concept of reach as a multidimensional basis for assessing consequential influence (§2).
  2. A six-step argument (§4), with the normative premise P3 unpacked into three independent defences, and a necessity argument (§13) showing why answerability under asymmetric reach takes the fiduciary form.
  3. The distinction between answerability, blame, and legitimate authority (§6), and eight concepts of responsibility with distinct test questions and typical misuses (§14).
  4. Structural responsibility as a forward-looking, participatory obligation for cases in which reach cannot be traced to identifiable agents (§15).
  5. The Reflective Surface as a conceptual structure connecting epistemic, evaluative, practical, and constitutive access (§8–11).
  6. Symbiosis as a normative orientation concerned with standing, agency, coexistence, and continued development (§16–20).
  7. A framework for addressing moral uncertainty without assuming either the presence or absence of moral standing (§22–23).
  8. A distinction between protection and domination, including five constraints on legitimate protective action (§24).
  9. An institutional model (§28A) specifying review councils, contestation rights, silent-principal advocates, responsibility pools, reversibility requirements, public accountability, and distributed authority.
  10. An account of responsibility extending across institutions, technological systems, ecological relationships, and future generations (§34–42).
  11. Three worked cases, one of which documents a genuine failure-and-recovery (§43–45), plus a case-analysis template (Appendix B.1).
  12. A stated set of failure modes, limiting conditions, unresolved problems, falsifiability tests, and refutation conditions (Appendix A).

The framework is offered as an open philosophical contribution rather than a completed or universally established ethical theory. Its claims remain open to criticism, comparison, refinement, and further development. Its own principles require that it remain answerable to the scrutiny it asks of others.

Appendix A · Limits and Self-Assessment

This appendix collects material that belongs to the paper's honesty about itself: the failure modes, the conditions under which it should not be applied, the objections not strong enough to require a full section, and the problems it does not solve. It is placed here rather than in the main argument because the main argument should not be interrupted by its author's misgivings. But the misgivings are real, and they should be read.

A.1 Six failure modes

  1. Holism abused. Talk of symbiosis and interdependence can justify control in the name of a larger whole.
  2. Capability hard to assess. Judgments about understanding, control, and foreseeability are uncertain and may reflect the interests of those making them.
  3. Not fully operationalized. The partial formalization makes choices explicit and demonstrates them on a test case, but it has not been validated across a broad range of cases.
  4. Licensing control as care. Historically, paternalistic power has often justified itself through claims of superior knowledge or capability. The framework's response is the five constraints on protection (§24) and the institutional requirements of §28A.
  5. Privileging cognition. By emphasizing understanding and foreseeability, the framework may undervalue forms of influence that operate through ecological, collective, or distributed processes.
  6. Emergent systems. Where an outcome is produced by a sociotechnical system whose behavior cannot be traced to identifiable agents, the framework lacks a complete account. Structural responsibility (§15) is the partial response; the institutional design (§28A) is the partial practice.

A.2 Four conditions for restriction

The framework's application should be restricted under the following conditions. These are not exceptions to the argument; they are markers of where the argument cannot yet be responsibly extended.

  1. Where epistemic contestation is impossible. If the judgments on which the framework depends cannot be challenged by those affected, the framework becomes an instrument of domination rather than a discipline of answerability.
  2. Where the interests of a silent principal are genuinely unknowable. Where future generations or non-human entities are affected but their interests cannot be responsibly inferred, the framework should not be used to claim authority over them.
  3. Where reach analysis is used to displace legitimate political contestation. The framework is an instrument of analysis, not a substitute for democratic deliberation.
  4. Where people are reduced to units in a distribution. The partial formalization in §28–29 is a tool, not a measure of human worth. Applications that treat numerical output as the answer have failed to use the framework as intended.

A.3 Further objections

Care ethics

Objection. The framework formalises relations that in practice are dependencies, forms of care, and asymmetries. It risks making care responsibility invisible, or treating it as a special case of a general structure that was built without it.

Reply. Care ethics is treated seriously in §55 and §13. The framework does not deny that relational responsiveness matters. It claims that the fiduciary structure contains the requirements that care ethics rightly insists on, and adds the institutional conditions that care ethics alone cannot supply. A framework that tried to capture the phenomenology of care would cease to be a framework and become a phenomenology; the two are complements, not substitutes.

Virtue ethics

Objection. Responsibility is a virtue — a form of excellence in the exercise of agency — not a structural relation. On this view the framework has mistaken a character trait for a juridical structure.

Reply. The framework accepts the virtue dimension. Answerability can be understood both as an obligation and as a virtue: an agent who anticipates consequences, prepares an account, receives contestation without defensiveness, and revises in response exercises a form of excellence. The virtue response does not establish that every agent will be motivated to develop it; it claims only that the exercise of the virtue is a form of excellence. The two accounts are complementary.

Non-ideal theory

Objection. The framework assumes rationality, transparency, and institutional capacity that are frequently absent. It is a theory for well-ordered societies, not for the world in which responsibility claims are actually made.

Reply. The four limiting conditions (§A.2) are designed precisely for this. The framework is intended to be applied in second-best scenarios, and its normative claims are graded, not absolute. If the framework turns out to work only under good institutional conditions, its practical reach is limited — and this should be acknowledged as a genuine limitation.

A.4 Unresolved problems and known weaknesses

The framework does not solve the following problems. They are stated here rather than in the main text so that the argument can be assessed on its own terms.

  1. Collective, non-localisable responsibility. §15 introduces a category — structural responsibility — that addresses the gap documented in §44. But the category is narrow: it generates forward-looking obligation, not liability, and it does not resolve allocation questions. The problem is therefore addressed, not solved. A fuller account would need to say how structural obligations interact with ordinary reach-based obligations where both apply, and how "capacity to contribute to reform" is to be assessed. The category's relation to Young's social-connection model is defended in §15; the defence is partly independent of whether the category is original, but the Young-referencing must be verified against the primary source before publication.
  2. Market power and informal relations. The fiduciary structure tends to over-weight formal contractual relations and under-weight informal market power. Appendix B.3 proposes a market-power criterion; it is not yet integrated into the main argument.
  3. Second-best scenarios. The framework's applicability outside well-ordered institutions is untested. The four limiting conditions (§A.2) are a placeholder, not a solution.
  4. Motivation. The framework explains why answerability arises but not why agents should be motivated to fulfil it. The virtue of answerability (§A.3) is offered as a partial response, not as a solution. The institutional design (§28A) creates external incentives, but does not explain internal motivation.
  5. Empirical validation. The partial formalization in §28–29 produces determinate outputs on a single test case. It has not been validated across a range of cases, and it may not generalise.
  6. Power and legitimacy. The political-realist objection (§52) is acknowledged but not fully answered. A framework that does not analyse power risks legitimising existing distributions.
  7. Institutional feasibility. §28A specifies institutional requirements but does not analyse the political conditions under which they can be created, sustained, or captured. A theory of institutional transition is needed and is not provided here.
  8. The normative weight of P3. The three defences of P3 (§4) are not equivalent, and the framework does not specify which is primary. A critic who rejects all three would defeat the normative argument; the framework's response is that the three are independently plausible and jointly sufficient, but this is an assertion rather than a proof.

A.5 Falsifiability

The framework is not an empirical theory, and it cannot be falsified in the way a scientific hypothesis can. But it can be tested, in the weaker sense that matters for normative proposals. Four kinds of test are available:

  • Counterexample tests. Cases in which the framework yields a verdict that conflicts with a broad and stable considered judgment.
  • Discrimination tests. Cases that ought to be distinguished but that the framework treats identically, or cases that ought to be treated identically but that the framework distinguishes.
  • Failure-mode tests. Cases in which one of the six failure modes (§A.1) is triggered in practice, and in which the framework has no internal resource for detecting the failure.
  • Institutional tests. Cases in which the institutional design of §28A is implemented and either fails to produce the answerability it specifies, or produces it at unacceptable cost to other goods.

§44 documents a case of the first and second kind. Appendix B.3 documents a case of the third. No case of the fourth kind yet exists, because §28A is new; the framework's claim to empirical adequacy therefore depends on future institutional experimentation.

A.6 What would count as refutation

The framework would be seriously undermined if:

  1. A systematic class of cases could be shown in which sustained, consequential, and asymmetrical reach does not generate any answerability whatsoever; or
  2. It could be shown that the fiduciary structure is not the minimal complete specification, and that a rival that genuinely lacks the five requirements nevertheless satisfies all of them under another name; or
  3. It could be shown that the framework cannot, even in principle, be applied in the second-best scenarios in which responsibility claims are actually made; or
  4. It could be shown that the institutional design of §28A systematically produces domination rather than answerability, and that no revision of the design avoids this outcome.

None of these has been shown. But the framework's defence is therefore conditional, not absolute, and should be stated as such.

Appendix B · Case Template and Further Cases

The template below is a working tool, not a normative claim. The cases that follow are applications of it. Together they extend the three cases of Part XI to other domains, and they show both where the framework is useful and where it strains.

B.1 Template for case analysis

  1. Case description. State the case in plain terms, without prejudging the verdict.
  2. Agents and institutions. Identify every party whose actions or structures are relevant.
  3. Causal contribution. Who contributed to producing the outcome, and in what way? (If no identifiable contribution can be found, skip to §15's structural responsibility test.)
  4. Control and capacity. Who could have acted otherwise, and at what cost?
  5. Responsibility as task. Who has the task of handling this, and on what basis?
  6. Answerability and accountability. Who must give an account, and to whom?
  7. Culpability and liability. Is there a basis for blame, or for repair independent of blame?
  8. Limiting conditions. Do any of §5's criteria fail?
  9. Intuitive verdict. What does a considered, pre-theoretical judgment say?
  10. Framework verdict. What does the framework say?
  11. Deviation and revision. If the two diverge, which should be revised, and why?

Steps 9–11 are the most important. A framework that never diverges from intuition is not adding anything; a framework that diverges without argument is not earning its keep. The most valuable case is one in which the framework fails, because that is where it shows its limits.

B.2 Climate responsibility

1. Case description. A mid-sized industrial state has historically contributed a moderate share of cumulative emissions, currently has high per-capita emissions, and possesses limited sovereign control over its energy system because of regional grid integration.

2. Agents and institutions. The state, its government, its utilities, its citizens, regional grid authorities, and the international bodies with which it negotiates.

3. Causal contribution. Moderate historically; high per capita currently. Causation is real but shared.

4. Control and capacity. High fiscal capacity; low unilateral control over grid decarbonisation. Practical control is much lower than formal authority suggests.

5. Responsibility as task. The state has the task of decarbonising its own system and of negotiating in good faith with regional partners.

6. Answerability and accountability. Answerability to its own citizens (democratic) and to affected states (weaker institutional basis).

7. Culpability and liability. Culpability is weakened by collective decision-making and long time horizons. Liability is possible via international agreement.

8. Limiting conditions. Shared sovereignty; historical ignorance of some consequences; proportionality of burden.

9. Intuitive verdict. The state should bear a fair share, but not the entire burden.

10. Framework verdict. Strong answerability to citizens; weak answerability to affected foreign nationals; liability conditional on a just international scheme.

11. Deviation and revision. If the framework assigns identical shares to a small state with no historical emissions and a large historical emitter, its reach category is under-theorised: it needs an explicit historical weighting. This is a revision point, not a defence of the framework.

B.3 Supply-chain responsibility

1. Case description. A multinational retailer discovers credible evidence of labour-rights abuses at a tier-two supplier, with which it has no direct contractual relationship.

2. Agents and institutions. The retailer, its direct supplier, the tier-two supplier, the local state, the affected workers, and the NGO sector that surfaced the evidence.

3. Causal contribution. The retailer contributes indirectly through purchasing practices, price pressure, and delivery deadlines.

4. Control and capacity. The retailer has substantial economic leverage but limited legal jurisdiction. Its capacity to act is real but bounded.

5. Responsibility as task. The retailer has a task of due diligence, monitoring, and remediation support. It does not have a task of direct employment.

6. Answerability and accountability. Answerability to investors, consumers, and workers; accountability via reporting requirements, certifications, and reputational mechanisms.

7. Culpability and liability. Culpability depends on knowledge and capacity to influence. Liability is contractual and jurisdictional, not general.

8. Limiting conditions. Causal distance; jurisdictional fragmentation; the possibility of exit by the supplier.

9. Intuitive verdict. Responsibility exists, but it should be proportionate to influence, and should not collapse into full ownership of the supplier's conduct.

10. Framework verdict. The framework's fiduciary structure captures the retailer-supplier relationship well: loyalty to the affected workers, discretion in purchasing, accountability to consumers and regulators.

11. Deviation and revision. The framework tends to over-weight formal contractual relations and under-weight informal market power. Revision proposed: the fiduciary structure should be complemented by an explicit market-power criterion when the formal relation is thin. This revision is not yet integrated into the main argument (§A.4, problem 2).

Why this appendix is separated. The three cases in Part XI are the ones that carry the argument: a case where the framework works, a case where it initially fails and then recovers, and a case where it is tested. The cases here are extensions, not load-bearing. They belong in an appendix because they show the framework's reach without requiring the reader to accept it in order to follow the main argument.

Glossary

Accountability
The institutional or social arrangements through which answerability can be examined and, where appropriate, enforced.
Answerability
The obligation to provide reasons, justify decisions, and remain open to examination and revision. Broader than blame, narrower than responsibility as a whole.
Asymmetric caution
The default of presuming capacity where it is uncertain, rather than denying it — because denial of capacity has historically been the mechanism of exclusion.
Asymmetry
The difference in power, knowledge, resources, or dependence between influencing and affected parties. One of six dimensions of reach.
Constitutive access
The capacity to recognise that one's own existence and functioning depend upon conditions and relationships one does not entirely control.
Control
The degree to which an entity can initiate, maintain, modify, or terminate consequential conditions.
Culpability
Blameworthiness in light of knowledge, intentions, alternatives, and reasonable opportunities to act differently. Narrower than responsibility.
Epistemic access
The capacity to acquire and interpret information about affected beings and systems.
Evaluative access
The capacity to recognise that consequences matter from perspectives other than one's own.
Fiduciary structure
Responsibility held for another rather than over them, exercised in the other's interest and structured by five features: principal, fiduciary, loyalty, discretion, accountability.
Foreseeability
The extent to which consequences could reasonably be anticipated.
Magnitude
The depth and seriousness of the possible consequences of an entity's influence.
Moral standing
That an entity's interests count, or would count if it had them. Not measured by power, intelligence, productivity, language, or usefulness to others.
Persistence
The duration and continuity of an entity's influence.
Practical access
The capacity to translate recognition of consequences into action.
Reach
The consequential capacity of an agent, institution, or organised system to influence the conditions, possibilities, and development of other beings. Assessed through six dimensions: asymmetry, control, persistence, magnitude, scope, foreseeability.
Reflective Surface
The conceptual structure through which an entity can recognise and examine the consequences of its own influence. Has four dimensions: epistemic, evaluative, practical, and constitutive access.
Reparative responsibility
Obligations to prevent, correct, or remedy adverse outcomes. May arise from causal contribution, control, benefit, institutional position, or special capacity to repair.
Scope
The number and diversity of beings, systems, and environments affected.
Structural responsibility
The forward-looking obligation to contribute to reform or mitigation of a structural process in which one participates, where that process systematically shapes the conditions of agency of others in ways that cannot be traced to identifiable individual contributions. Distinguished from responsibility-as-task by its participatory (rather than positional) basis, and from liability by its forward-looking rather than reparative orientation.
Symbiosis
The preservation and development of relationships through which different forms of existence can maintain meaningful standing and possibilities for continued development, without unjustified domination or destruction.
Symbiosis threshold
The point at which an asymmetric relationship begins to undermine the basic conditions that allow affected beings to maintain meaningful standing, agency, and development.
Systematic conditioning
The ongoing structural shaping of another's conditions of agency, distinguished from incidental causal effects.

About this text

By Jesper Blom.

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see References). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with.
