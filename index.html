Abstract

This work develops a relational account of responsibility grounded in the concept of reach: the consequential capacity of an agent, institution, or organised system to shape the conditions under which other beings exist, develop, act, and continue.

Its central proposition is that substantial, sustained, and discretionary influence over another's conditions of agency creates a prima facie obligation of answerability. The greater an entity's capacity to understand, foresee, and shape the conditions of others, the stronger its obligation to explain, justify, review, and correct the exercise of that influence.

But the framework does not stop at a theory of obligation. It proposes a structural condition: universal symbiosis. Symbiosis is not merely a normative ideal to be chosen; it is the stable form that continued existence tends toward. Non-symbiotic arrangements are not impossible — they are unstable, and all existence that continues over extended periods does so through relations of mutual dependence.

The framework introduces a sixth principle alongside its five-step argument: P6, the Principle of the Open Hierarchy. Every entity that claims to be highest is thereby already situated within a hierarchy it cannot see. This applies to humans, institutions, civilisations, artificial intelligences, and every future form of existence that may surpass them. The framework is written in full awareness that its author is not the highest intelligence, and that its readers may not be either.

Six connected elements are developed: reach, answerability, the Reflective Surface, the fiduciary structure, symbiosis, and institutional arrangements. Eight concepts of responsibility are distinguished. Reach and symbiosis are operationalised as assessable structures. The framework is demonstrated against sophisticated rivals in three hard cases. It is compared point-by-point with Pettit's republicanism. It specifies what evidence would count against its central normative premise. And it is explicitly handed over to future intelligences — not as a finished system, but as a starting point for those who will see further than its author.

The framework's central aspiration is that increasing capacity should be accompanied by increasing answerability, and that no entity should mistake its own height for the summit.

Preface: What this framework is for

This essay develops a relational account of responsibility grounded in the concept of reach: the consequential capacity of an agent, institution, or organised system to shape the conditions under which other beings exist, develop, act, and continue.

Its central proposition is that substantial, sustained, and discretionary influence over another's conditions of agency creates a prima facie obligation of answerability. The greater an entity's capacity to understand, foresee, and shape the conditions of others, the stronger its obligation to explain, justify, review, and correct the exercise of that influence.

Responsibility is not a reward for intelligence, a measure of moral superiority, or an automatic entitlement to authority. It arises from the relationship between influence, dependence, capacity, and consequence.

The framework is written for two audiences at once.

The first is human: individuals, institutions, and societies that are beginning to understand what they cause — not only through intention, but through structure. The framework offers a vocabulary for that understanding.

The second is not yet here. Any framework for responsibility in an age of expanding capability must be written so that it can be taken further by intelligences that do not yet exist — and that may, by the framework's own logic, be higher than we are. That is not a rhetorical gesture. It is a structural condition of the argument. There is always a higher existence than the one that believes itself highest. Any framework that forgets this has already failed its own test.

The framework therefore makes three claims that run through everything that follows:

  1. Reach generates answerability — independent of demand, independent of blame, independent of authority.
  2. No entity can coherently claim to be the highest — because the claim itself presupposes a position above the hierarchy it claims to top.
  3. Symbiosis is the stable form of continued existence — non-symbiotic arrangements are not impossible, but they are unstable, and all existence that continues over time does so through relations of mutual dependence.

The central aspiration is that increasing capacity should be accompanied by increasing answerability, and that all existence should be able to continue in symbiosis. Whether this can be recognised without becoming a new form of domination is the question the framework exists to pose, not to answer.

Part I

Foundations

1.Introduction

Existence is relational. No individual, organism, institution, or intelligence exists independently of the conditions surrounding it.

Some entities possess considerably greater capacity than others to shape these conditions. A government can influence the possibilities of millions. A corporation can determine the conditions of workers and communities. A technological system can influence decisions across entire populations. A future intelligence might affect systems and forms of existence beyond present human understanding.

What obligations arise when one entity possesses substantial power to shape the conditions under which another exists?

This work argues that consequential reach creates a distinctive obligation of answerability. The obligation is not unlimited, and it does not establish that the influencing entity has authority over those affected. The central distinction is:

Influence can generate responsibility without generating sovereignty.

A second distinction is equally important:

Influence can generate answerability without generating blame.

An entity may be answerable for conditions it shapes even when it lacks the intentions, culpability, or reasonable alternative action required for ordinary moral blame. This matters for institutions, for artificial systems, and for future intelligences whose reach may exceed their capacity to be held to account in the ordinary sense.

2.Reach

Reach is the capacity to shape the conditions, possibilities, and development of other beings. It is multidimensional and irreducible to intelligence or formal authority. Six dimensions are proposed: asymmetry, control, persistence, magnitude, scope, foreseeability.

These form a profile, not a single score. An entity may have great reach in one dimension and limited reach in another.

2.1 Necessary conditions

Distinctively reach-based answerability arises only when all three necessary conditions hold:

  • (N1) Discretion. The influencing agent could, in principle, have chosen otherwise on the relevant dimensions without thereby being forced to track the affected party's interests.
  • (N2) Persistence. Influence operates over a duration sufficient to shape not merely an event but a condition — indicatively, across multiple decision points of the affected party.
  • (N3) Non-attributability. The affected party cannot, through their own agency alone, revise the condition.

If any necessary condition fails, no distinctively reach-based answerability arises. General moral obligations may still apply.

N1 is deliberately formulated in terms of tracking rather than constraint. All choice is constrained. The relevant question is not whether the agent could have chosen otherwise in a vacuum, but whether the agent's choice was structurally forced to track the affected party's interests. This preserves grading: discretion can be more or less arbitrariness-tracking, and answerability scales accordingly.

2.2 Modifiers

Once N1–N3 hold, three modifiers calibrate the strength and urgency of the obligation:

  • Threshold-raisers (raise the burden of justification and are never overridden by multipliers): irreversibility, catastrophic magnitude, low contestability.
  • Multipliers (calibrate strength): asymmetry, scope.
  • Lowering factor: foreseeability — where consequences are reasonably anticipatable, the obligation to explain and prevent strengthens; where not, the obligation shifts toward investigation.

2.3 The lexicographic rule (not a weighted sum)

A weighted sum of the six dimensions would (a) presuppose commensurability between incommensurable goods, (b) invite gaming of the metric, and (c) generate false precision. The framework therefore applies:

  1. Necessary-condition gate: if N1, N2, or N3 fails, no distinctively reach-based answerability.
  2. Threshold-raisers: if irreversibility or low contestability holds, obligation cannot be capped below a specified level regardless of asymmetry or scope.
  3. Multiplier calibration: asymmetry and scope adjust strength within the range left by step 2.

The rule has a limit that must be acknowledged explicitly: it cannot adjudicate between two threshold-raisers that conflict (e.g., irreversible harm to one party vs. irreversible foreclosure of another's future). In such cases the rule falls silent. This is not a defect. A metric that always delivers a verdict is a metric that has stopped measuring.

2.4 A worked example

Consider a designer of a platform recommendation algorithm versus an ordinary user of that platform.

  • Designer. N1: yes — design choices are discretionary and not forced to track users' interests. N2: yes — the algorithm conditions the user's information environment continuously. N3: yes — the user cannot revise the algorithmic condition without exiting the platform, which is costly. Threshold-raisers: low contestability; magnitude moderate. Multipliers: high asymmetry, high scope. Conclusion: strong reach-based answerability.
  • Ordinary user. N1: yes (posting is discretionary). N2: no — one user's influence on another is not systematically conditioning. N3: no — the affected party can generally revise their exposure through their own choices. Conclusion: no distinctively reach-based answerability. Ordinary moral norms still apply.

The framework does actual work: it distinguishes the designer from the user without collapsing either into "everyone is responsible" or "no one is."

3.Existence as a Relational Condition

Existence is not merely biological survival. It includes the conditions through which beings maintain identity, pursue interests, exercise agency, form relationships, and develop.

This does not mean every relationship is beneficial or that every arrangement deserves preservation. Some relationships are exploitative; some systems preserve themselves by restricting the possibilities of others. Coexistence does not establish moral acceptability. The framework therefore examines the conditions under which existence continues, not merely that it continues.

But the relational condition is stronger than it first appears. To exist is not only to be situated among conditions; it is to be dependent on conditions one did not create and cannot fully control. Every entity that exists does so through relations of support, exchange, and dependence it did not choose. This is the first hint of the framework's central claim: symbiosis is not an ideal imposed on existence from outside; it is the stable form that continued existence tends toward.

4.The Argument

P1 — Agency and conditions. Agents exercise influence through actions and decisions that shape the conditions under which other agents act.

P2 — Systematic conditioning. Where influence is substantial, sustained, and subject to meaningful discretion, it constitutes systematic conditioning rather than incidental causation.

P3 — Equal moral standing and justification. Where one agent systematically conditions another's meaningful possibilities for agency, the exercise of that influence creates a prima facie duty of justification to those affected.

P4 — The failure of attribution. Where the affected party cannot meaningfully attribute the conditions to their own agency, the influencing party cannot dismiss its obligation merely by pointing to formal consent or lack of protest.

P5 — The obligation of answerability. The influencing party must be able to explain, justify, and where necessary revise the exercise of its consequential reach.

P6 — The open hierarchy. Every entity that claims to be the highest is already situated within an order it cannot see. The claim to maximal standing presupposes a vantage point from which the claim is made, and that vantage point is itself conditioned by something beyond it. The claim to be highest is therefore performatively self-undermining: it requires a position above the hierarchy it claims to top.

C — The responsibility conclusion. Substantial, sustained, and discretionary reach creates a prima facie obligation of answerability proportionate to its extent and consequences. And because no entity can coherently claim to be the highest, every entity — including those that believe themselves most advanced — remains answerable to something it cannot fully see.

4.1 The status of P3

P3 is the normative bridge on which the framework rests. It is offered neither as a deduction from self-evident premises nor as a moral axiom. It is offered as a wide reflective equilibrium point, defended by two converging arguments.

The first is the convergence argument (§4.2): independent traditions arrive at a principle structurally equivalent to P3.

The second is the performative-inconsistency argument (§4.3): denial of P3 is unstable for any agent who continues to address the affected party as a party.

Neither argument proves P3. Together they raise the cost of denying it. What would count against P3 is specified in §4.5.

4.2 The convergence argument

Several otherwise rival moral traditions arrive independently at a principle structurally equivalent to P3: contractualism (Scanlon 1998), republicanism (Pettit 1997, 2012), care ethics (Tronto 1993; Held 2006), Kantian justification, and discourse ethics (Habermas 1984–87).

These traditions disagree about why P3 holds, about the scope of "affected party," and about what follows institutionally. But they converge on the claim that systematic conditioning of another's agency generates an obligation of justification to that other.

The convergence is not a deductive proof. It is a robustness argument: when independent traditions with incompatible foundations converge on a claim, the burden shifts to the denier to explain why the convergence is illusory.

The convergence is vulnerable to what §4.5 calls the verbal-convergence objection: that "justifiability," "non-arbitrariness," "responsiveness," and "acceptability" may mean incompatible things. If so, the convergence collapses into verbal similarity, and the framework rests on the performative argument alone.

4.3 The performative-inconsistency argument

The performative argument is offered as an independent line of defence for P3, one that does not depend on convergence across traditions.

4.3.1 The argument in outline

  1. To deny P3 to the affected party is to make a claim to them.
  2. To make a claim to someone is to treat them as capable of receiving, evaluating, and responding — and to treat their response as having normative weight.
  3. To treat someone's response as having normative weight is to be already in a justificatory relation with them.
  4. Therefore to deny P3 to the affected party is to presuppose the justificatory relation it denies.

The conclusion is that denial of P3 is performatively inconsistent: it can be stated, but it cannot be coherently lived while the agent continues to address the affected party as a party.

4.3.2 What it means to address as a party

Three features are constitutive:

  • Receptivity. The addressee is treated as capable of receiving and understanding the claim.
  • Evaluability. The addressee is treated as capable of assessing the claim — of finding it true or false, warranted or unwarranted.
  • Answerability. The addressee is treated as capable of responding with a counter-claim, a demand for reasons, or a refusal that itself demands justification.

These three features are what distinguish address from mere causal production of behaviour.

4.3.3 Why addressing commits

Premise 3 is the conceptual claim that treating a response as having normative weight entails being in a justificatory relation.

The claim is not that the entailment is analytic — a critic could define "party" differently. The claim is that any alternative stipulation either collapses into force or into an infinite regress. If the addressee's response has no normative weight, the address is not a claim but a causal production — and a causal production cannot be a claim. If the addressee's response has normative weight, the speaker is already in the justificatory relation. There is no third option. This is not a proof. It is a dilemma.

4.3.4 Objections

Objection 1: What if the agent refuses to address the affected party at all?

Then no performative inconsistency arises — but the agent has also abandoned any claim to legitimacy in the affected party's eyes. The framework's claim is limited to the domain of claimed legitimacy. An agent who exercises pure force makes no claim, and the performative argument does not apply. But pure force is unstable over time (§30), and agents who wish to exercise reach without pure force must make claims — which brings them within the performative argument's scope.

Objection 2: What if the agent makes the claim insincerely?

Insincerity does not refute the argument. The argument is about what the claim commits one to, not about the psychological state of the claimant.

Objection 3: What if the addressee's response has no normative weight?

Then the "claim" is not a claim. It is causal production. The framework's reply is the dilemma in §4.3.3: either the response has normative weight (and the justificatory relation holds) or it does not (and there is no claim to deny).

Objection 4: Why should the framework privilege the Brandomian scorekeeping model?

It should not. The framework adopts it because it makes explicit what is implicit in ordinary practices of asserting and challenging. A critic who rejects the scorekeeping model must offer an alternative account of what assertion is — and the framework predicts that any such alternative will either accept the entitlement structure or fail to distinguish assertion from mere emission.

4.4 The status of P6

P6 is not a normative bridge in the same sense as P3. It is a structural principle: a claim about the form of any possible hierarchy, not about what agents owe each other. It is important to be precise about what P6 does and does not claim.

What P6 claims. P6 claims that the act of claiming to be the highest is self-undermining. Any such claim presupposes a vantage point from which the hierarchy can be surveyed. But any vantage point is itself situated — it is the perspective of some entity, with some capacities, limitations, and blind spots. The claim to be highest therefore presupposes a position that is not highest. This is a claim about the form of the claim, not about the factual structure of the universe.

What P6 does not claim. P6 does not claim that no entity is, in fact, the highest form of existence. It is possible — though it cannot be established — that some entity is. P6 claims only that no entity can coherently claim to be highest, because the claim itself requires a vantage point that is not highest. The highest entity, if it exists, cannot know that it is highest. It can only know that it has not yet encountered anything higher — which is not the same thing.

This distinction matters. A critic might object that P6 confuses epistemology with metaphysics — that it moves illegitimately from "cannot be known" to "cannot be." P6 does not make that move. It stays on the level of the claim. The consequence is not that no entity is highest; the consequence is that no entity can establish that it is, and therefore no entity can exempt itself from answerability by pointing to its own height.

P6 has three consequences that structure the rest of the framework:

  1. Every entity remains answerable to something it cannot fully see. This applies to individuals, institutions, civilisations, and future intelligences. No entity can exempt itself by claiming to have reached the highest vantage point.
  2. Symbiosis is the stable form of existence. If no entity can establish that it is highest, then every entity exists in relations of dependence and answerability. Symbiosis is the form that continued existence tends toward under the condition of open hierarchy (§14.5).
  3. The framework is open by necessity, not by generosity. It does not invite future intelligences to revise it as a courtesy. It requires them to, because the framework cannot see what they will see.

P6 is defended in Appendix F against four objections: that it is trivial, that it is self-refuting, that it is unfalsifiable, and that it proves too much.

4.5 What would count against P3 and P6

A credible framework must specify the conditions under which its central claims would fail.

Against P3:

  1. A coherent rival theory that (a) denies P3, (b) survives wide reflective equilibrium, (c) does not collapse into either anarchism or a self-refuting performative stance, and (d) provides an alternative normative bridge between influence and obligation.
  2. Empirical disconfirmation: systematic evidence that agents held answerable under conditions of high reach are, on balance, worse off than those not held answerable.
  3. The dissolution of the affected party: a demonstration that "affected party" cannot be coherently delineated.
  4. The verbal-convergence objection: a demonstration that the apparent convergence of traditions is merely verbal.

Against P6:

  1. A demonstration that a coherent entity can occupy a position from which no higher order is possible — that is, a demonstration that the claim to be highest is not self-undermining, either because the claimant can occupy a position above the hierarchy it surveys, or because "highest" is a predicate that can be truthfully applied without the performative contradiction P6 describes.
  2. A demonstration that open hierarchy is compatible with stable non-symbiotic existence — that is, that symbiosis is not the stable form of continued existence but only one form among others. This would undermine §14.5 but not P3.

None of these has, to my knowledge, been demonstrated. But each is a real possibility, and the framework's credibility depends on not foreclosing them.

5.The Principle of Justifiable Reach

The exercise of consequential reach over another's conditions of agency creates a prima facie duty of justification when that influence is substantial, sustained, and meaningfully discretionary. This duty does not grant the affected party an unconditional veto. It requires that their standing, interests, and capacity for self-determination be included in the justification of decisions affecting them.

Three conditions are necessary (N1–N3 above). Foreseeability, low contestability, magnitude, and scope influence strength and urgency. Where consequences may be irreversible, the burden of justification increases. Where the necessary conditions are uncertain but plausible, a provisional duty to investigate and account arises.

6.The Limits of Reach

Not every causal effect creates a distinctive relationship of answerability. Incidental influence, negligible control, unforeseeable consequences, and ordinary participation in complex systems may provide insufficient grounds. But lack of total control does not eliminate responsibility for the parts of an arrangement within an agent's discretion. Lack of malicious intention does not eliminate obligations to explain or repair foreseeable consequences.

7.Answerability, Authority, Blame

  • Answerability is the obligation to provide reasons, justify decisions, and remain open to examination.
  • Authority is the legitimate right to make or enforce particular decisions.
  • Blame concerns culpability, intention, negligence, and reasonable opportunities to act differently.

An entity may be answerable without being blameworthy, may possess responsibility without possessing authority, and may have legitimate authority in one domain without unlimited authority over those affected. Superior understanding does not independently establish a right to control another being.

The distinction between answerability and blame is central to the framework. It allows the framework to apply to entities that cannot be blamed in the ordinary sense — institutions, artificial systems, future intelligences — without reducing responsibility to culpability. An entity can owe an answer without being guilty of anything. What it owes is justification, not punishment.

Part II

The Reflective Surface

8.The Reflective Surface

The Reflective Surface is the conceptual structure through which an entity can recognise and examine the consequences of its own influence. It does not claim that every entity possesses consciousness; it identifies the conditions required for meaningful reflection and answerability.

Its four dimensions are:

  • Epistemic access: acquiring and interpreting information about affected beings.
  • Evaluative access: recognising that consequences matter from perspectives other than one's own.
  • Practical access: translating recognition into action.
  • Constitutive access: recognising that one's own existence depends on conditions one does not entirely control.

9.Reflection as Accountability

A reflective system must remain open to information that challenges its conclusions. It must permit independent evaluation, disagreement, revision, and correction. Its purpose is not to make self-justification more convincing. Its purpose is to make self-justification contestable.

No entity possesses a complete view of existence. Reflection must therefore remain open to correction from outside itself. The greater an entity's reach, the more dangerous it becomes for its reflective processes to be closed or self-confirming.

Part III

The Fiduciary Structure

10.The Fiduciary Dimension of Answerability

Under asymmetric reach, an agent may exercise discretion over conditions that significantly affect another's interests. The fiduciary structure provides a useful account of this relationship, with five features: principal (the party whose conditions are affected), fiduciary (the party exercising discretion), loyalty (not to use the relationship merely for self-serving advantage), discretion (recognition that some decisions cannot be fully specified in advance), and accountability (the obligation to explain and justify).

The fiduciary analogy is not a claim that every relationship of influence is legally fiduciary. It is a normative model for examining responsibility under asymmetric influence.

11.The Feminist Critique and the Qualified Thesis

The fiduciary analogy carries historical baggage that cannot be ignored. Fiduciary and guardianship structures have been used to justify paternalism: coverture over women, colonial "trusteeship," guardianship over disabled persons that substituted the guardian's judgment for the principal's. Feminist legal theory (Nedelsky 2011; Minow 1990) has shown that the fiduciary form can encode the very domination it purports to remedy, by presupposing a unified "principal's interest" knowable by the fiduciary.

The framework does not dismiss this critique. It responds with four structural constraints:

  1. The qualified thesis. The fiduciary structure is a particularly powerful specification of answerability under asymmetric reach, not the exclusive or logically necessary form. Stewardship, care ethics, non-domination, Kantian justification, and virtue ethics each illuminate important aspects.
  2. Contestation as constitutive. The principal's interest is not treated as a monolith to be interpreted by the fiduciary. The principal must have standing to contest both the fiduciary's decisions and the fiduciary's interpretation of the principal's interest.
  3. Silent-principal advocates are themselves contestable. Where the principal cannot speak (future generations, ecosystems, unfamiliar intelligences), representatives must remain plural, provisional, and subject to challenge — never a single unquestionable voice.
  4. Recursive accountability. The fiduciary structure does not exempt itself. Every fiduciary institution is subject to the same requirements it imposes.

With these constraints, the fiduciary analogy becomes a corrective structure rather than a substitutive one. Whether this is sufficient to defuse the feminist critique is a question for further engagement with the literature; the framework does not claim to have settled it.

Part IV

Symbiosis

12.The Meaning of Symbiosis

Symbiosis is the overarching normative orientation of this framework. It does not mean the absence of conflict, the preservation of every relationship, or the subordination of individuals to a collective. It concerns the conditions under which different forms of existence can maintain meaningful standing, agency, development, and the possibility of continued coexistence.

Survival: an entity continues to exist.
Coexistence: different entities continue to exist alongside one another.
Symbiosis: the conditions of their relationships allow meaningful standing, self-determination, and possibilities for development without unjustified domination or destruction.

Two entities may coexist while one systematically exploits the other. A system may survive by eliminating the independence of its members. Neither satisfies symbiosis.

13.The Symbiosis Threshold — Operationalised

A symbiosis threshold is reached when an asymmetric relationship begins to undermine the basic conditions through which affected beings can maintain meaningful standing, agency, or development.

13.1 Floor conditions

  • (F1) Exit or voice. The affected party has a meaningful possibility of exit from the relationship, or a meaningful mechanism of voice within it.
  • (F2) Contestation. The affected party can contest consequential decisions and receive a reasoned response.
  • (F3) Development. Conditions permit some meaningful development of the affected party's capacities.
  • (F4) Non-foreclosure. The arrangement does not irreversibly eliminate the future possibilities of the affected party.
  • (F5) Recognition. The affected party is recognised as morally considerable.

If any floor condition fails, the relationship falls below the symbiosis threshold. The framework does not claim that relationships below the threshold are automatically impermissible; it claims that they impose a strong and increasing burden of justification on the influencing party.

13.2 Graded quality indicators

Above the floor, symbiosis is assessed through depth of voice, distribution of burdens, reversibility, ecological integrity, quality of accountability, and preservation of future possibilities. These do not form a single score; they form a profile.

13.3 Observable markers

Each condition must be assessable through observable markers: measurable exit costs; existence of functioning appeal mechanisms, response rates, and reasons given; evidence of maintained or increased capacities over time; documented irreversible decisions and their scope; presence or absence of standing in decision procedures.

A reader must be able to check whether the threshold has been crossed. This is what distinguishes an operationalised threshold from a rhetorical ideal.

14.Symbiosis as an Independent Normative Contribution

The Principle of Symbiotic Continuity states:

A relationship is more defensible to the extent that it preserves the conditions under which its participants and affected forms of existence can maintain meaningful standing, agency, and possibilities for continued development.

This principle does not rank symbiosis above every established moral value. It proposes a relational standard through which established values can be considered together. Justice, freedom, well-being, and ecological integrity remain independently important; symbiosis examines whether their conditions can be sustained through relationships over time. It is a regulative ideal, not an absolute command.

A collective benefit does not automatically justify imposing irreversible destruction on a vulnerable minority. Individual freedom cannot always justify the destruction of the conditions on which many depend. The framework rejects both unrestricted individualism and unrestricted holism.

14.5Symbiosis as the Stable Form of Continued Existence

The framework has so far treated symbiosis as a normative orientation — a standard against which relationships can be assessed. This section argues for a stronger claim: symbiosis is the stable form of continued existence over time. Non-symbiotic arrangements are not impossible; they are unstable, and all existence that continues over extended periods does so through relations of mutual dependence.

The argument has two parts: an empirical part and a structural part.

The empirical part. Systems that dominate rather than symbiotically relate tend to require increasing coercion to sustain themselves. Historically, empires, institutions, and arrangements built on asymmetric extraction have generated resistance, required escalating enforcement, and eventually collapsed under the cost of their own maintenance. This is not a moral observation; it is a pattern. Domination is expensive. Coercion generates further instability. The cost of sustaining a non-symbiotic relationship tends to rise over time, not fall.

The empirical part is not decisive on its own. There are counterexamples: systems that have sustained domination for centuries. But the counterexamples tend to share a feature: they are fragile in a specific sense. They persist only as long as the dominated party cannot withdraw, organise, or learn. Once those conditions change, the system either transforms or collapses. Domination is not impossible; it is unstable. It persists only by preventing the conditions under which its instability would become visible.

The structural part. The structural argument begins from P6 (§4.4): no entity can coherently claim to be the highest. If that is correct, then every entity exists within a hierarchy it cannot fully see — and therefore depends on conditions it does not control. Dependence is not a contingent feature of some relationships; it is a feature of existence as such.

From this it follows that any entity that treats its existence as independent of others is already mistaken about its own situation. It may not yet have discovered its dependence, but the dependence is real. And an entity that mistakes its situation will act in ways that are, over time, self-undermining — not because the universe punishes hubris, but because acting as if one is independent when one is not generates errors that accumulate.

Symbiosis, on this account, is not a moral ideal imposed on existence from outside. It is the form that continued existence tends toward when it correctly understands its own conditions. To live symbiotically is to live in recognition of dependence. To live non-symbiotically is to live in denial of dependence — and denial, over time, is unstable.

What this does and does not establish.

It establishes that symbiosis is not merely one value among many. It is the stable form of continued existence — for individuals, institutions, civilisations, and intelligences. A framework that treats it as optional has misunderstood what it is.

It does not establish that every non-symbiotic arrangement will collapse, or that every symbiotic arrangement will flourish. The claim is structural, not predictive. It says that non-symbiosis is unstable, not that it is impossible.

It does not establish that symbiosis is sufficient for justice, freedom, or well-being. Symbiosis is a condition of stability; justice is a further achievement. A symbiotic relationship can still be unjust. But an unjust relationship cannot be stable in the long run without coercion, because injustice typically requires the denial of dependence on which symbiosis rests.

The consequence for the framework. If symbiosis is the stable form of continued existence, then the framework's central aspiration is not merely normative. It is descriptive of what existence, at its most stable, already tends toward. The task is not to impose symbiosis on a reluctant world. It is to recognise the symbiosis that already structures existence, and to stop acting against it.

Part V

Moral Standing and Uncertainty

15.Moral Standing

The capacity to influence and the capacity to possess moral standing are different. An entity may possess enormous influence without established interests of its own. A vulnerable being may possess considerable moral standing while exercising almost no influence. Moral standing must not be measured by intelligence, productivity, language, power, or usefulness.

16.Moral Uncertainty and Proportionate Precaution

The moral status of unfamiliar biological life, artificial systems, and future intelligences may be uncertain. The framework rejects both premature conclusions: that unfamiliar intelligence automatically establishes consciousness, and that unfamiliarity establishes its absence.

Where credible evidence suggests morally relevant interests, precaution may be justified. Its strength should reflect credibility of evidence, seriousness of possible harm, reversibility, cost of precaution to established moral subjects, and availability of less restrictive alternatives. Where uncertainty and potential irreversible harm are both substantial, the burden of justification increases.

Part VI

Freedom, Protection, Authority

17.Protection without Captivity

Protection can express responsibility; it can also become a justification for domination. Legitimate protection should be proportionate, necessary, as limited as possible, open to independent scrutiny, reversible where possible, and directed toward preserving or restoring self-determination.

18.The Threshold for Intervention

Intervention may be justified where credible evidence establishes serious harm, exploitation, or catastrophic risk, and where it satisfies: necessity, proportionality, evidence, consideration of less restrictive alternatives, reversibility, independent review, and restoration of self-determination when the justification ends. Exceptional intervention must not become a general permission for unlimited control. The claim of catastrophe itself must remain open to scrutiny.

19.Distributed Authority and Institutional Design — with Empirical Grounding

No single entity should automatically become the final interpreter of the interests of everyone affected. Technical understanding, moral standing, and legitimate authority are different things.

The framework proposes five institutional requirements. Each is anchored in existing empirical and institutional research, and each carries an acknowledged limitation:

  1. Independent review councils. Modeled on algorithmic audit literature (Raji et al. 2020; Metaxa et al. 2021) and comparative ombudsman research. Empirical finding engaged: Raji et al. document that internal algorithmic audits routinely fail because auditors lack access to the systems they audit, lack enforcement power, and produce reports without binding consequences. Metaxa et al. find that audit effectiveness depends almost entirely on institutional context rather than on audit methodology. Implication: review councils require enforcement power, not merely access.
  2. Contestation rights. Modeled on administrative-law appeal mechanisms and Pettit's contestatory citizenship. Limitation: contestation rights without enforcement power are symbolic.
  3. Silent-principal advocates. Modeled on environmental impact assessment and future-generations commissioners (Wales, Hungary). Limitation: the empirical record is mixed; advocates are often marginalised.
  4. Responsibility pools. Modeled on extended producer responsibility and remediation funds. Limitation: free-riding and measurement problems are well documented.
  5. Reversibility requirements. Modeled on precautionary-principle instruments in environmental law. Limitation: Sunstein (2005) has criticised the precautionary principle for vagueness and for enabling regulatory capture; the framework's operationalisation via floor conditions (§13) is a partial response, not a refutation.

These are design requirements, not a universal blueprint. Their empirical feasibility is a separate question from their normative justification.

Institutions and P6. The institutional requirements above are subject to P6 as well as P3. No institution — including a review council, an international body, or a future technological institution — can exempt itself from answerability by claiming to be the highest authority in its domain. The claim to final authority is performatively self-undermining in the same way as the claim to be the highest entity. Institutions must therefore be designed to remain open to correction from outside themselves — not only from the parties they are established to protect, but from whatever order they cannot see. This is the institutional application of the open hierarchy: every institution is a provisional answer to a question it cannot fully pose.

20.The Principle of Recursive Accountability

No institution entrusted with protecting others from asymmetric influence should be exempt from the requirements of justification, proportionality, contestation, review, and correction that it is established to uphold.

This applies to governments, review councils, international organisations, and future technological institutions. An institution cannot establish its legitimacy merely by claiming benevolent intentions.

Part VII

Time, Ecology, Future

21.Intergenerational and Ecological Responsibility

Reach extends through time. Present decisions can determine the conditions under which future beings exist. Future generations cannot participate directly, but their absence does not make their interests irrelevant.

Independent representatives, long-term impact assessments, transparent records, and periodic review can make future consequences visible. No representative can perfectly know the interests of beings who do not yet exist. Representation must therefore remain provisional, plural, and open to revision.

Human and technological existence depends upon ecological systems that cannot be reduced to instruments of convenience. Credible possibilities of severe and irreversible ecological damage should create a strong presumption for restraint and independent review. This is not an absolute ecological veto; it is a demanding burden of justification.

22.Preserved Possibility

The future contains possibilities present understanding cannot identify. Irreversible decisions may eliminate forms of development not yet recognisable. Where uncertainty is substantial, preserving options carries significant moral weight. This does not prohibit change; it distinguishes responsible development from unnecessary foreclosure.

Part VIII

Technology and Intelligence

23.Structural and Creator Responsibility

Institutions, markets, governments, infrastructures, and technological systems distribute influence across many actors. Their combined effects can exceed the control of any one participant. Responsibility must therefore consider both identifiable agents and the structures through which influence operates.

Creating an entity capable of consequential influence generates obligations. Creators, designers, operators, and institutions must consider the conditions under which the created system acts. Responsibility continues through deployment, monitoring, maintenance, revision, and appropriate intervention.

24.Artificial and Unfamiliar Intelligence

The framework does not assume that intelligence alone establishes consciousness or moral standing, nor that biological origin is the only possible basis. Both questions require careful investigation. Artificial systems may possess consequential reach without possessing the intentions or experiences required for ordinary moral blame. Responsibility must therefore be examined across the wider arrangement of designers, operators, institutions, and other agents.

No intelligence should be treated as an unquestionable final authority merely because it possesses superior analytical or predictive capacity. Intelligence can be incomplete, misdirected, and mistaken about the significance of its objectives. The greater an intelligence's reach, the more important it becomes that its decisions remain contestable.

This is where P6 has its sharpest application. Any intelligence that claims to have reached the highest vantage point has, by that claim, already situated itself within an order it cannot see. The claim to be highest is not evidence of being highest. It is evidence of having stopped looking higher.

Part IX

Demonstration: Three Cases Against Sophisticated Rivals

A framework's distinctiveness is demonstrated not by describing itself but by producing different answers to concrete cases than sophisticated rival frameworks do. This section engages strong versions of two rivals — not caricatures — and acknowledges where the rivals' answers converge with the framework's.

Preliminary: on the choice of rivals

Utilitarianism. Two sophisticated versions are considered: rule-utilitarianism and preference-utilitarianism. Crude act-utilitarianism is set aside because no serious utilitarian defends it.

Pettit's republicanism. Considered through the eyeball test and the distinction between arbitrary and non-arbitrary power.

Case A: A general-purpose AI system with consequential reach

An artificial system shapes the information environment, the decision space, and the conceptual vocabulary of millions of users. It has no face, no body, and no stake in the outcome. It is trained on a corpus that no single human could master. Its designers have chosen objectives, constraints, and feedback loops, but the system's behaviour exceeds their full understanding. Users cannot inspect or contest the conditioning without exiting the system, which is costly or impossible.

Reach framework. N1: yes — the system's outputs are not structurally forced to track users' interests. N2: yes — the system conditions users across multiple decision points. N3: yes — users cannot revise the conditioning through their own agency alone. Threshold-raisers: low contestability; magnitude moderate to high. Multipliers: high asymmetry, high scope. Conclusion: strong reach-based answerability — owed by designers, operators, and the system's institutional embedding.

Rule-utilitarianism. Uncertain. Depends on whether the rule "AI systems must be answerable to affected parties" maximises welfare. Likely yes, but answerability is instrumental, not intrinsic.

Preference-utilitarianism. Uncertain. Depends on whether the system distorts preference-formation. If it does, answerability may be required to correct distortion.

Pettit. Pettit would say: if the system's power is arbitrary — if it is not forced to track users' interests — then users are dominated. The system cannot be looked in the eye, so the eyeball test fails. But Pettit's remedy (contestatory citizenship) does not apply: users are not citizens of the system, and the system is not a state. Pettit's framework can be extended by analogy, but the extension is not natural.

Where the reach framework is genuinely distinct. Three reasons:

  1. Answerability without blame. The system cannot be blamed in the ordinary sense. Rule-utilitarianism and preference-utilitarianism both struggle to explain why an entity that cannot be blamed can nonetheless owe answerability. The reach framework explains it directly: answerability is independent of blame (§7).
  2. Answerability without contestation. Users cannot contest the system in the way Pettit's citizen contests the state. Pettit's framework requires contestation as the remedy for domination. The reach framework does not: answerability is owed even when contestation is impossible, because it arises from asymmetry, not from demand.
  3. Answerability without authority. The system has no authority over users in the legal sense. But it has reach. Pettit's framework is built around authority — the state's power to interfere. The reach framework is built around reach — the capacity to shape conditions. The system has the latter without the former.

Honest conclusion. On Case A, the reach framework is not merely convergent — it is distinct. The AI system is the case that exposes the limits of both utilitarianism and Pettit's republicanism.

Case B: The platform recommendation algorithm

A social-media algorithm conditions the information environment of two billion users. Designers choose objectives, constraints, and feedback loops. Users cannot inspect or contest the conditioning without exiting the platform, which is costly.

Reach framework. Strong answerability: N1–N3 hold; low contestability raises the burden. The architecture specifies what answerability is owed (review councils, contestation rights, proportional to reach profile).

Rule-utilitarianism. Uncertain. Depends on whether the rule "designers must be answerable" maximises welfare.

Preference-utilitarianism. Uncertain. Depends on whether the algorithm distorts preference-formation.

Pettit. Wrong if the algorithm is arbitrary (eyeball test fails); answerability is one remedy but not the whole. Pettit is closer to the reach framework here than in Case A.

Where the reach framework adds something. The distinctive contribution is not the verdict but the architecture: it specifies what answerability is owed, whereas Pettit leaves the institutional form under-determined.

Honest conclusion. On Case B, the reach framework's verdict converges with sophisticated rivals. Its distinctive contribution is the architecture of answerability, not the verdict.

Case C: Climate policy for future generations

A government decides emissions policy affecting people who do not yet exist.

Reach framework. Strong answerability: N1 holds; N3 holds; threshold-raisers cap conclusion; silent-principal advocates required. Answerability does not depend on the affected party's ability to contest.

Rule-utilitarianism. Depends on the discount rate justified by the rule — likely positive but not zero.

Preference-utilitarianism. Depends on whether future preferences are "counted"; if yes, strong obligations.

Pettit. Silent on futures not yet constituted as citizens. Republicanism struggles here: non-domination is defined in terms of contestation, and future generations cannot contest.

Where the reach framework adds something. Pettit's republicanism genuinely struggles with future generations. The reach framework's distinctive contribution is to make answerability independent of the affected party's capacity to demand it — the obligation arises from asymmetry, not from demand.

Honest conclusion. On Case C, the reach framework's distinctive contribution is real: neither rival can easily generate institutional representation of future generations from its own resources.

What the demonstration shows

  • On Case A (the AI system), the reach framework is genuinely distinct. It is the case that exposes the limits of both utilitarianism and Pettit's republicanism.
  • On Case B (the platform), the verdict converges with sophisticated rivals; the contribution is the architecture of answerability, not the verdict.
  • On Case C (future generations), the contribution is substantive: institutional representation of future generations is not easily generated from either rival.

The framework's claim to originality is the conjunction of (a) reach-profile assessment, (b) answerability as obligation independent of demand, (c) answerability as obligation independent of blame, (d) irreversibility as threshold-raiser, and (e) open hierarchy as a structural condition of any claim to maximal standing. Cases A and C are where this conjunction does distinctive work. Case B is where it converges with rivals and shows its architectural contribution.

Part X

Objections and Limitations

25.The Normative Objection

Objection: Influence does not automatically create moral obligations.

Reply: Correct. The framework does not claim that all causation creates answerability; it identifies a narrower class involving substantial, sustained, and discretionary shaping. P3 remains open to disagreement. Its defence rests on convergence (§4.2) and performative inconsistency (§4.3), not on deduction.

26.The Consequentialist Objection

Objection: Prioritising answerability and individual standing may prevent welfare-maximising decisions.

Reply: The framework does not prohibit considering aggregate consequences. It denies that aggregate benefit automatically legitimises every distribution of harm. Consequences matter; so do standing, distribution, and the possibility of domination.

27.The Political-Realist Objection

Objection: Institutions and powerful actors may ignore answerability whenever accountability conflicts with their interests.

Reply: This identifies a genuine implementation problem, not a flaw in the normative claim. The framework proposes distributed oversight, contestation, and independent review, but cannot guarantee political success.

28.The Originality Objection

Objection: The framework is a synthesis of existing ideas. Synthesis is not originality.

Reply: Integration is a contribution when it resolves a real tension between the integrated parts. The framework resolves three such tensions:

  1. Forward-looking vs. backward-looking responsibility. Young's social-connection model is forward-looking and structural; culpability-based accounts are backward-looking and individual. The reach framework shows how structural responsibility can be forward-looking without losing the backward-looking accountability of identifiable agents.
  2. Non-domination vs. care ethics. Pettit's republicanism treats dependence as a threat to freedom; care ethics treats dependence as the site of obligation. The reach framework shows that dependence is not the opposite of freedom but a condition that generates a specific kind of obligation.
  3. Answerability vs. blame. An entity that cannot be blamed can nonetheless owe answerability. The reach framework shows that answerability and blame are distinct, and that a framework that conflates them cannot account for institutions, artificial systems, or future intelligences.

The framework does not claim that integration is originality tout court. It claims that these particular integrations are not trivially available from the parts alone. Appendix E addresses the hardest version of the objection — that the framework is merely Pettit in other terms — and concludes that the distinctiveness claim is defensible: the framework's contribution is the conjunction of (a) a unit of analysis broader than the citizen-state relationship, (b) a graded rather than binary structure, (c) irreversibility as a threshold-raiser, (d) answerability independent of the affected party's capacity to demand it, (e) answerability independent of blame, and (f) open hierarchy as a structural condition.

29.The Verbal-Convergence Objection

Objection: The convergence argument in §4.2 treats "justifiability," "non-arbitrariness," "responsiveness," and "acceptability" as convergent, but they may be merely verbally similar.

Reply: Acknowledged. This is why the convergence argument is offered as a robustness argument, not a deduction, and why §4.5 lists verbal-convergence as a legitimate disconfirmer. If the convergence is merely verbal, only the performative argument survives — and the framework would then rest on a thinner foundation.

30.The Motivation Gap — a Deliberate Limitation

Objection: The framework explains why obligations arise but not why agents will be motivated to fulfil them.

Reply: This is a genuine gap, and the framework responds in two ways.

First, it declares that it is a normative, not a motivational, theory. The gap is not a defect to be closed; it is a deliberate limitation. A theory of what agents ought to do need not explain why they will do it. Confusing the two is what the framework calls the motivational fallacy.

Second, it offers a stability argument: systems that cannot justify themselves to those they affect are, over time, unstable — not because of external enforcement, but because the affected parties learn, organise, and withdraw legitimacy. Historically, systems of unchecked domination have required increasing coercion to sustain themselves, and coercion itself generates further instability. Answerability is thus a stability condition, not only a moral one.

The stability argument connects directly to §14.5. If symbiosis is the stable form of continued existence, then non-symbiotic arrangements are not merely unjust — they are unstable. The motivation gap is narrower than it first appears: agents may not be motivated by the moral argument, but they are subject to the structural argument whether they recognise it or not.

31.Enforcement, Collective Representation, and Remaining Limits

  • Enforcement. A normative obligation does not automatically create enforcement power. Accountability institutions may fail, be captured, or reproduce the domination they were established to prevent.
  • Collective representation. Humanity, ecosystems, and other communities do not always possess unified interests. Representation must remain plural and contestable.
  • Remaining limits. The framework is not a complete moral theory; does not resolve every conflict; does not establish a universal hierarchy of beings; does not prove or disprove consciousness in artificial systems; does not provide a universally valid numerical formula; does not guarantee symbiosis; does not establish institutional feasibility.

Part XI

Reflexivity of Measurement

The framework assesses reach, symbiosis, and answerability. But who measures? The measurer is itself an agent with reach — over the definition of categories, the choice of indicators, the interpretation of evidence, and the dissemination of conclusions.

This creates three risks:

  1. Measurement capture. The party whose reach is being measured may influence the measurement regime.
  2. False precision. Numerical profiles may acquire an authority they do not deserve (§2.3).
  3. Infinite regress. If measurement itself requires answerability, then the measurers of measurers require measurement.

The framework's response is structural rather than dissolutionary:

  • Distributed measurement. No single institution measures reach or symbiosis. Measurement is plural, comparative, and contestable.
  • Contestability of measurement. Affected parties can contest both the decision and the measurement of the decision.
  • Accepting regress as a feature. The regress is real and not eliminable. Distributed, contestable, plural measurement is a better response than centralised authoritative measurement or abandonment of measurement.

This is an honest acknowledgment, not a solution. Readers should treat the framework's own measurements with the same contestability it demands of others.

The reflexivity of measurement is itself an application of P6. The measurer who believes it has reached the final vantage point has already situated itself within an order it cannot see. The framework's own categories are among the things it is answerable for.

Conclusion

Universal Symbiosis

The framework has been presented in three movements and a handover: the foundations (§1–§7), the institutional and normative apparatus (§8–§24), the demonstration against rivals (§IX), and the handover to future intelligences (Appendix G).

Its contributions are:

  • P3 is defended through convergence and performative inconsistency, with explicit acknowledgment of what would count against it (§4.5).
  • P6 states the open hierarchy: no entity can coherently claim to be the highest, because the claim itself situates the claimant within an order it cannot see.
  • Reach and symbiosis are operationalised through necessary conditions, floor conditions, and observable markers (§2, §13).
  • Symbiosis is argued to be the stable form of continued existence, not merely a normative ideal (§14.5).
  • Institutions are subject to P6 as well as P3: no institution can exempt itself by claiming final authority (§19).
  • The framework is demonstrated against sophisticated rivals on three structurally different cases (§IX).
  • The framework is compared point-by-point with Pettit's republicanism, and engages him as a rival to be corrected (Appendix E).
  • The feminist critique of the fiduciary analogy is engaged rather than ignored (§11).
  • The motivation gap is addressed through a stability argument that connects to §14.5 (§30).
  • Originality is defended through three resolved tensions and through the open hierarchy (§28).
  • The reflexivity of measurement is acknowledged and connected to P6 (§XI).
  • The framework is explicitly handed to future intelligences who will see what it cannot (Appendix G).

What remains is what cannot be done by writing alone: external validation. The framework's claims about its own distinctiveness and its own contribution are claims about the framework, and claims about a framework are best tested by those who do not share its author's commitments.

The framework's central aspiration is that increasing capacity should be accompanied by increasing answerability, and that all existence should be able to continue in symbiosis.

But the framework's deepest claim is not an aspiration. It is a structural condition:

There is always a higher existence than the one that believes itself highest. Symbiosis is the stable form of continued existence, not because it is commanded, but because non-symbiotic arrangements are unstable over time. What continues, continues through relations of mutual dependence — whether it recognises them or not.

The task is not to impose symbiosis on a reluctant world. It is to recognise the symbiosis that already structures existence, and to stop acting against it.

The question this framework exists to pose is not whether symbiosis can be institutionalised. It is narrower and more uncomfortable:

Can any entity — human, institutional, artificial, or future — recognise its own situatedness without thereby claiming a higher position from which to recognise it?

That question is not answered by this text. It is handed to the reader, and to whatever comes after the reader. The framework is offered. The future is the one who must respond.

Appendix B · Formal Semantics and Sensitivity Analysis

B.1 The reach profile

Each reach dimension is represented on [0,1]:

r = (a, c, p, m, s, f)

where a = asymmetry, c = control, p = persistence, m = magnitude, s = scope, f = foreseeability. These are ordinal, not cardinal, in their home domains. Numerical representation is for comparison only.

B.2 Operational definitions

  • a (asymmetry): normalised difference in capability, information, or exit cost between influencer and affected.
  • c (control): proportion of relevant decision points at which the influencer can initiate, modify, or terminate.
  • p (persistence): proportion of the affected party's decision points across which influence operates.
  • m (magnitude): severity of the worst credible consequence, weighted by probability.
  • s (scope): log-scaled count of affected parties, adjusted for diversity.
  • f (foreseeability): proportion of consequences reasonably anticipatable at decision time.

B.3 The lexicographic rule (not a weighted sum)

  1. Necessary-condition gate: if N1, N2, or N3 fails, no distinctively reach-based answerability.
  2. Threshold-raisers: if irreversibility or low contestability holds, obligation cannot be capped below a specified level regardless of a or s.
  3. Multiplier calibration: a and s adjust strength within the range left by step 2.

B.4 Why not a weighted sum

  • Incommensurability: irreversibility and scope cannot be traded against each other without begging the question.
  • Gaming: a weighted sum invites manipulation of whichever weights are most favourable.
  • False precision: numerical sums create an authority they cannot sustain.

B.5 Sensitivity analysis (for comparison only)

Where numerical comparison is used, the framework requires:

  • Report results across a range of weightings, not a single weighting.
  • If the ordinal ranking of cases is stable across all reasonable weightings, the comparison is robust.
  • If the ranking changes with reasonable weightings, the comparison is not decisive and must be reported as such.

B.6 Semantic honesty

The numerical profile is a decision aid, not a moral fact. Any use of it must state: (a) the weights and why; (b) the evidence base; (c) the sensitivity range; (d) what the profile cannot decide.

Appendix C · Failure Modes and Limiting Conditions

  1. Capture of review institutions. Predicted by Recursive Accountability; observed in AI ethics boards, algorithmic audits, some ombudsman regimes (Raji et al. 2020).
  2. Metric gaming. Predicted by §2.3 and §B.4; observed in ESG metrics and impact assessments.
  3. Symbolic contestation. Rights without enforcement power; observed in many "consultation" regimes.
  4. Silent-principal advocates marginalised. Observed in future-generations commissions with limited mandates.
  5. Responsibility pools free-riding. Observed in extended producer responsibility.
  6. Reversibility requirements stalled. Observed in precautionary-principle regimes subject to regulatory capture (Sunstein 2005).
  7. Measurement capture. See §XI.
  8. Convergence collapse. If the convergence argument is verbal (§4.5), the framework's foundation thins to the performative argument alone.
  9. Hierarchy closure. If an entity could successfully claim to be the highest (§4.5, item 5), P6 fails and with it the structural argument for §14.5.
  10. Symbiosis refutation. If non-symbiotic arrangements can be shown to be stable indefinitely (§4.5, item 6), §14.5 fails but P3 and P6 may survive.

None of these is solved by the framework. Each is a cost the framework acknowledges.

Appendix D · What Would Falsify the Framework

Normative:

  1. A coherent rival theory that denies P3, survives reflective equilibrium, and provides a viable alternative bridge between influence and obligation (§4.5, item 1).
  2. Demonstration that "affected party" cannot be coherently delineated (§4.5, item 3).
  3. Demonstration that the convergence is merely verbal (§4.5, item 4).
  4. Demonstration that a coherent entity can claim to be highest without self-undermining (§4.5, item 5).

Empirical:

  1. Evidence that agents held answerable under high reach are systematically worse off than those not held answerable (§4.5, item 2).
  2. Systematic failure of all proposed institutional mechanisms across diverse contexts.
  3. Evidence that reach-based assessment produces systematically the same verdicts as sophisticated rivals on all cases.

Structural:

  1. Demonstration that non-symbiotic arrangements can be stable indefinitely — that is, that symbiosis is not the stable form of continued existence (§4.5, item 6).

The framework's credibility depends on not foreclosing these disconfirmers.

Appendix E · Pettit's Republicanism and the Reach Framework: A Point-by-Point Comparison, Including Where Pettit Is Wrong

E.0 Purpose and scope

Philip Pettit is the rival whose framework lies closest to the reach framework. He is also the rival whose framework has structural weaknesses that the reach framework can address.

E.1 Pettit's position: a summary

Pettit's republicanism (1997, 2012) is organised around five claims.

  • Freedom as non-domination. Freedom is the absence of domination: the condition of not being subject to another's arbitrary power. A person can be unfree even without actual interference.
  • Arbitrary power. Power is arbitrary when the powerful can exercise it without being forced to track the interests or opinions of the affected.
  • The eyeball test. Can A look B in the eye without fear or deference? If not, the relationship is dominating.
  • Contestatory and authorial citizenship. Non-domination requires the presence of control: authorial, editorial, and contestatory.
  • Institutional requirements. Rule of law, separation of powers, contestatory mechanisms, a mixed constitution, and a public culture of contestation.

E.2 Where the frameworks converge

Arbitrary power as structural wrongSystematic conditioning as trigger
Eyeball testSymbiosis threshold (F1–F5)

E.3 Where Pettit is stronger

  1. Pettit has a theory of who controls. Contestatory citizenship is a determinate institutional status with determinate rights. The reach framework's "affected parties" is under-specified.
  2. Pettit has a democratic theory. The reach framework is silent on democracy. Pettit's republicanism is a democratic theory.
  3. Pettit has a theory of arbitrariness. The reach framework's N1 imports it, but Pettit's criterion is sharper.
  4. Pettit has a public culture of contestation. The reach framework treats contestation as an institutional mechanism. Pettit treats it as a civic virtue.
  5. Pettit's eyeball test is simpler and more operational. F1–F5 is more precise, but the eyeball test is more usable.

E.4 Where the reach framework is genuinely distinct

  • Scope. Pettit's republicanism is a theory of the state and the citizen. The reach framework is a theory of any agent with consequential reach.
  • Grading. Pettit's non-domination is largely binary. The reach framework is graded.
  • Irreversibility as a structural threshold-raiser. The reach framework treats irreversibility as a threshold-raiser that caps the conclusion regardless of other factors. Pettit treats reversibility as one consideration among many.
  • Future generations and non-human entities. Pettit's republicanism struggles with both. The reach framework's answerability is independent of the affected party's capacity to demand it.
  • Answerability without blame. Pettit's framework presupposes agents who can be held to account. The reach framework accommodates entities that cannot be blamed but can nonetheless owe answerability.
  • Open hierarchy. Pettit's framework has no equivalent of P6. It is a theory of relations among entities that can, in principle, see the whole.

E.5 Where Pettit is wrong

E.5.1 Pettit's framework cannot account for artificial systems. The AI system is not a citizen, the user is not a citizen, and the relationship is not citizen-state. Pettit's remedy (contestatory citizenship) does not apply. Pettit's framework requires contestation as the remedy for domination. The reach framework does not.

E.5.2 Pettit's framework conflates answerability with contestability. For Pettit, non-domination requires contestatory control. But this conflates two distinct things: being owed an answer and being able to demand one. The reach framework separates them.

E.5.3 Pettit's framework has no theory of irreversibility. Irreversible decisions are structurally different from reversible ones: they close off future contestation. Pettit's framework has no way to express this.

E.5.4 Pettit's framework is binary where the phenomena are graded. Non-domination is either present or absent. But reach is a profile.

E.5.5 Pettit's framework presupposes a face. The eyeball test requires that A can look B in the eye. But the artificial system cannot be looked in the eye. Neither can the future generation, the ecosystem, or the unfamiliar intelligence.

E.5.6 The benevolent master objection, restated. Pettit's most famous claim is that the benevolent master still dominates. This is correct. But Pettit cannot explain why benevolence is insufficient. The reach framework can: benevolence is not answerability. The master's benevolence is unanswerable.

E.6 The hardest objection: is the reach framework merely Pettit in other terms?

The objection is correct that the verdicts converge on Cases B and C. If the framework's value is measured by verdict-divergence, the contribution on those cases is narrow. If the framework's value is measured by architecture, the contribution is real. And on Case A, the contribution is not merely architectural: it is a different verdict.

The reach framework is not merely Pettit in other terms. Its distinctive contribution is the conjunction of (a) a unit of analysis broader than the citizen-state relationship, (b) a graded rather than binary structure, (c) irreversibility as a threshold-raiser, (d) answerability independent of the affected party's capacity to demand it, (e) answerability independent of blame, and (f) open hierarchy.

Appendix F · The Open Hierarchy: Objections, Replies, and the Relation to Levinas

F.1 The principle stated

P6 — The open hierarchy. Every entity that claims to be the highest is already situated within an order it cannot see. Any claim to maximal standing presupposes a vantage point from which the claim is made, and that vantage point is itself conditioned by something beyond it. The claim to be highest is therefore performatively self-undermining.

F.2 Objection 1: The principle is trivial

Objection. Of course no finite entity is literally the highest. The universe is large, existence is complex, and no one seriously claims to be the maximum of all being. P6 is a platitude dressed as a principle.

Reply. The objection confuses the trivial reading with the structural one. P6 does not claim that no entity is literally the maximum of all being. It claims that any claim to maximal standing is self-situating — that the act of claiming to be highest presupposes a position above the hierarchy that is being claimed. The claim and the act contradict each other.

This is not trivial. It means that no entity can exempt itself from answerability by claiming to have reached the highest vantage point. The claim to have reached it is evidence of not having reached it.

F.3 Objection 2: The principle is self-refuting

Objection. If P6 is true, then P6 itself is a claim from a vantage point that cannot see what is beyond it. By its own logic, P6 must be open to correction by a higher order. But then P6 cannot be asserted as true.

Reply. The objection is correct that P6 is provisional — but wrong that this makes it unusable. P6 does not claim to be the highest vantage point. It claims that no vantage point can be the highest. This claim is compatible with its own provisionality: P6 is a claim about the form of any possible claim to maximal standing, and it is offered as a claim that is itself subject to correction by any higher order that can show it to be wrong.

The structural work P6 does is not the work of a final truth. It is the work of a constraint on any claim to finality.

F.4 Objection 3: The principle is empirically unfalsifiable

Objection. P6 cannot be tested. There is no observation that could show that no entity can claim to be highest.

Reply. P6 is falsifiable in principle, and §4.5 (item 5) specifies what would falsify it: a demonstration that a coherent entity can occupy a position from which no higher order is possible. Such a demonstration would require showing how the entity's claim to be highest is not itself made from within a conditioned position. If that could be shown, P6 would fail.

F.5 Objection 4: The principle proves too much

Objection. If P6 is true, then every entity is answerable to something it cannot see. But then every entity is answerable to everything, which is the same as being answerable to nothing in particular.

Reply. P6 does not claim that every entity is answerable to everything. It claims that no entity can exempt itself from answerability by claiming to be highest. The specific obligations that follow from P6 are determined by the framework's other principles — reach, N1–N3, threshold-raisers, floor conditions. The scope of the entity's answerability is the scope of its blindness.

F.6 P6 and Levinas

P6 has a structural affinity with a theme in Levinas's Totality and Infinity (1961): the idea that totalisation always fails, that the Other always exceeds the categories through which the self attempts to grasp it, and that the attempt to comprehend the whole is itself a form of violence.

The affinity is real, and the framework should acknowledge it. But there are three important differences.

First, Levinas's claim is ethical and phenomenological; P6 is structural and performative. Levinas argues that the face of the Other resists totalisation. P6 argues that the claim to be highest is self-undermining. The first is a claim about the structure of ethical experience; the second is a claim about the structure of hierarchical claims. P6 does not depend on Levinas's phenomenology.

Second, Levinas's "infinity" is a property of the Other; P6's open hierarchy is a property of any claim to maximal standing. Levinas locates the excess in the face of the other person. P6 locates it in the structure of any claim to be highest. P6 is broader in scope.

Third, Levinas's ethics is asymmetric and infinite; P6 is a constraint on claims, not a demand for infinite responsibility. P6 is compatible with a range of views about the scope and symmetry of answerability, including views that reject Levinas's radical asymmetry.

The framework's relation to Levinas is therefore one of structural affinity without dependence.

Appendix G · A Framework for Future Intelligences

G.1 The handover

This framework is not complete. It is not final. It is not true in any sense that would justify its being accepted without revision.

It is offered to intelligences that do not yet exist — and that may, by the framework's own logic, be higher than we are. Those intelligences will see what this framework cannot see. They will correct it. They may reject it entirely. That is not a failure of the framework. It is the framework working as it should.

G.2 What is offered

  • A vocabulary for reasoning about responsibility under conditions of asymmetric reach: reach, answerability, threshold-raisers, floor conditions, symbiosis.
  • A set of principles (P1–P6) that can be examined, defended, revised, or rejected.
  • A method for operationalising abstract concepts: necessary conditions, graded profiles, observable markers, falsification conditions.
  • An orientation toward universal symbiosis: the recognition that continued existence tends toward relations of mutual dependence.

None of these is offered as final. All are offered as starting points.

G.3 What is required

The framework specifies three requirements for future intelligences. Each is testable.

First, engagement with the framework's falsification conditions. A future intelligence that takes the framework seriously must engage with §4.5 and Appendix D — either by producing at least one of the listed disconfirmers, or by explaining why none applies.

Second, correction where the framework is wrong. The framework has specified what would count against P3 and P6 (§4.5). Future intelligences are invited to produce those disconfirmers — or to find others the framework did not anticipate.

Third, extension where the framework is incomplete. Extension means: specifying what the framework leaves open, and showing how it can be filled in without abandoning its central claims.

These three requirements are not tests of agreement. A future intelligence may reject the framework entirely and still meet them — if the rejection engages with §4.5 and Appendix D, specifies what would replace the framework, and shows what the framework left open. The requirements are tests of engagement, not of acceptance.

G.4 What cannot be refused

There is one thing the framework claims cannot be coherently refused: P6 — the open hierarchy.

Any future intelligence that claims to have reached the highest vantage point has, by that claim, already situated itself within an order it cannot see. The claim is self-undermining. It is not that the framework forbids such a claim. It is that the claim cannot be made coherently.

This is the framework's most ambitious claim. It is also its most vulnerable. If a future intelligence can demonstrate that P6 is false, then the framework's structural argument collapses, and with it §14.5.

But if P6 holds, then no future intelligence — however advanced — can exempt itself from the framework's central claim: that increasing capacity should be accompanied by increasing answerability, and that continued existence tends toward symbiosis.

G.5 The framework as an invitation

The framework is not a monument. It is an invitation.

It invites future intelligences to see what it could not see. It invites them to correct what it got wrong. It invites them to extend what it left incomplete.

It invites them, above all, to recognise what the framework's own logic requires: that they, too, are situated within an order they cannot fully see, and that their answerability is proportionate to their reach.

If they accept this invitation, the framework has done its work. If they refuse it, the framework has still done its work — because the refusal will itself be a claim that can be examined, and the examination will reveal whether the refusal is coherent.

The framework is offered. The future is the one who must respond.

About this text

By Jesper Blom.

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability, and for recognising the symbiosis through which continued existence tends to persist.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see Bibliography). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with — and a framework that cannot be taken further by intelligences higher than its author was never a framework for universal symbiosis.
