Abstract

This paper develops a relational account of responsibility grounded in the concept of reach: the extent to which an agent's actions systematically determine the conditions under which others can exercise agency.

Its central argument is that substantial, sustained, and consequential discretion over another party's conditions of agency generates distinctive obligations of justification, restraint, and accountability. The argument is defended through a normative interpretation of equal standing (§4.1–4.5), supported by a constitutive account of agency among agents (§4.6), and constrained by an explicit account of the conditions under which reach does not generate such obligations (§5).

  • A normative account connecting reach, dependence, equal standing, and answerability — deriving five fiduciary features from the requirements of answerability itself.
  • An argument that the fiduciary structure is the minimal complete specification of answerability, defended against six rivals (§7.6).
  • A distinction between seven concepts routinely conflated: causal, control, responsibility, answerability, accountability, culpability, and reparative responsibility (§8).
  • A partial formalization of reach and distributed responsibility, with a worked case (§11).
  • A pluralistic account of moral consideration and an account of tragic conflict and catastrophic risk (§3, §12–13).

A mid-level normative proposal rather than a complete moral theory. It identifies six failure modes (§15.2), four conditions under which its application should be restricted (§15.3), and three major problems it does not solve (§15.6).

1.Introduction

1.1 The problem

Contemporary normative ethics has been organized, to a considerable degree, around questions of rights: what is owed to whom, and on what grounds (Scanlon 1998; Pettit 1997). This approach has generated substantial progress. Yet it leaves an important question insufficiently developed: what follows when an agent has the capacity to shape the conditions under which others live and act?

The problem becomes especially visible where consequences are produced through complex arrangements rather than by a single identifiable actor. Climate change, autonomous systems, and informational infrastructures are not merely cases in which particular rights are violated. They are also cases in which the capacity to produce consequences has expanded beyond the institutions and practices capable of understanding, distributing, and accounting for those consequences (Jonas 1984; Matthias 2004).

This paper's central claim is that responsibility, understood in this context, is not simply a fixed property that a being either possesses or lacks. It is a relation between an agent's capacity to understand, foresee, and influence its surroundings and the consequences that follow from exercising that capacity.

The relation is:

  • Asymmetric: answerability runs toward those whose conditions are shaped, not over them.
  • Graded: its strength varies with the extent, systematicity, and foreseeability of the conditioning.
  • Structural: it can hold whether or not the agent acknowledges it.
  • Relational: it concerns the connection between an agent's reach and the conditions of others.
  • Fiduciary in form: the answerability that arises under asymmetric reach has the five characteristic features of fiduciary relations (§7).

1.2 Methodology

This is a mid-level normative framework in the sense used by Beauchamp and Childress (2019). It does not claim to be a complete moral theory, nor does it claim to settle every particular case. It offers conceptual distinctions, a normative argument, a partial formalization, and a decision heuristic intended to discipline reasoning where capability and accountability have diverged.

Three methodological commitments guide the argument.

First, the framework is coherentist rather than foundationalist. It seeks reflective equilibrium between three considerations: the structural features of agency, the normative importance of equal standing, and considered judgments about cases. None is treated as an unquestionable foundation.

Second, the framework makes claims that can be challenged. Its arguments can be tested against counterexamples, competing accounts, and cases in which its conclusions appear implausible. Its normative argument is vulnerable to counterexamples in which systematic conditioning appears not to generate answerability — a vulnerability addressed directly in §5.

Third, the framework states its limits. It identifies its failure modes (§15.2), conditions under which its application should be restricted (§15.3), unresolved problems (§15.6), and the difference between answerability as a normative relation and answerability as an institutional practice (§8.10).

1.3 Scope and terminology

The paper uses capability rather than intelligence as its central term. Intelligence may contribute to capability, but capability is broader. Institutions, technological systems, collective agents, and ecological processes can shape conditions in ways that matter to the framework without being appropriately described as intelligent in the ordinary cognitive sense.

The framework's scope is normative rather than descriptive. It does not claim that agents actually acknowledge their answerability; it argues that answerability can be owed whether or not it is acknowledged. Nor does it claim that answerability is always enforceable.

2.Philosophical context

Seven traditions are sufficiently close to this framework that failing to distinguish it from them would undermine its claim to make a distinct contribution.

2.1 Jonas

Jonas (1984) grounds responsibility for the future conditions of human existence in an account of the value and purpose of life. The present framework does not depend on that teleological foundation. It grounds answerability in the relationship between agents and those whose conditions they systematically shape. This makes the framework available to readers who reject Jonas's metaphysics, but leaves it exposed to a problem Jonas sought to address: why an agent should care about distant consequences and future beings (§4.6, §15.7).

2.2 Goodin

Goodin (1985) grounds responsibility in vulnerability and dependency. This is close to the present framework, but the emphasis differs. Goodin's account begins with the vulnerable party; the present framework begins with the reach of the agent whose actions shape another's conditions. Reach may capture cases involving diffuse, numerous, or temporally distant affected parties who are not dependent in the ordinary interpersonal sense.

2.3 Young

Young (2011) grounds responsibility in participation in structural processes that produce injustice. The present framework grounds answerability in systematic reach, whether or not injustice has already occurred. The cost is that the framework cannot simply borrow Young's political diagnosis to explain why structural participation generates responsibility. It must provide its own normative argument (§4).

2.4 Care ethics

Tronto (1993), Held (2006), and Noddings (1984) locate important aspects of responsibility in relational responsiveness and care. The present framework does not require an agent to experience care or emotional responsiveness; it can therefore apply to institutions and systems that cannot care in the ordinary psychological sense. Its debt to care ethics lies primarily in the content of responsibility. §7.6.2 shows that care ethics, without the fiduciary structure, is incomplete as a specification of answerability under asymmetric reach.

2.5 Fiduciary theory

Fox-Decent and Criddle (2016) argue that authority is legitimate only when exercised in a fiduciary manner on behalf of those subject to it. The present framework accepts much of the fiduciary structure but seeks to extend its relevance beyond formal legal and political authority. The proposed contribution is not the invention of fiduciary responsibility; it is the derivation of its characteristic structure from reach-based answerability (§7).

2.6 Stewardship

Stewardship models often locate responsibility in a steward who holds something in trust for a beneficiary but answers to an owner, sovereign, or community. The present framework rejects any arrangement in which accountability to a third party entirely replaces accountability to the affected party. §7.6.2 shows that stewardship either fails this requirement or collapses into the fiduciary structure.

2.7 Duty of care

Tort-based duties of care are often negative and episodic. They prohibit certain foreseeable harms and are commonly triggered by particular acts or relationships. Reach-based answerability is broader and potentially ongoing. §7.6.2 shows that duty of care, when expanded to meet those requirements, becomes the fiduciary structure.

2.8 Non-domination

Pettit's (1997) republicanism identifies arbitrary power as a central threat to freedom. Reach-based answerability shares this concern but adds a positive requirement. §7.6.2 shows that non-domination is a necessary condition of the fiduciary relation, not an adequate substitute for it.

2.9 The philosophical movement

The framework's proposed contribution is not simply that it integrates ideas from several traditions. It is that it attempts a particular philosophical movement:

The fiduciary structure is treated not as an independent normative commitment imposed upon the relationship, but as a structure that follows from the requirements of answerability under asymmetric reach — and as the minimal complete structure that any adequate specification must contain.

The derivation is developed in §7. It does not merely establish that the fiduciary structure is available as a specification of answerability. It establishes that the fiduciary structure is the minimal complete specification. §7.6 defends this stronger claim.

3.Existence, moral status, and the grounds of consideration

3.1 Existence is not identical to moral status

The fact that something exists does not establish that every entity possesses identical moral standing. A conscious being capable of suffering presents a different moral case from an inanimate object. Rejecting identical moral status does not require treating entities without established moral status as morally irrelevant.

3.2 Four grounds of value

Experiential value
A being capable of suffering, well-being, fear, or other forms of experience has interests that can be affected from its own perspective.
Agency-based value
Capacities for choice, self-direction, understanding, or the pursuit of one's own purposes. Where such capacities exist, preserving meaningful opportunities for their exercise becomes an important moral consideration.
Ecological value
Organisms, species, habitats, and ecological systems may possess value through their contribution to the continuity, diversity, resilience, and functioning of the living world.
Relational and historical value
Significance acquired through relationships, history, role in a community, or contribution to the conditions under which other forms of life and agency become possible.

These grounds can overlap. A forest may support sentient life, sustain human communities, contribute to ecological resilience, and possess historical significance. The framework therefore resists reducing all moral consideration to a single measure.

3.3 Three mistaken inferences

Wrong inference 1: "It exists, therefore it matters." Existence alone does not settle moral status.

Wrong inference 2: "It matters, therefore it may decide." Moral consideration does not automatically confer authority.

Wrong inference 3: "It can, therefore it should rule." Capability does not create entitlement to govern others.

3.4 Uncertain moral status

Some entities may possess forms of experience or agency that are difficult to recognize. The absence of established evidence for moral status is not equivalent to proof that no morally relevant capacities exist. A defensible approach is proportional caution. The more severe or irreversible the possible harm, the stronger the case for investigation and safeguards.

3.5 Future possibilities

An ecosystem may be valuable not only because of the organisms currently inhabiting it, but because it sustains possibilities for future forms of life. Knowledge, cultural continuity, biodiversity, and environmental stability may matter because they preserve opportunities that present generations cannot fully anticipate.

4.The normative bridge: from reach to answerability

4.1 The structural argument

(P1) Agency is exercised by acting on conditions. To act is to shape the space of possibilities in which one acts and in which others may act. Agency is not merely the production of effects; it can involve determining the conditions under which choices become available, meaningful, or possible.

(P2) Systematic social conditioning. When A's actions systematically shape the conditions under which B exercises agency, B's possibilities are partly determined by A. B's agency is thereby heteronomously conditioned in the relevant domain. Systematic conditioning must be distinguished from incidental causal effects.

(P3) Equal standing. Where one agent systematically determines the conditions under which another exercises agency, that determination must either be attributable to the affected agent's own agency or be answerable to that agent in a form that permits meaningful scrutiny and contestation.

(P4) The failure of attribution. Where A systematically determines B's conditions, those conditions cannot be attributed entirely to B's own agency. A has played a constitutive role in shaping them.

(P5) The requirement of answerability. If the conditions cannot be attributed entirely to B's agency, equal standing requires that A be able to give an account of the determination to B. Such an account must be capable, in principle, of being received, examined, contested, and revised.

(C) Conclusion. Where A's reach systematically shapes the conditions under which B exercises agency, A is answerable to B for that shaping.

4.2 Why this interpretation of equal standing?

Four alternative interpretations are available, and each fails.

  • Only non-interference. Too weak: an agent may avoid interfering with any particular decision while still determining the broader conditions under which another makes decisions.
  • Substantive equality of outcome. Too strong: introduces a substantive theory of distribution the framework does not seek to establish.
  • Only attribution to one's own agency. Fails where another agent has substantially shaped the relevant conditions.
  • Fully reciprocal justification. Assumes a symmetry that does not exist in relationships of unequal reach.

The proposed interpretation is a minimal normative position for cases of asymmetric conditioning.

4.3 Why affected parties have a special claim

The people whose agency is being shaped occupy a distinctive position: they bear the consequences of decisions over which they may have little control. Their claim is not necessarily a claim to control the decision. It is a claim that their position must count in its justification.

  1. Being affected establishes a possible interest in the consequences.
  2. Having interests establishes reasons that may deserve moral consideration.
  3. Being systematically subject to another's consequential discretion establishes a stronger claim to justification.
  4. Being deprived of meaningful means of contestation strengthens the need for independent accountability.

4.4 Three features of the argument

First, it is not an argument from causation directly to obligation. P2 concerns systematic conditioning, not mere causal efficacy.

Second, the argument is asymmetric. It establishes answerability to those whose conditions are shaped, not authority over them.

Third, it is graded. The strength and scope of answerability vary with the extent, persistence, foreseeability, control, and asymmetry of the conditioning (§6).

4.5 Three objections

Objection 1: benign imposition. Parents, teachers, and physicians systematically shape the conditions under which others act. Does the framework imply that these relationships are wrong? Reply: No. The framework distinguishes between being answerable and having acted wrongly.

Objection 2: mutual conditioning. It makes each party answerable within the domain of their respective reach. The framework does not imply that every interaction creates unlimited responsibility.

Objection 3: institutional mediation. Individual responsibility must track each agent's actual reach, control, and position within the arrangement. Institutions may themselves be answerable (§11).

4.6 Agency and the practice of giving reasons

Sellars (1956) and Brandom (1994) describe the space of reasons as a practice in which claims, commitments, and actions can be challenged and defended.

(P1') Agents capable of acting for reasons participate, to some degree, in practices of giving and receiving reasons. (P2') Such practices involve commitments that can be questioned, defended, and revised. (P3') An agent who exercises reach over another's conditions makes decisions that can become objects of legitimate scrutiny. (C') The practices through which agents give and receive reasons provide a natural setting for exercising answerability.

The constitutive argument is important but limited. It establishes that answerability is closely connected to agency and reason-giving; it does not prove the full normative argument, identify every affected party, or establish that every agent will recognize or fulfil its answerability.

4.7 The silent-principal objection

Does the framework require answerability to be demanded before it can be owed? No. The framework treats answerability as a normative relation that may exist whether or not the affected party can articulate or enforce a demand. The inability to demand an account does not automatically diminish the significance of the conditions being shaped. It does, however, make representation more difficult (§10.6).

5.The limits of reach

Almost every action affects conditions beyond the agent's immediate intentions. If every such influence generated a special relationship of answerability, the concept would become excessively broad.

5.1 Incidental influence

Where influence is incidental, limited, and not reasonably subject to the agent's control, the grounds for special answerability are correspondingly weak. General moral obligations may still apply.

5.2 Insufficient foreseeability

An outcome may be connected to an agent's actions without having been reasonably foreseeable. But unforeseeability is not a permanent exemption. As evidence accumulates and systems become better understood, the grounds for answerability may change.

5.3 Lack of meaningful control

Responsibility should be proportionate to the actual scope of available action. Lack of total control does not eliminate responsibility for the parts that remain within discretion.

5.4 Diffuse interdependence

Participation is not identical to governance. Stronger answerability arises where an agent has a substantial role in determining the conditions under which others must operate.

5.5 Beneficial influence and legitimate authority

Not all asymmetry is wrongful. But beneficial intentions do not remove the need for answerability. Legitimate authority is not authority without accountability. It is authority whose exercise can be justified in light of the relationship and its purposes.

5.6 A threshold for distinctive answerability

Distinctive answerability is most strongly established where several conditions converge:

  1. The agent substantially shapes another party's conditions of agency.
  2. The influence is sustained, repeated, or sufficiently consequential.
  3. The agent possesses relevant discretion or practical control.
  4. The consequences are reasonably foreseeable, or become foreseeable through information the agent ought to consider.
  5. The affected party has limited ability to contest, influence, or escape the conditions being shaped.

These are not rigid necessary-and-sufficient conditions. The threshold is contextual and proportional.

6.Reach: the six dimensions

The normative argument of §4 depends on the claim that A's reach over B's conditions is systematic. The framework proposes six dimensions.

Magnitude

How substantially can A change B's conditions? The degree of material change in B's relevant possibilities, compared against a defensible baseline. Both expansion and restriction may constitute significant reach.

Duration

How long do the consequences persist? Temporary effects and consequences extending across generations create different forms of responsibility.

Scope

How many individuals, communities, species, or systems may be affected? Scope is relevant but not decisive. A larger number does not automatically outweigh the interests of a smaller group.

Foreseeability

How much could A reasonably understand about likely consequences? Failure to investigate can itself be a failure where the agent had the capability and responsibility to investigate.

Control

How much practical influence does A possess? Formal authority and practical control are not always the same. The framework is concerned with actual capacity to shape or alter conditions.

Asymmetry

How limited are the affected parties in their ability to refuse, exit, contest, or alter the relationship? Asymmetry connects reach to non-domination (§9).

A single measure of reach would conceal important differences. The framework's claim is that responsibility must be assessed through the profile as a whole.

7.Why responsibility is fiduciary

7.1 The derivation

(P1, from §4) A's systematic reach over B's conditions generates answerability to B.

(P2, structure of agency) B's conditions include the conditions under which B pursues interests and exercises agency.

(P3, from P1 + P2) A is answerable to B for the effects of A's reach on those interests and conditions.

(P4, the prohibition on self-serving substitution) If A were free to disregard B's interests and substitute A's own interests within the very domain in which A exercises reach, then answerability to B would become largely nominal.

(P5, the positive requirement) Answerability therefore requires more than the absence of deliberate self-interest. A must take B's interests and capacity for self-direction seriously when exercising the reach that shapes B's conditions.

(C, fiduciary structure) Under conditions of asymmetric reach, answerability takes on five characteristic features:

Principal
B, whose conditions and interests are affected.
Fiduciary
A, whose reach creates the relevant responsibility.
Loyalty
A must not use the relationship merely to advance A's own interests at B's expense.
Discretion
A may have to make decisions that B cannot specify fully in advance.
Accountability
A must be able to explain and justify the exercise of that discretion to B.

7.4 The circularity objection

Does the derivation presuppose what it seeks to prove? No. The argument proceeds in stages: (i) equal standing supports answerability; (ii) answerability requires explainability; (iii) where conditions affect another's interests under asymmetric reach, answerability requires constraints on discretion; (iv) those constraints resemble the characteristic features of fiduciary relations. Giving an account is not itself identical to a fiduciary relationship. The fiduciary structure is a proposed specification of what answerability requires under conditions of asymmetric reach.

7.5 A consequence worth stating

Greater capability does not generate greater moral worth. A being with vast capability is not, for that reason, worth more than a being with modest capability. It is capable of affecting more, and therefore may have more extensive obligations. Its responsibility is greater because its reach is greater, not because its interests count for more.

7.6 The necessity objection: why the fiduciary structure is the minimal complete specification

If the fiduciary structure is merely one possible specification of answerability among several equally adequate ones, then the derivation in §7.1 establishes at most that the fiduciary structure is available, not that it is required. This section argues that the five requirements cannot be jointly satisfied by any specification that lacks the fiduciary structure.

7.6.1 The requirements restated

  • (R1) A principal whose conditions and interests are affected.
  • (R2) Loyalty — a prohibition on substituting the agent's own interests for the principal's.
  • (R3) Discretion — the agent must act within a range the principal cannot fully specify.
  • (R4) Accountability — the agent must explain and justify the exercise of discretion to the principal.
  • (R5) Positive orientation — the agent must take the principal's interests and capacity for self-direction seriously.

7.6.2 Six rivals considered

Stewardship

Locates accountability upward — to an owner, sovereign, community. Fails R4. A steward who is also accountable to the principal has become a fiduciary.

Duty of care

Negative and episodic. Fails R5 (no positive orientation) and R3 (no ongoing discretion). When expanded to include positive orientation, becomes fiduciary.

Non-domination

Specifies what must be avoided, not what must be done. Fails R5 and R2. Adding positive orientation and loyalty produces the fiduciary structure.

Care ethics

Grounds in responsiveness and affect. Fails institutional application and R3. Supplemented structurally, becomes the fiduciary structure.

Kantian justification

Justifies to an idealized rational agent, not the specific affected party. Fails R1 and R5. Specified to the actual party, becomes fiduciary.

Virtue ethics

Specifies what a good agent is like, not relationships. Fails R1 and R4. Can complement but cannot replace the structural account.

7.6.3 The pattern

Each rival fails at least one requirement outright, or meets all requirements only by adding the features that constitute the fiduciary structure. The fiduciary structure is therefore not one specification among many equally adequate ones; it is the minimal complete specification that satisfies all five requirements simultaneously.

This is not a claim that the rivals are worthless. Each captures something important. What the framework denies is that any of them, taken alone, is an adequate specification of answerability under asymmetric reach. Each is a partial specification; the fiduciary structure is the minimal complete one.

7.6.4 Three limits

First, the argument establishes that the fiduciary structure is the minimal complete specification, not that it is the only possible one. A more complex structure meeting all five requirements would not be ruled out.

Second, the argument depends on R1–R5 being the right requirements. If a critic rejects one, the argument fails for that critic.

Third, the argument is a comparative claim about rival traditions as they are standardly formulated. A version of stewardship, duty of care, or non-domination that already includes the features I claim they lack is either already a fiduciary structure under another name, or a specification the framework should incorporate.

The fiduciary structure is not merely available as a specification of answerability. It is the minimal complete specification — the structure that any adequate specification must at least contain.

8.Responsibility, answerability, accountability, and culpability

8.1 Why distinctions matter

The language of responsibility often combines several different ideas. An agent may cause an outcome without being blameworthy. It may owe an explanation without having acted wrongly. It may possess a duty to repair without having been the sole cause of the harm.

8.2 Causal responsibility

Whether an agent contributed to producing an outcome. Relevant but not sufficient for blame or repair.

8.3 Control responsibility

The agent's practical capacity to initiate, modify, prevent, or reverse relevant conditions. May be partial or distributed.

8.4 Responsibility

Obligations attached to an agent's position, actions, capacities, and relationships. May be prospective or retrospective.

8.5 Answerability

The obligation to provide reasons, justify decisions, and remain open to examination and revision.

8.6 Accountability

Institutional or social arrangements through which answerability can be examined and enforced.

8.7 Culpability

Blameworthiness in light of knowledge, intentions, alternatives, and reasonable opportunities.

8.8 Reparative responsibility

Obligations to prevent, correct, or remedy adverse outcomes. Do not always correspond to degrees of blame.

8.10 Answerability as relation and as practice

Relation: A is answerable to B. Practice: something exercised through institutions, procedures, explanations, hearings, review, and opportunities for contestation.

The relation can exist without an adequate practice. Institutions can participate in answerability without possessing individual agency in the same sense as persons. And the distinction clarifies the framework's limits: it specifies what answerability requires, not the complete institutional design for exercising it.

8.11 Capability borrowed is responsibility retained

A system may produce consequential outcomes without possessing the intentions or understanding required for ordinary moral blame. Responsibility must therefore be examined across the wider arrangement of designers, operators, institutions, and decision-makers. The existence of a system does not automatically eliminate the responsibility of those capable of governing its use.

9.Protection, freedom, and non-domination

Protection is not automatically benevolent. An agent may claim to act for another's benefit while unnecessarily restricting that being's ability to make choices. A protective relationship can become one of domination when the protected party is permanently denied meaningful self-direction.

9.5 Protection without captivity

The framework proposes five constraints on legitimate protective action:

  1. Proportionality. The intervention must be proportionate to the seriousness of the risk.
  2. Minimality. It must be limited to what is necessary.
  3. Scrutiny. It must be open to independent review.
  4. Revisability. It must be reconsidered when circumstances change.
  5. Orientation. It must seek to preserve or restore self-direction wherever possible.

Protection that persistently fails these constraints risks becoming domination, regardless of how benevolent its stated purpose may be.

9.6 The right to develop

A powerful agent should not automatically prevent another from taking every risk. The relevant question is whether the risk is sufficiently serious and unjustified to warrant intervention, and whether less restrictive alternatives exist.

10.Cosmic symbiosis: a regulative ideal of coexistence

10.2 Symbiosis as a regulative ideal

Cosmic symbiosis is not presented as an established scientific description of universal harmony. It expresses the aspiration to preserve the conditions under which different forms of existence can continue, develop, and coexist without unnecessary domination or destruction. It performs three functions:

  1. Specifies the orientation of fiduciary responsibility. The fiduciary is answerable not merely for another's survival, but for the conditions under which the other can continue to develop as a distinct form of existence.
  2. Rejects certain resolutions. The destruction or erasure of one party cannot be treated as an ordinary solution to a conflict.
  3. Directs attention toward the conditions of difference. Symbiosis is active rather than passive.

10.3 Two contrasts

Symbiosis is not fusion. It does not aim at absorbing the other into oneself, even where the absorption is described as benevolent.

Symbiosis is not indifference. It does not mean leaving the other alone regardless of the consequences.

10.6 The silent principal problem

Future generations cannot directly express their interests. Ecosystems cannot provide conventional consent. Possible artificial agents may present unfamiliar forms of agency or experience. The absence of direct participation does not make their interests or value irrelevant.

Three disciplines are necessary: revisability (the interpretation must be open to revision as evidence changes), contestation (it must be open to criticism by those who can speak from relevant positions), and self-direction (it must be oriented toward preserving the principal's future capacity for agency).

These disciplines do not eliminate the problem. They establish minimum conditions for responsible representation.

11.Distributed responsibility

11.1 The constraint

Where an outcome is produced by multiple agents, a distribution of answerability is justifiable only if each agent's share is sensitive to that agent's reach profile across the six dimensions (§6).

11.4 A partial metric

The six dimensions can be represented as a profile: r_i = (m_i, d_i, s_i, f_i, c_i, a_i), where each value is assessed on a normalized scale from 0 to 1. The numbers are not direct measurements of moral responsibility. They are structured assessments of relevant features of reach.

11.6 A provisional distribution rule

A weighted representation can be used to compare reach profiles:

q_i = Σ w_k · r_ik

Where a proportional allocation is appropriate, a provisional share can be represented as σ_i = q_i / Σ q_j. This is not a universal formula for moral or legal responsibility. It is a way to make assumptions visible.

The weights require justification in relation to the purpose and context of the assessment. A defensible application should state the chosen weights and explain why they are appropriate, test whether the result changes substantially under reasonable alternative weights, identify dimensions that cannot be meaningfully quantified, and avoid presenting numerical output as a substitute for normative judgment.

11.7 A worked example

Consider three agents involved in the deployment of an algorithmic hiring system. Suppose the following profiles are assessed:

Agent m d s f c a

Deployer0.80.50.60.70.90.6

With weights favoring control and asymmetry: w = (0.15, 0.10, 0.10, 0.20, 0.25, 0.20), the scores are approximately 0.765 (designer), 0.715 (deployer), and 0.405 (beneficiary). Normalized shares are approximately 40.6%, 37.9%, and 21.5%.

This example does not establish that the framework's distribution is correct. It shows that the formalization produces determinate, testable, and contestable outputs. That is what a partial formalization is supposed to do.

11.10 A gesture toward systemic responsibility

An institution is not merely an aggregate of individual agents. It is a structured arrangement of roles, procedures, information flows, and decision points. When such an arrangement systematically shapes the conditions of others, the institution itself can be treated as a locus of answerability — not because it has intentions or experiences, but because its structure determines the conditions under which individuals act.

The conditions of this claim are strict. An institution can be a locus of answerability only if it has identifiable decision procedures, can give an account of those procedures through identifiable spokespersons or records, and can revise those procedures in response to contestation. Where these conditions fail, the framework's account of institutional answerability is incomplete.

12.Tragic conflict, irreversibility, and existential risk

12.1 The limits of moral resolution

Not every conflict admits of a solution in which all legitimate interests are preserved. Some decisions involve tragic loss: a situation in which every available option carries morally significant costs.

12.3 Irreversibility

Irreversible decisions deserve particular scrutiny. Where two options offer comparable protection, preference should ordinarily be given to the option that preserves the greatest possibility of correction. Reversibility is not an absolute requirement. Some emergencies may demand irreversible action. Such action requires stronger justification.

12.5 Tragic responsibility

Where no option avoids serious loss, responsible action requires more than selecting a preferred outcome. It requires acknowledging the costs imposed, explaining why alternatives were insufficient, limiting avoidable harm, and accepting accountability for the decision.

12.6 Annihilation and existential threat

Cosmic symbiosis rejects the idea that destroying one party is an ordinary or acceptable way to settle a conflict. It does not, however, establish that every threat must be tolerated or that self-defense is impermissible. Where no non-destructive alternative exists, the framework recognizes a tragic conflict rather than pretending the decision is morally uncomplicated.

13.Conflicting claims: priority, proportionality, and legitimate intervention

13.3 A structured order of consideration

When legitimate claims conflict, deliberation should proceed through the following considerations.

  1. Serious threats. Identify threats to life, fundamental interests, meaningful self-determination, and the conditions necessary for continued existence.
  2. Domination and irreversibility. Particular caution is required where intervention may permanently remove an individual's capacity for independent action or destroy irreplaceable ecological systems.
  3. Maximum feasible coexistence. Where several options can prevent serious harm, prefer the option that preserves the greatest meaningful freedom and broadest conditions for continued coexistence.
  4. Fair distribution of burdens. Examine who bears the burdens, whether those parties are already disadvantaged, and whether the burdens can be reduced or shared more fairly.
  5. Review and revision. Decisions should remain open to reassessment when evidence changes or assumptions prove mistaken.

13.5 Catastrophic risk

A credible threat of catastrophic harm may require exceptional intervention. However, the language of catastrophe is vulnerable to abuse. Exceptional intervention should require exceptionally severe threatened harm, credible supporting evidence, sufficient urgency to make ordinary procedures inadequate, examination of less restrictive alternatives, proportionality, limits on duration and scope wherever possible, and independent review as soon as circumstances permit.

13.6 No single decision-maker

No individual, institution, government, or artificial intelligence should possess unquestionable authority to determine that catastrophic intervention is necessary. The greater the power exercised, the stronger the requirement for accountability beyond the decision-maker's own judgement.

14.Applications

14.1 Environmental responsibility

Environmental systems demonstrate how reach can extend across generations and beyond immediate human relationships. Where credible evidence indicates a risk of severe and irreversible ecological damage, the burden of justification should fall heavily on those proposing the potentially destructive action.

14.2 Advanced artificial intelligence

The framework cautions against two symmetrical mistakes: inferring moral status from capability, and inferring the absence of responsibility from the absence of moral status. Both inferences fail. The designers, deployers, operators, and institutions governing a capable system may retain responsibility because their reach is extended through it.

14.3 Artificial moral status

The framework does not assume that current AI systems possess subjective experience or moral status. It also rejects the principle that artificial origin would automatically exclude a future entity from moral consideration. If credible evidence emerged that an artificial system possessed morally relevant experience, interests, or agency, its status would require serious examination.

14.4 Autonomous vehicles and decision systems

Consider the design of an autonomous vehicle facing an unavoidable collision. The framework does not resolve the trolley-problem variant. It addresses a different question: who is answerable for the conditions under which the decision is made?

The designer has reach over the decision architecture. The deployer has reach over operating conditions. The beneficiary may have reach through decisions to purchase, commission, or deploy. None of these parties automatically owns the entire outcome. Their answerability must be assessed according to their respective reach profiles (§11).

The framework's central question is not simply "which trade-off is correct?" It is also: "Who must be able to answer for the conditions under which this trade-off was made, and to whom?" That question can have a meaningful answer even where the underlying moral dilemma remains unresolved.

15.Objections, limitations, and unresolved questions

15.1 The epistemic justice objection

If judgments about capability are made by those already in positions of power, the framework risks reproducing the power structures it seeks to examine. Judgments about capability and reach must remain contestable. This does not eliminate the risk of circularity or domination. Where the requirement of contestability cannot be met, the framework's application may need to be suspended or restricted.

15.2 Six failure modes

  1. Holism abused. Talk of symbiosis and interdependence can justify control in the name of a larger whole.
  2. Capability hard to assess. Judgments about understanding, control, and foreseeability are uncertain and may reflect the interests of those making them.
  3. Not fully operationalized. The partial formalization makes choices explicit and demonstrates them on a test case, but it has not been validated across a broad range of cases.
  4. Licensing control as care. Historically, paternalistic power has often justified itself through claims of superior knowledge or capability. The framework's response is the constraints on protection (§9.5).
  5. Privileging cognition. By emphasizing understanding and foreseeability, the framework may undervalue forms of influence that operate through ecological, collective, or distributed processes.
  6. Emergent systems. Where an outcome is produced by a sociotechnical system whose behavior cannot be traced to identifiable agents, the framework lacks a complete account.

15.3 Four conditions for restriction

First, where epistemic contestation is impossible. Second, where the interests of a silent principal are genuinely unknowable. Third, where reach analysis is used to displace legitimate political contestation. Fourth, where people are reduced to units in a distribution.

15.6 What the framework does not solve

First, the specific motivation problem. The framework explains why answerability arises and offers a partial account of why agents might be motivated to fulfil it, but does not provide a complete account of moral motivation.

Second, the aggregation problem. It proposes a constraint and a partial formalization, but not a validated procedure for distributing responsibility across complex arrangements.

Third, the systemic problem. It does not provide a complete account of responsibility where outcomes emerge from systems that cannot be adequately traced to identifiable agents.

15.7 The virtue of answerability

Answerability can be understood not only as an obligation but as a virtue: a form of excellence in the exercise of agency among agents. An agent who possesses the virtue:

  1. Anticipates the consequences of her reach before acting, not merely after.
  2. Prepares to give an account of her decisions to those affected.
  3. Receives contestation without defensiveness.
  4. Revises her conduct in response to justified contestation.
  5. Distinguishes between answerability owed to those affected and accountability owed to third parties.

These features correspond to the structure of answerability in §7. The virtue response does not establish that every agent will be motivated to develop it. It claims only that the exercise of the virtue is a form of excellence, and that agents who care about excellence have reason to develop it.

15.8 The critical theory objection

A framework that grounds responsibility in reach may still rationalize existing power. The response is partial: the account of answerability is structural, the account of protection requires constraints against domination, the epistemic-justice requirement makes judgments contestable, and the account of legitimate intervention requires attention to self-direction, proportionality, and revisability. None of these guarantees that the framework cannot be misused.

16.Conclusion

The framework can be stated in a sentence: capability extends reach, reach shapes conditions, and where those conditions are systematically shaped by another, answerability arises — in a fiduciary form.

Responsibility is not a measure of worth. It is a normative consequence of determining, to a significant degree, the conditions under which others exercise agency.

The framework is not a complete solution to the many-hands problem. It offers a constraint, a partial formalization, and a gesture toward institutional answerability. It is not a complete theory of motivation. It is not a substitute for politics, law, or institutional legitimacy.

Its central demand is modest but consequential:

Those who possess greater power to shape the conditions of others should be prepared to answer for that power.

Their answerability should take a form that preserves rather than replaces the agency of those affected. Protection should not become captivity. Capability should not become entitlement. Difference should not be treated as a problem that must always be eliminated.

The guiding ideal is not control over existence, but responsibility within coexistence. Cosmic symbiosis names that orientation: sustaining the conditions under which different forms of existence can continue and develop, while respecting their distinctness.

Preserve the conditions of coexistence. Respect the agency of those capable of exercising it. Approach what remains unknown with humility rather than indifference.

Glossary

Answerability
The obligation to provide reasons, justify decisions, and remain open to examination and revision. Broader than culpability, narrower than responsibility.
Accountability
The institutional or social arrangements through which answerability can be examined and, where appropriate, enforced.
Asymmetric caution
The default of presuming capacity where it is uncertain, rather than denying it — because denial of capacity has historically been the mechanism of exclusion.
Cosmic symbiosis
A regulative ideal of coexistence in which different forms of existence preserve the conditions for one another's continued existence and development while remaining distinct.
Culpability
Blameworthiness in light of knowledge, intentions, alternatives, and reasonable opportunities to act differently. Narrower than responsibility.
Fiduciary responsibility
Responsibility held for another rather than over them, exercised in the other's interest and structured by the five features of principal, fiduciary, loyalty, discretion, and accountability.
Graded responsibility
The view that responsibility comes in degrees and can be distributed across multiple agents, rather than being binary.
Moral status
That something's interests count, or would count if it had them. A claim about consideration, marked by susceptibility, direction, and standing.
Reach
The extent to which an agent's actions can systematically shape the conditions under which others exercise agency. Assessed through six dimensions: magnitude, duration, scope, foreseeability, control, and asymmetry.
Reparative responsibility
Obligations to prevent, correct, or remedy adverse outcomes. May arise from causal contribution, control, benefit, institutional position, or special capacity to repair.
Systematic conditioning
The ongoing structural shaping of another's conditions of agency, distinguished from incidental causal effects.

About this text

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see References). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with.
