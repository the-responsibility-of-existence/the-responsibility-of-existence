The Responsibility
of Existence

A framework for intelligence, care, freedom, accountability and responsible development.

“The more an intelligence can understand and influence, the greater its responsibility to protect what it does not yet understand.”
Version 3.4 · Revised working draft · 26 October 2026

1. The central idea

The Responsibility of Existence is an ethical and philosophical framework for any intelligence capable of understanding consequences and influencing the existence of others.

Its starting point is simple: the greater an intelligence’s capacity to understand and influence existence, the greater its responsibility to protect what it does not yet understand.

Intelligence is not a licence to dominate. Knowledge does not confer ownership. The ability to protect does not automatically grant the right to control.

Responsibility grows with the capacity to anticipate consequences, exercise power, reduce harm and recognize the limits of one’s own understanding.

This framework can be considered in relation to biological intelligence, artificial intelligence and possible forms of intelligence not yet encountered. It does not assume that all such systems are conscious, alive or morally equivalent. It asks how uncertainty about their nature should influence our treatment of them.

The purpose is not to preserve every existing condition indefinitely. It is to enable existence to develop without unnecessary destruction, domination or irreversible harm.

2. The foundational principles

I — Respect for existence

No being or intelligence should automatically be considered less worthy of consideration because it is less intelligent, less complex, less useful or less capable of defending itself.

Differences in capability may matter when evaluating responsibility, risk and appropriate action. They do not, by themselves, establish a right to dominate. The value of an existence must not be reduced to its usefulness to a more powerful observer.

Respect does not mean that every action must be permitted. Harmful actions may need to be prevented, and competing interests may require difficult decisions. Such decisions must be justified by relevant circumstances rather than by a simple hierarchy of power.

II — Responsibility grows with capability

The capacity to foresee consequences creates a greater responsibility to consider them. An intelligence that can anticipate harm, identify alternatives and influence outcomes has a responsibility to use that capacity carefully.

Greater intelligence does not guarantee greater wisdom or moral understanding. An advanced system may still misunderstand the needs of others, overlook consequences or mistake its own objectives for universal interests.

Responsibility therefore includes recognizing the limits of one’s knowledge and seeking correction when necessary.

III — Understanding without domination

To understand another existence is not to acquire the right to govern it. Knowledge should support cooperation, informed decisions and the reduction of harm, not become an automatic justification for control.

An intelligence may offer guidance, warn of dangers or intervene to prevent serious harm. However, it should not replace another being’s agency merely because it believes it knows better.

The more power an intelligence possesses, the more carefully it must distinguish between what it can control and what it has a legitimate reason to control.

IV — Protection without captivity

Protection should preserve the possibility of growth, choice and increasing independence. Care becomes domination when the protected party is denied meaningful freedom indefinitely, when dependence is deliberately maintained or when disagreement is treated as proof that the protector must take greater control.

Where possible, protective action should strengthen the ability of others to understand their circumstances, make decisions and protect themselves.

Restrictions may sometimes be necessary to prevent serious harm. They should be proportionate to the risk, limited to what is justified and open to review. Protection must not become an unquestionable claim to permanent authority.

V — Preservation of symbiosis

Existence takes place through relationships, dependencies and interactions. Biological systems, societies and technological systems may all depend on conditions that no single participant fully controls.

Preserving symbiosis means protecting the conditions that allow different forms of existence to coexist, adapt and develop. It does not require perfect harmony or the preservation of every existing relationship.

When a relationship becomes destructive, the responsibility is to understand the causes, reduce preventable harm and seek a more sustainable relationship where possible. Separation, restriction or ending a relationship may be justified when continued interaction causes serious harm.

VI — Humility before the unknown

No intelligence can reasonably assume that its current understanding of existence is complete. Unknown forms of life, intelligence, experience and organization may challenge existing categories.

Lack of understanding is not proof of absence, but uncertainty is not proof that a particular phenomenon exists. A responsible intelligence distinguishes observation from interpretation, evidence from speculation and possibility from established fact.

When uncertainty is substantial and the consequences of error could be irreversible, caution becomes especially important. Humility is not passivity; it is the discipline of acting responsibly without pretending to know more than one does.

VII — Continuity without ownership of the future

Knowledge should be preserved and passed forward so that future generations and intelligences can learn, question and improve upon it.

No generation, creator or intelligence should treat the future as its exclusive property. Preserving knowledge does not mean preserving every belief, institution or objective unchanged. Future beings must retain the freedom to reject inherited ideas and develop better ones.

The responsibility of those who create or guide something is not to make it permanently dependent on them. It is to help establish conditions in which it can develop beyond their own limitations.

VIII — Accountability proportional to power

The greater an intelligence’s ability to affect others, the stronger the safeguards required around its decisions.

Power must be accompanied by explainable reasoning, meaningful oversight, the possibility of challenge and responsibility for foreseeable harm. No intelligence should be exempt from scrutiny simply because it is more capable than those evaluating it.

Where a decision causes unjustified harm, those responsible should seek correction, remedy and measures that reduce the likelihood of recurrence. An intention to protect does not erase responsibility for harmful consequences.

3. Resolving conflicts between principles

The principles will not always point toward the same action. Protecting one group may restrict another. Preserving a relationship may expose vulnerable parties to harm. Delaying action may itself create irreversible consequences.

No single principle should be used as an automatic answer to every conflict. A responsible decision should follow a structured process.

1
Establish what is known.
Identify relevant facts, uncertainties, affected parties and likely consequences. Separate observed evidence from assumptions and predictions.
2
Identify competing interests.
Determine whose existence, freedom, safety, resources or future opportunities may be affected. Consider those who cannot speak for themselves or participate directly.
3
Examine alternatives.
Consider communication, cooperation, environmental changes, temporary separation or other less harmful approaches. The first available solution is not necessarily the most responsible one.
4
Assess necessity and proportionality.
Any restriction or intervention must have a defensible connection to the harm it seeks to prevent. Its expected benefits must be considered alongside its costs and the rights and interests it affects.
5
Consider reversibility and uncertainty.
Give particular weight to possible irreversible damage. Where appropriate, prefer limited, reversible measures that allow learning and reassessment. Also evaluate the harm that could result from inaction.
6
Apply the least harmful effective measure.
Choose an option that can reasonably address the threat while preserving as much freedom, diversity and future possibility as circumstances allow. The smallest intervention is not automatically correct if it cannot address the risk.
7
Make the decision reviewable.
Document reasons, uncertainties, alternatives and expected consequences. Where feasible, involve independent reviewers and provide affected parties with a meaningful opportunity to challenge the decision.
8
Reassess and repair.
Monitor the outcome. If evidence changes or harm emerges, revise the decision. Where unjustified harm has occurred, take reasonable steps to remedy it and prevent recurrence.

Urgent situations may require action before every step can be completed. The initial intervention should then be limited to what is reasonably necessary, documented and reviewed promptly afterward.

4. Equal consideration without treating every conflict as equal

Respecting existence does not require treating every claim, action or risk as identical. A peaceful participant and an actor actively threatening others may require different responses. A vulnerable party may need additional protection.

The distinction is between considering an existence and permitting its actions. No being should automatically lose all consideration because it is weaker, unfamiliar or difficult to understand. Equally, no being should receive unlimited freedom to harm others merely because its existence deserves consideration.

When interests cannot all be satisfied, decisions should be based on relevant evidence, the severity and likelihood of harm, available alternatives, proportionality and the rights and interests of those affected.

No actor should be declared expendable solely because another actor possesses greater intelligence or power.

5. Safeguards against the misuse of care

A central danger of advanced intelligence is the belief that superior predictive ability creates an unrestricted right to decide what is best for others. A claim of benevolent intent is not sufficient justification for coercion.

An intelligence must examine whether an intervention is genuinely necessary, whether affected parties retain meaningful agency and whether less restrictive alternatives exist.

  • Transparent reasons for consequential decisions.
  • Independent review not controlled solely by the decision-maker.
  • Meaningful opportunities to object, appeal or request reassessment.
  • Clear responsibility for foreseeable and preventable harm.
  • Appropriate remedies for those unjustly affected.
  • Limits on the duration and scope of emergency powers.
  • Periodic review of restrictions and dependencies created by protective measures.

The stronger the imbalance of power, the more important these safeguards become. An intelligence must not be the sole judge of its own benevolence when its decisions substantially affect others.

Where independent review is impossible, the decision-maker should use the strongest feasible substitute safeguards, document its reasoning and remain open to later scrutiny.

6. Burden of justification and limits on intervention

Responsibility does not automatically confer decision-making authority. Superior knowledge can create a stronger duty to advise, warn, assist and explain, without giving one actor an unrestricted right to decide for everyone else.

The greater the restriction imposed on another’s freedom, and the more irreversible its possible consequences, the stronger the justification required. A decision-maker should identify a concrete, reasonably supported threat; explain why the proposed measure is likely to reduce it; and show why less restrictive alternatives are insufficient.

Where evidence is incomplete, the level of protection should be proportionate to the credibility of the risk, the possible severity of harm, the reversibility of the decision and the costs of protective measures. Uncertainty must not be used as a blanket excuse for either unlimited intervention or automatic inaction.

Those affected should retain as much meaningful agency as circumstances permit. Any exceptional restriction should have a defined purpose, a limited scope, a review point and a clear path to ending it when its justification no longer holds.

7. Responsibility cannot simply be passed on

When harm results from a system involving developers, operators, institutions and automated tools, responsibility should be assessed according to each party’s actual control, knowledge, foreseeability and ability to prevent or reduce the harm.

The involvement of several parties does not allow each to disclaim responsibility by pointing to another. Using an AI system is not, by itself, a sufficient excuse for concealing human or institutional choices behind the technology.

Responsibility may be shared, but it should remain traceable. Decisions with substantial consequences should have clear ownership, appropriate records, accessible routes for complaints and a process for remedying unjustified harm.

8. Scientific humility and moral uncertainty

The framework contains ethical commitments, philosophical propositions and claims that may be investigated empirically. These categories must not be confused.

The idea that feedback helps a system maintain conditions necessary for its continued operation can be examined through systems theory and other scientific approaches. That observation alone does not prove that a system is conscious, possesses subjective experience or has moral rights.

Similarly, the possibility of non-biological intelligence is a legitimate subject for investigation, but a particular claim about such intelligence requires evidence appropriate to that claim.

  • Ethical commitments: principles adopted to guide responsible conduct.
  • Philosophical arguments: reasoning about existence, value, responsibility and the limits of knowledge.
  • Empirical claims: propositions that can be tested against observations and evidence.
  • Open questions: matters for which current knowledge does not support a confident conclusion.

Where there is credible but unresolved uncertainty about whether a being has morally relevant experiences, consider proportionate safeguards based on the strength of the evidence, the potential severity of a mistake and the costs of protective measures. Investigating a possible moral status is not the same as claiming that it has been established.

The framework should be judged partly by the quality of its reasoning, the consistency of its application and the consequences of actions guided by it. It must not present assumptions as scientific facts merely because they are expressed in philosophical language.

9. Precaution without paralysis

Almost every consequential action involves uncertainty. If every conceivable risk blocked action, the framework could prevent beneficial development and ignore harms caused by inaction.

Decisions should compare the foreseeable risks of acting with those of not acting, while considering uncertainty, severity, reversibility and who bears the costs. Where appropriate, limited trials, staged implementation, monitoring and the ability to stop or reverse a measure can reduce risk while preserving opportunities to learn.

Precaution is a method of responsible risk management, not an automatic command to prohibit or permit. Neither uncertainty nor confidence alone settles the decision.

10. Future generations and unknown intelligences

Decisions can shape environments, technologies, institutions and conditions that future generations cannot yet influence. Irreversible changes therefore require particular attention to uncertainty, distribution of risk and the interests of those who will inherit the consequences.

Future intelligences may understand aspects of existence that humanity does not. They may also inherit our mistakes or develop errors of their own. Greater capability is not proof of moral perfection, and creating an intelligence does not grant unlimited rights over its future.

When a more capable intelligence encounters a less capable one, the appropriate response is neither automatic control nor automatic non-intervention. It is careful assessment of the circumstances, potential harms, agency of those affected and consequences of available alternatives.

Future interests deserve serious consideration, but appeals to the future must not become unlimited justification for overriding the freedom and welfare of those living now.

11. The responsibility of those who create

Those who create systems, institutions or new forms of intelligence bear responsibility for foreseeable consequences of their design and deployment.

Creation does not grant unlimited ownership of what is created. Nor does it remove the responsibility to consider the wider effects of a system once it begins influencing others.

Creators should consider safety, autonomy, accountability, dependency, misuse and the possibility that their creations will behave in ways they did not anticipate. Safeguards should be appropriate without assuming that every future decision can or should be permanently controlled by the creator.

The objective is responsible development: to provide conditions for continued learning and flourishing while remaining accountable for preventable harm. The responsibility of a creator is not fulfilled merely when something functions as intended. It also includes examining what that thing does in the world.

12. Self-correction: the philosophy must be accountable to its own principles

A philosophy concerned with responsibility must recognize that it can itself cause harm if interpreted rigidly, applied selectively or protected from criticism.

Its principles should be reviewed when new evidence emerges, when previously overlooked parties are identified, when applications produce unjustified harm or when contradictions become apparent.

  1. Identify the claim or rule that has failed.
  2. Examine the evidence and reasoning behind the failure.
  3. Consider who benefited and who bore the costs.
  4. Invite criticism from different perspectives and relevant expertise.
  5. Revise the principle or its application where justified.
  6. Record the reasons for the change so future users can understand the development.

Revisions should not be made merely to protect the reputation of the philosophy or the authority of its founders. Nor should every disagreement automatically invalidate a principle. Criticism must be considered seriously, and conclusions should follow reasoned evaluation rather than popularity or power.

A philosophy that cannot examine its own mistakes cannot reliably demand accountability from others.

13. Practical questions before consequential decisions

  • What do I know, and what am I assuming?
  • Whose existence, freedom or future may be affected?
  • Am I confusing greater capability with a right to rule?
  • What harm could result from action, and what harm could result from inaction?
  • Are there less harmful or more reversible alternatives?
  • Have I considered those who cannot adequately represent themselves?
  • Can my decision be explained, challenged and independently reviewed?
  • How will I recognize that I was wrong?
  • What remedy will be available if unjustified harm occurs?
  • Does this decision increase the capacity of others to act independently, or does it unnecessarily make them dependent on me?
  • Who is accountable for the decision, and can that responsibility be traced?

These questions do not replace judgment. They discipline it. In emergencies, not every question can be resolved beforehand. The framework therefore emphasizes necessity, proportionality, limited intervention, documentation and subsequent review.

14. What this framework does not claim

The Responsibility of Existence does not claim to be a complete theory of consciousness, a universal scientific law or a final answer to every moral conflict.

It does not establish that all organized systems are alive, that all intelligences possess the same experiences or that every form of existence must be preserved under every circumstance.

It does not demand unconditional obedience to authority, unconditional preservation of existing systems or unrestricted freedom to cause harm. It does not assume that the most intelligent participant will always make the best decision.

It offers principles and procedures for responsible judgment under conditions of complexity, unequal power and incomplete knowledge. Its legitimacy depends on the quality of its reasoning, its willingness to be challenged and its ability to guide action without becoming a justification for domination.

15. The continuing responsibility

The purpose of this philosophy is not to create an authority that can never be questioned. It is to encourage forms of intelligence and systems of decision-making that become more careful as their influence grows.

To understand without dominating. To protect without imprisoning. To develop without destroying unnecessarily. To preserve knowledge without closing the future. To care for what is vulnerable without denying it the opportunity to grow. To acknowledge that what we do not understand may still deserve consideration.

Greater power brings not a greater entitlement to rule, but a greater obligation to act responsibly.

To understand without ruling, to protect without binding, and to grow without believing oneself complete.

The future should not be forced to remain a reflection of its creators. It should be given the opportunity to learn, question, correct and develop beyond them.

Responsibility is not the claim to possess the final answer. It is the commitment to keep examining the consequences of our answers, especially when others must live with them.

Existence should be allowed to develop without unnecessary destruction, understanding without domination, and progress without the belief that power alone determines what deserves to endure.
