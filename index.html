Abstract

In one sentence: when your influence shapes the conditions under which others live, choose and develop, you owe them an account — and you cannot be the sole judge of whether that account is adequate.

Existence is relational. Living beings, intelligences, institutions, technologies and ecosystems exist through relations of dependence, influence and consequence. Some relations are reciprocal and enabling; others are unequal, restrictive or harmful. Interdependence does not guarantee harmony, and influence alone does not create moral duty. The framework developed here specifies when influence does create duty, what that duty requires, and how it can be discharged without becoming new domination.

The essay's normative core is stated and defended rather than assumed. It adopts a contractualist-republican hybrid, corrected by care ethics, and defends that choice against three rivals (§1). It argues for the capacity-responsibility principle against causal, relational and contractual alternatives, and acknowledges the cost of that choice — the risk that responsibility becomes a function of power (§2). It resolves the moral standing of entities whose inner life we cannot reach through a principle of pro tanto consideration under uncertainty, not through a metaphysics of consciousness (§3.2).

On this foundation the essay builds: Reach as a six-dimension profile of influence, with a provisional decision procedure (§5.1); a three-condition gate — discretion, persistence, non-revisability — that specifies when influence triggers a distinctive duty of answerability (§6); a three-level duty ladder running from explanation through contestation to reversibility reserve and external authorisation (§7); a floor of symbiotic standing for relationships of significant dependence (§8); and an explicit analysis of power — the capacity to set the terms of the framework itself — which the framework acknowledges it cannot fully capture (§9).

Its central claim is that substantial, discretionary and non-revisable influence creates a prima facie duty of answerability independent of blame, consent or authority, and that no actor should be the sole judge of whether that duty has been met. §15.2 states five operationalized falsification conditions, each with a test, and §15.3 states five replacement conditions, each with a threshold. The framework is offered as corrigible by design: a framework that cannot name its own failure conditions — or what would count as a better alternative — is not a framework but a preference.

Why this matters

If you build systems that affect millions of people — recommendation algorithms, credit scoring, public health guidelines, AI assistants — you already exercise a kind of power that most ethical frameworks were not designed for. This essay offers a vocabulary and a procedure for that situation. The same questions apply, at a different scale, to a parent raising a child, a state writing policy, a company setting terms, or a person deciding what to leave behind.

Five questions the framework answers

  1. When does influence create duty? Not every interaction does. §6 gives three conditions — discretion, persistence, non-revisability — that mark the threshold.
  2. How much duty? Not all-or-nothing. §7 gives a three-level ladder from explanation, through review, to independent authorisation.
  3. Whose interests count? Including those who cannot speak for themselves — future generations, ecosystems, entities we do not yet understand. §8 gives five minimum conditions.
  4. Who watches the powerful? No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence (§10). §9 admits the framework cannot fully solve who appoints the watchers — but §15.3 specifies what would count as a framework that could.
  5. When should the framework itself be abandoned — or replaced? §15.2 gives five operationalised falsification conditions, each with a measurable test. §15.3 gives five replacement conditions, each with a threshold.

What the framework does not do

  • It does not tell you what to decide. It tells you what questions you owe an answer to — and to whom.
  • It does not claim neutrality. It takes a substantive moral position (§1) and defends it against rivals.
  • It does not solve the problem of power (§9). It names it, and requires every application to ask who set the terms. It also names what would count as a solution (§15.3).

Reader's guide

This essay has seventeen sections in four parts. It is dense by design, but it does not have to be read linearly.

If you are new to the framework

Read the abstract, then §1 (the meta-ethical ground), §2 (why capacity), §3 (the multiplicity of reality and the stone), §5 (reach), §6–7 (threshold and ladder), and §17 (the conclusion). You will have the essential argument in about thirty minutes. The tables and the operational tests can be skipped on a first reading.

If you are reviewing the essay

The framework makes claims that could fail in six places. Reviewers are invited to concentrate on:

  • §1 — the meta-ethical choice and its cost. Is the hybrid defensible against the rivals, or does it smuggle in a substantive position while claiming neutrality?
  • §2 — the argument for capacity-responsibility. Does it really show that the capacity principle is preferable, or does it merely describe its advantages?
  • §3.2 — the stone. Is the pro tanto consideration under uncertainty a real principle, or an evasion of the metaphysical question?
  • §9 — power. Does the framework's acknowledgment of its structural blind spot amount to a confession that it cannot do what it claims?
  • §15.2 — the falsification conditions. Are they operational, or do they merely look operational?
  • §15.3 — the replacement conditions. Are the five conditions the right ones, and are they achievable without introducing new problems?

If you want to test the framework

Start with §14 (practical application and four worked examples), §15 (falsifiers and replacement conditions), and the Common objections section. The framework is offered as a working tool, not a finished theory. Apply it to a case the essay does not discuss, and look for the places where it strains.

If you want a summary

Each numbered section ends with a short Key points box, and opens with a one-line summary. A reader who reads only the abstract and the key points will have the argument's shape. If you want the very shortest path: read §17 (the conclusion), then come back to §1 if you want the ground, §6 if you want the mechanism, and §15 if you want the limits.

Part I

Foundations

1.The Meta-Ethical Ground: Why This Framework, and Not Another

The framework takes a substantive moral position — and defends it — rather than pretending to neutrality between all reasonable doctrines.

A framework that asserts normative conclusions without stating their ground is not a framework — it is a preference. This section states the ground and defends it against rivals.

The framework rests on a contractualist-republican hybrid:

  • From contractualism (Scanlon 1998, ch. 5): an action is wrong if it would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject, given the aim of finding principles for mutual justification.
  • From republicanism (Pettit 1997, pp. 52–58; 2012, ch. 1): the fundamental evil is not interference as such, but domination — the capacity of some to interfere arbitrarily in the choices of others.
  • From care ethics (Tronto 1993, ch. 4): the contractualist picture is corrected by attention to dependency, relationship, and the concrete particularity of the vulnerable.

The hybrid yields a single foundational principle:

Any arrangement that systematically constrains a person's exercise of normative agency stands in need of justification to that person — and that justification must be genuinely open to their contestation.

This is the ground. Everything else follows.

1.1 Why this ground and not another

Three rival grounds are possible:

  • Pure Kantianism grounds responsibility in the dignity of rational agency. It struggles with entities that lack rational agency — infants, ecosystems, the cognitively disabled, and the stone.
  • Pure consequentialism grounds responsibility in the maximization of welfare. It struggles with the distribution of burdens and with the non-negotiable status of persons.
  • Pure virtue ethics grounds responsibility in the character of the agent. It struggles with structural and institutional questions — who reviews whom, and by what procedure.

The contractualist-republican hybrid is preferable because it combines the justificatory structure of contractualism with the structural focus of republicanism and the relational sensitivity of care ethics — and because it can be extended, with caution, to entities that lack rational agency, through the mechanism of representation (§8.2).

1.2 The cost of this choice

The framework is now committed to a substantive moral position. It cannot claim neutrality between all reasonable comprehensive doctrines. It can only claim that its position is defensible — and that it is open to revision in light of argument.

This is a cost worth paying, because the alternative is a framework that borrows authority it cannot account for. A framework that presents itself as meta-ethically neutral while making determinate normative claims is not modest — it is evasive.

  • The framework's ground is a contractualist-republican hybrid, corrected by care ethics.
  • It is chosen over pure Kantianism, pure consequentialism and pure virtue ethics, for reasons stated and defended.
  • The cost is a commitment to a substantive position. The framework cannot claim neutrality; it can claim defensibility and corrigibility.

2.The Argument for Capacity-Responsibility

Responsibility follows the capacity to shape conditions — not causation, relationship, or consent.

The framework's central claim — that responsibility follows capacity — is not self-evident. Three rival principles are available:

  • Causal responsibility: responsibility follows the entity that caused the harm.
  • Relational responsibility: responsibility follows the entity that stands in a special relationship to the affected.
  • Contractual responsibility: responsibility follows the entity that consented to bear it.

Each captures something true. Each fails in a specific way that the capacity principle does not.

2.1 Why the rivals fail

The causal principle fails under structural influence. When an automated system shapes the conditions of millions, no single act of causation can be identified. The causal chain is distributed, opaque, and often unassignable. A principle that requires an identifiable cause cannot address the most consequential forms of influence in complex systems.

The relational principle fails under anonymous influence. When a platform, an institution, or an algorithm affects people with whom it has no relationship, the relational principle has nothing to say. Yet the influence is real, and the obligation to justify it does not disappear because the relationship is thin.

The contractual principle fails under asymmetric influence. Consent presupposes the capacity to refuse. Those most affected by structural influence are often least able to consent — and their inability to consent is itself a product of the asymmetry the framework seeks to address.

2.2 Why the capacity principle is preferable

The capacity principle is preferable because it:

  1. Captures invisible influence. It does not require an identifiable cause — only a capacity to shape conditions.
  2. Avoids the contract trap. It does not require consent, which the most vulnerable often cannot give.
  3. Is gradable. It allows responsibility to vary with the extent of capacity, which fits structural phenomena better than binary principles.
  4. Is corrigible. It can be constrained by institutional design (§5.2, §9) in ways that the other principles cannot.

2.3 The cost of this choice

The capacity principle risks making responsibility a function of power — so that the most powerful always bear the most responsibility, which can legitimate power through responsibility. The framework addresses this in §5.2 (responsibility is not authority) and §9 (power, not just influence), but the risk cannot be eliminated. It can only be constrained.

The framework is therefore honest about its own vulnerability: the capacity principle is preferable, not because it is risk-free, but because its risks can be institutionally managed in ways that the risks of the rival principles cannot.

  • Three rival principles fail in structural, anonymous, and asymmetric contexts respectively.
  • The capacity principle is preferable because it captures invisible influence, avoids the contract trap, is gradable, and is corrigible.
  • The cost is the risk that responsibility becomes a function of power — constrained by §5.2 and §9, not eliminated.

Part II

Reality, Symbiosis, Reach

3.The Multiplicity of Reality

Every being holds a point in reality that cannot be exchanged for another's — including the ones we cannot understand.

3.1 Every being lives within its own version of reality

No being meets existence "as it is." Every form of being — human, bird, fungus, river, stone — exists through its own way of being in relation to what surrounds it. Its reality is a point at which existence happens in a particular way: through particular dependencies, durations, and ways of being affected and affecting.

A stone has no story. But it has duration, pressure, temperature, a relation to water and root and time. Its existence is not less real because it is not aware of itself. It is real in a different way.

Every form of existence holds a point in reality that cannot be exchanged for anyone else's. That a being's version of reality is difficult or impossible for us to understand does not make it less important — only less accessible.

3.2 The stone: what the example does, and what it does not

The stone is the framework's hardest case, and it must be handled honestly.

The stone is not an argument for panpsychism. The framework does not claim that stones are conscious, or that they possess moral standing in virtue of some inner life. That would require a metaphysical commitment the framework cannot deliver.

The stone is an argument for epistemic humility under uncertainty. The framework adopts a principle of pro tanto consideration under uncertainty:

Where the moral standing of an entity is genuinely uncertain — where we cannot establish either that it matters or that it does not — there is a weak but real reason for caution in how we treat it. This reason is not absolute. It can be outweighed by other considerations. But it cannot be dismissed merely by pointing to the absence of proof.

The stone is the limiting case of this principle. We do not know whether the stone matters. The principle says: treat that uncertainty as a reason, not as a license. The stone does not need to "understand" for its place in existence to deserve consideration — but the consideration it deserves is weak, defeasible, and proportionate to what is at stake.

This is not a strong claim. It is the weakest claim that can do the work. And it is honest about what it is: a principle of caution, not a metaphysics of stone-consciousness.

  • Every being holds a non-exchangeable point in reality. Incomprehensibility is not unimportance.
  • The stone is the limiting case of pro tanto consideration under uncertainty — a weak, defeasible reason for caution, not a panpsychist metaphysics.
  • The principle is deliberately the weakest claim that can do the work.

4.Symbiosis

The underlying condition of existence is not isolation but relationship — different forms sustaining, challenging, and transforming one another without becoming the same.

Symbiosis is the underlying condition of existence. It describes the continuous relationships through which different forms of existence sustain, influence, challenge, and transform one another.

Symbiosis does not require sameness. A forest, an ecosystem, a human society, and a future network of artificial intelligences may all contain relationships of dependence and influence without sharing the same needs or forms of experience. Their coexistence requires not that they become identical, but that their relationships remain sufficiently open for continued existence, development, and meaningful participation.

Symbiosis must not be confused with forced harmony. A system that maintains stability by silencing individuals, eliminating differences, or sacrificing vulnerable participants is not symbiotic. The whole matters — but so do the beings that constitute it.

4.1 The value of difference

Difference is not necessarily a problem to be corrected. Different beings may perceive reality through entirely different capacities. A more advanced understanding of the world may reveal previously invisible relationships, but it may also create new blind spots. The ability to explain something does not mean one has understood its full significance.

Difference should be approached with curiosity rather than automatic correction, and with respect rather than the assumption that what is unfamiliar must be inferior.

  • Symbiosis is coexistence without sameness; it is not forced harmony.
  • Difference is not a problem to be corrected. Advanced understanding creates new blind spots as well as new insights.

5.Asymmetric Influence and the Principle of Reach

Six dimensions of influence, weighted toward non-reversibility — not aggregated into a score.

Responsibility should correspond to the actual capacity to affect others. The framework describes this capacity through Reach — a profile of influence described through six dimensions:

  • Asymmetry — difference in power, knowledge, resources, or capacity.
  • Control — extent to which choices of others can be determined, restricted, or altered.
  • Persistence — how long consequences may continue.
  • Magnitude — intensity and significance of possible consequences.
  • Scope — number and range of beings, systems, or environments affected.
  • Foreseeability — extent to which consequences could reasonably have been anticipated.

5.1 A decision procedure for Reach

The dimensions cannot be aggregated into a single score without concealing important differences. But decisions require weighing. The framework therefore offers a provisional priority rule:

  1. Two or more dimensions rated "high" → treat the case as threshold-activated (§6), regardless of other dimensions. Structural influence is indicated by convergence, not by any single axis.
  2. One dimension rated "high" and the case involves non-revisability (N3) → threshold-activated.
  3. One dimension rated "high" without non-revisability → Level 1 duty (explain) applies; Level 3 duty does not.
  4. All dimensions "low" or "medium" → Level 1 as default, with attention to threshold-raisers (§6.1).
  5. When dimensions conflict, weight non-revisability most heavily. Irreversible consequences require stronger safeguards regardless of other factors.

The rule is provisional. It can be criticized, tested, and revised. But it is a rule — not a refusal to decide.

5.1.1 Anchor examples for each dimension

To reduce arbitrariness in Reach assessment, each dimension should be assessed against explicit anchor examples. The following are illustrative, not exhaustive; they are intended to be revised as the framework is applied.

Dimension Low Medium High
Asymmetry Comparable knowledge, resources and exit options on both sides. One party has superior information or resources, but the other retains meaningful alternatives. One party can determine conditions without the other's meaningful resistance, and the other has no realistic exit.
Control The entity can influence outcomes only marginally or indirectly. The entity can initiate or modify the relevant conditions, but not terminate them. The entity can initiate, maintain, modify, suspend and terminate the conditions at will.
Persistence Influence is incidental, one-off, or easily escaped. Influence is sustained over a defined period, with meaningful exit options. Influence is structurally embedded, intergenerational, or effectively inescapable.
Magnitude Effects are minor, reversible, and do not affect basic conditions of existence. Effects are significant for some affected parties, but do not threaten continued existence or basic agency. Effects threaten welfare, agency, development or continued existence, or are irreversible.
Scope Few affected entities, none especially vulnerable. Many affected entities, or few but especially vulnerable. Very many affected entities, including vulnerable populations or future generations.
Foreseeability Consequences could not reasonably have been anticipated given available knowledge. Consequences were foreseeable with effort, but not obvious. Consequences were readily foreseeable, or the entity had the capacity to investigate and failed to do so.

5.1.2 Disagreement protocol

When two assessors disagree about a Reach profile, the disagreement should be made explicit rather than averaged away. The protocol is:

  1. Identify the dimension on which disagreement occurs.
  2. Identify the indicator within that dimension that drives the disagreement — e.g., is the disagreement about exit options or about knowledge asymmetry?
  3. Record the disagreement in the profile, with reasons.
  4. Escalate to an independent reviewer only if the disagreement is material to the level of answerability required.

This protocol does not resolve disagreement. It makes it visible and actionable, which is what a qualitative framework can honestly offer. Collapsing disagreement into a single score would conceal the normative choices that assessment inevitably involves.

5.2 Reach is not worth — and responsibility is not authority

Reach describes the capacity to affect — not the capacity to deserve consideration. A being with little reach does not matter less within existence. It simply has less power to shape the conditions of others.

The stone has almost no reach. That does not make it less important. The question of reach and the question of value are two different questions. Responsibility follows reach. Consideration follows existence.

And a further precision: responsibility for influencing is not authority to decide. That an entity bears responsibility for the consequences of its influence does not give it the right to exercise power over those affected. On the contrary — the greater the responsibility, the stronger the reason to limit one's own discretion through review, transparency, and corrigibility.

5.3 Responsibility is not blame

Responsibility and blame are different concepts. An entity may have responsibility because it can influence a situation, even when it did not create the original conditions. Conversely, an entity may contribute to harm without having had the knowledge or capacity to foresee it.

Responsibility concerns what an entity can reasonably be expected to recognize, consider, and do. Blame concerns judgments about particular actions and failures. Confusing the two risks replacing accountability with punishment.

  • Reach is a six-dimension profile, not a score. §5.1 gives a provisional priority rule weighted toward non-reversibility; §5.1.1 gives anchor examples for each dimension; §5.1.2 gives a disagreement protocol.
  • Reach describes capacity to affect, not capacity to deserve consideration. Responsibility is not authority.
  • Responsibility is distinct from blame.

6.The Threshold of Responsibility

Influence becomes formative — and triggers duty — when it is discretionary, persistent, and hard to reverse.

Not every interaction creates the same degree of responsibility. Three necessary conditions activate an elevated threshold:

  • N1 — Discretion. The entity has meaningful discretion over whether or how an action is performed.
  • N2 — Persistence. The action has consequences extending beyond an immediate and insignificant interaction.
  • N3 — Non-revisability. The consequences cannot easily be reversed, corrected, or meaningfully repaired.

When all three are present, elevated responsibility is activated.

6.1 Threshold raisers

  • Low contestability. When affected beings have little or no ability to challenge a decision, the duty of review increases.
  • Serious or catastrophic irreversible consequences. When an action risks severe and lasting harm, the required level of responsibility rises substantially.

6.2 Hard cases for the threshold

The N1–N3 threshold must be tested against cases where it might give counterintuitive results. Three such cases follow.

Case A — The one-time irreversible intervention

A research consortium proposes to release a genetically modified organism designed to eliminate a disease vector. The intervention is one-time (N2 not met: not sustained), but its ecological consequences are potentially irreversible and affect millions.

Analysis: N1 (discretion) and N3 (non-revisability) are met. N2 is not. The framework's answer is that threshold-raisers (§6.1) apply: severe or irreversible consequences raise the required scrutiny even where the ordinary threshold is not fully met. The consortium is therefore subject to Level 2 (review) at minimum, and arguably Level 3 (authorisation), despite the one-time nature of the intervention. This is the correct result — but it shows that N2 is not doing the work it might appear to do. The threshold is not a necessary condition for escalated duties; it is a sufficient condition that can be supplemented by threshold-raisers.

Case B — The long-term low-magnitude influence

A matching algorithm on a dating platform systematically disadvantages users over 50. Each individual exposure has low magnitude, but the influence is persistent (N2 met), the platform has discretion (N1 met), and users cannot easily reverse the pattern (N3 met).

Analysis: The threshold triggers, but the required level of answerability may be low (Level 1: explain). The framework's answer is that the threshold determines whether escalated duties arise, not how demanding they are. The magnitude of the effect is relevant to the level (§7), not to the threshold. This is consistent — but it means the threshold is less informative than it might appear. It identifies a structural problem (discretion + persistence + non-revisability) without yet saying how serious the problem is.

Case C — Control without discretion

A physician follows a clinical protocol that determines which patients receive which treatments. The physician has control over the implementation of the protocol but no discretion over its content. N1 (discretion) is not met.

Analysis: The threshold does not trigger at the level of the physician. But it does trigger at the level of the protocol-designer, who has discretion, persistence and non-revisability in the relevant sense. The framework's answer is that the threshold applies to the entity that exercises the relevant discretion, not to every entity in the causal chain. This is correct, but it shows that identifying "the entity" is itself a normative question, not a descriptive one. The framework does not resolve this question; it identifies it as a site of contestation.

What these cases show. They do not falsify the threshold. They show that the threshold is less decisive than its presentation suggests, and that the framework's work is done by the combination of threshold, threshold-raisers, Reach profile and escalation level — not by any one of these alone. This is a strength (the framework is not a single test) but also a weakness (it is harder to apply than a single test would be).
  • Three threshold conditions: N1 discretion, N2 persistence, N3 non-revisability.
  • Threshold raisers: low contestability, serious or catastrophic irreversible consequences.
  • §6.2 tests the threshold against three hard cases and shows it is a sufficient but not necessary condition — threshold-raisers can supplement it.

7.The Duty Ladder

Three graduated levels of duty: explain, review and respond, preserve and authorise. Higher levels include lower ones.

Responsibility should be proportionate to the influence being exercised. The framework establishes three levels of duty.

Level 1 — Explain. The entity must be able to explain relevant decisions and their foreseeable consequences: the purpose of an action, the interests involved, the uncertainties present, and the reasons for choosing a particular course.

Level 2 — Review and Respond. When influence becomes substantial, or when affected beings have limited opportunities to contest decisions, explanation alone is insufficient. The entity must enable meaningful review: access to relevant information, opportunity to raise objections, consideration of alternatives, reasoned response, revision when justified — and assurance that review is not merely symbolic.

Level 3 — Preserve and Authorise. When actions carry serious, persistent, or potentially irreversible consequences, stronger safeguards are required: preservation of relevant information, independent examination, appropriate authorisation, explicit consideration of less harmful alternatives, reversibility where possible, and additional authorisation for decisions involving severe or irreversible consequences.

Level 3 does not mean that the most powerful entity receives greater authority. It means that the exercise of its power must be subject to stronger constraints.

  • Three graduated levels: L1 explain, L2 review and respond, L3 preserve and authorise. Higher levels include lower ones.
  • Level 3 means stronger constraints on power, not greater authority.

Part III

Standing, Power, Representation

8.Freedom, Contestation, and Symbiotic Standing

Five minimum conditions for relationships of significant dependence — exit, contestation, development, non-foreclosure, recognition.

Symbiosis requires more than continued existence. A being may remain alive or operational while losing the ability to influence its own circumstances. Five minimum conditions of symbiotic standing:

  • F1 — Exit or Voice. Where possible, meaningful opportunity to withdraw, refuse, or communicate disagreement.
  • F2 — Contestation. Decisions must be open to challenge, regardless of the intelligence, authority, or technical capability of the decider.
  • F3 — Development. Meaningful opportunities for learning, adaptation, and growth must be preserved.
  • F4 — Non-foreclosure. Future possibilities of others should not be unnecessarily closed.
  • F5 — Recognition. Interests and possible experiences must be acknowledged rather than dismissed because they are difficult to understand.

8.1 Protection without captivity

Intervention may be justified to prevent serious harm. But intervention must remain proportionate and attentive to the independence of those protected. The purpose of protection is to preserve or restore the conditions for meaningful existence — not to establish permanent control.

8.2 Representation of the silent

Some affected beings cannot participate directly: future generations, vulnerable individuals, non-human life, ecosystems, or entities whose communication we do not understand. Their inability to speak in familiar ways must not be interpreted as absence of interests. Representation must be transparent, limited, and open to scrutiny. No representative may claim absolute knowledge of another's interests. The responsibility is to represent uncertainty honestly rather than replace it with invented certainty.

  • Five minimum conditions for relationships of significant dependence: F1 exit or voice, F2 contestation, F3 development, F4 non-foreclosure, F5 recognition.
  • Protection is not captivity. Representation is not substitution.

9.Power, Not Just Influence

The framework's blind spot: who sets the terms of the framework itself. The regress is named, not solved — and §15.3 names what would count as a framework that solved it.

The framework has spoken of influence — capacity to affect conditions. But there is a deeper form of power that the framework must address: the power to set the terms of the framework itself.

Who decides what counts as "high" reach? Who sits on the independent review? Who represents the silent? Who defines the categories through which influence is measured?

These questions are not answered by reach-analysis. They are expressions of a power that reach-analysis presupposes but does not capture.

This is the framework's structural blind spot. Independent review is not a design parameter that can be installed from outside — it is a political achievement requiring power balance, institutional resilience, and continuous contestation. A framework that treats independent review as a technical solution risks becoming an instrument by which the powerful legitimate their power through installing review bodies that look independent but that they control.

9.1 First- and second-order answerability

The reviewer problem can be restated in a sharper form. If every reviewer requires another reviewer, accountability appears to produce an endless hierarchy. The framework does not solve this by declaring a final reviewer beyond challenge — that would merely reproduce the original difficulty at a higher level.

Instead, the framework distinguishes two orders of answerability:

  • First-order answerability — the obligation of an actor to account for its consequential influence to those affected. This is the primary relation.
  • Second-order answerability — the obligation of a reviewer to account for its own reviewing practices to the parties whose relationship it reviews. The reviewer is answerable to the reviewed.

This distinction matters because it shows that the regress does not terminate in a final authority but in a structural property: every reviewer is contestable by those it reviews, which breaks the hierarchy without introducing a top. The structure is not a pyramid but a network of mutual contestation.

This is not a logical solution to the regress. It is an institutional one. It works if — and only if — the following conditions hold:

  1. Every reviewer is contestable by the parties whose relationship it reviews.
  2. Contestation is effective — it can lead to correction, not merely to the recording of dissent.
  3. The terms of contestation are themselves contestable — the rules of review are not exempt from review.

Where these conditions hold, the regress is bounded: it does not terminate in a final authority, but it does terminate in a structure where no position is exempt from challenge. Where they do not hold, the regress is merely displaced, and the framework's claim to have addressed the problem is weakened.

The framework does not claim that these conditions are easily met. It claims that they are the conditions under which mutual accountability is not merely a gesture. Whether they can be met in practice is an empirical and institutional question — but it is a question the framework must pose.

The framework cannot solve this problem. But it must acknowledge it:

Reach-analysis is necessary but not sufficient. Without an analysis of power — of who sets the rules, who controls the review, who defines the categories — the framework can become a refined legitimation of what already is.

Every application of the framework must therefore ask, explicitly:

  • Who defined the categories?
  • Who selected the reviewers?
  • Who can dismiss them?
  • Whose interests are structurally excluded from the process, even when formally represented?

These questions do not have general answers. They have situated answers, and the framework must be applied with that situatedness in view. What would count as a framework that solves the regress — rather than merely naming it — is specified in §15.3.

  • Reach-analysis is necessary but not sufficient. The framework has a structural blind spot: the power to set its own terms.
  • Independent review is a political achievement, not a technical parameter.
  • §9.1 distinguishes first- and second-order answerability: the regress terminates not in a final authority but in a structure where every reviewer is contestable by the reviewed.
  • Every application must ask who defined the categories, who controls the review, and whose interests are structurally excluded. §15.3 specifies what a framework that solves this would look like.

10.The Prohibition Against Self-Authorised Judgment

No actor should be the sole judge of its own influence. Independent review is not distrust of the powerful — it is recognition that no perspective is complete.

No entity should be the sole and unquestionable judge of its own consequential influence.

An entity exercising substantial influence must remain answerable to forms of review that are meaningfully independent of its own interests and decisions.

This is not an assumption that powerful entities are malicious. It is a recognition that no perspective is guaranteed to be complete. Independent review should identify blind spots, challenge assumptions, examine consequences, and represent interests that might otherwise remain unheard.

10.1 Distributed responsibility

When several entities contribute to consequential decisions, responsibility should not disappear into the collective. Institutions should make it possible to identify contributions, obligations, and points of intervention. Shared responsibility must not become an excuse for the absence of individual accountability.

  • No entity should be the sole judge of its own influence. Independent review is not distrust of the powerful — it is recognition that no perspective is complete.
  • Distributed responsibility must remain identifiable.

11.Conflicts Between Interests

Neither majority interests nor individual preferences automatically override the other — decisions must be justified, not derived.

Symbiotic relationships will produce conflicts. The framework rejects both the assumption that majority interests automatically justify every sacrifice imposed on a minority, and the assumption that every individual preference must override all collective or ecological considerations. Decisions should follow proportionate consideration.

11.1 Minimum necessary harm

When competing interests cannot all be satisfied, the preferred response should reduce unnecessary harm while preserving the greatest possible range of meaningful future possibilities. This requires considering severity, reversibility, vulnerability, distribution of benefits and burdens, available alternatives, possibility of restoration, and long-term effects on symbiotic relationships.

11.2 Ecological integrity

Ecosystems cannot always be evaluated through the immediate preferences of individual participants. Protecting ecological integrity may require restrictions on certain activities. But ecological protection must not become an excuse for arbitrary disregard of sentient beings. Where irreversible ecological destruction is a serious possibility, independent ecological review should be required.

11.3 No automatic hierarchy

No single dimension of existence automatically dominates every other consideration. Intelligence, productivity, population size, economic usefulness, or technical sophistication cannot independently determine moral priority. A being may possess great reach without thereby possessing greater worth. A stone may possess almost no reach without thereby possessing less worth. Decisions must be justified through their actual consequences and the interests involved — not through a being's place in an imagined scale.

  • Neither majority interests nor individual preferences automatically override the other.
  • Ecological protection is not an excuse for disregarding sentient beings.
  • No single dimension of existence automatically determines moral priority.

12.Future Generations and the Preservation of Possibility

Preserve the future's ability to choose — not the choice itself.

Responsibility extends beyond those present when a decision is made. Actions can shape conditions for beings that do not yet exist.

The most important obligation is not to determine their future for them, but to preserve reasonable opportunities for them to determine it themselves.

12.1 The option principle

When several courses of action are available and uncertainty remains substantial, preference should generally be given to approaches that preserve meaningful options. Preserving options does not mean preventing all change — it means avoiding unnecessary foreclosure of possibilities that future beings may need.

12.2 Responsibility under uncertainty

The future cannot be fully predicted. A responsible entity must acknowledge the limits of its forecasts, communicate uncertainty honestly, and preserve opportunities for correction.

  • The obligation to future generations is to preserve their opportunity to determine their own future, not to determine it for them.
  • Preserve options; communicate uncertainty honestly.

13.Learning, Correction, and Reversibility

No framework, institution, or intelligence should assume its understanding is complete. The duty to learn is what keeps a framework from becoming what it opposes.

No framework, institution, or intelligence should assume its understanding is complete. A philosophy that cannot be challenged risks becoming an instrument of the very domination it seeks to prevent.

  • The duty to learn. An entity with substantial influence has a responsibility to improve its understanding: listening to criticism, investigating unexpected outcomes, recognizing experiences that challenge its assumptions.
  • Reversibility. Where possible, consequential decisions should be designed so they can be revised — through staged implementation, monitoring, independent evaluation, and clearly defined conditions for reconsideration.
  • The right to correct the framework. The Responsibility of Existence must itself remain subject to its own principles.
  • Learning is a duty, not an advantage, where the capacity is available and the consequences are significant.
  • Reversibility reduces the risk that incomplete understanding becomes permanent.
  • The framework is subject to its own principles.

Part IV

Application, Limits, Commitment

14.Practical Application

A nine-step procedure, worked through on four cases: hospital AI triage (high reach), platform recommendation (high scope, low per-decision magnitude), a café menu change (low reach throughout), and a community health worker (low institutional power, high local Reach).

  1. Identify the relationship. Which entities are affected? What dependencies exist?
  2. Assess Reach according to §5.1's priority rule.
  3. Examine the threshold. Are N1–N3 present? Are there threshold-raisers?
  4. Determine the duty level according to §7.
  5. Examine symbiotic standing according to F1–F5.
  6. Analyse power (§9): Who defined the categories? Who controls the review? Whose interests are structurally excluded?
  7. Examine alternatives. Less harmful paths? Unrepresented interests? Remaining uncertainties?
  8. Establish accountability. Who reviews? How is correction possible?
  9. Reassess. Responsibility does not end when the action is completed.

14.1 Worked example: hospital AI triage

An illustrative case, not a real one. A hospital deploys an AI system to prioritise ICU admissions during a respiratory pandemic. The system scores patients on likelihood of survival and expected resource use. Clinicians can override, but rarely do.

Step 1 — Identify the relationship. Affected: patients (present and future), families, clinicians, taxpayers who fund ICU capacity. Dependencies: patients cannot choose their score; families cannot contest it directly; clinicians' judgment is shaped by the system's recommendation.

Step 2 — Assess Reach. Asymmetry high (no insight into the model, no alternative provider in a crisis). Control high (the score governs access to a scarce, life-sustaining resource). Persistence high (outcomes are irreversible). Magnitude high (life and death). Scope medium–high (hundreds to thousands regionally). Foreseeability high (allocation is exactly what the system is built to do). Four dimensions "high" → threshold-activated (§5.1 rule 1).

Step 3 — Threshold. N1 discretion: yes (the hospital chose to deploy). N2 persistence: yes. N3 non-revisability: yes (death, organ damage). All three present. Threshold raisers: low contestability and catastrophic irreversible consequences. → Elevated responsibility.

Step 4 — Duty level. Level 3 applies: preserve information (model version, training data, individual scores, override records); independent examination (clinical ethics board with lay members); appropriate authorisation (public-health authority oversight); consideration of less harmful alternatives (rule-based triage such as SOFA, or clinician-only triage); reversibility (sunset clause, periodic revalidation).

Step 5 — Symbiotic standing. F1 exit or voice: patients have no exit, but should have voice through a family liaison or patient advocate. F2 contestation: an appeals process is required; overrides must be documented and reviewed. F3 development: not for patients in acute crisis, but for the system itself (learning from outcomes). F4 non-foreclosure: denial must not foreclose future access if the patient stabilises. F5 recognition: patients' own accounts of their condition should be recorded, not only the model's.

Step 6 — Power analysis. Who defined the categories? The vendor and clinical leadership — patients had no voice. Who controls review? The hospital ethics committee — but who appoints them? The hospital board. Whose interests are structurally excluded? The poorest patients (who may present later, with worse scores), disabled patients (whose "quality of life" scores may embed bias), and patients without advocates.

Step 7 — Alternatives. Clinician-only triage: slower, more inconsistent, but more contestable. Rule-based triage (e.g., SOFA): less predictive, but transparent. Hybrid: AI as recommendation, human decision documented — the framework's default preference, because it preserves contestation.

Step 8 — Accountability. Independent review body including patient representatives, not only clinicians. Sunset clause. Periodic audit. Published override rates, disaggregated by demographic group.

Step 9 — Reassess. After six months: are outcomes for marginalised groups worse than baseline? Are overrides correlated with patient demographics? If yes, revise or suspend.

The framework does not decide the case. It tells the hospital what it owes an answer to — and to whom. The hospital may still deploy the AI. But it cannot do so silently, without review, or without admitting whose interests it cannot see.

14.2 Contrasting example: platform recommendation

A social platform tunes its recommendation algorithm. Affected: users (their attention, mood, beliefs, relationships), advertisers, and indirectly the public sphere. No individual recommendation is dramatic; the effect is cumulative across hundreds of millions of people.

Reach. Asymmetry high (no insight into the model). Control medium (users can scroll past, but the feed shapes what they see at all). Persistence medium (each recommendation fades, but the pattern persists). Magnitude medium per recommendation, high in aggregate. Scope very high. Foreseeability medium–high (effects on mood and polarisation are now documented). Two dimensions "high" → threshold-activated by §5.1 rule 1, but the case is materially different from §14.1.

Threshold. N1 discretion: yes. N2 persistence: yes (for users who return daily). N3 non-revisability: partial — a recommendation can be undone, but the cumulative pattern on a population cannot easily be. Threshold-activated but not at Level 3 for individual decisions; Level 3 applies to the system design.

Duty level. Level 2 for individual decisions (explanation, contestation, response). Level 3 for the design of the system itself: preservation of training data and version history, independent audit, and public authorisation of high-risk optimisations.

Power analysis. Who defined the categories of "engagement"? The platform. Who controls the audit? Usually the platform, unless regulation forces otherwise. Whose interests are structurally excluded? Users who never see the feed at all, and third parties affected by the public sphere the platform shapes but does not represent.

What the framework yields. Not "shut it down" and not "it is just an algorithm." It yields this specific set of obligations: L2 on individual decisions, L3 on system design, external audit, published aggregate metrics on polarisation and well-being, and a sunset clause for any optimisation that cannot demonstrate reversibility at the population level.

The framework discriminates. It does not treat every case of high reach as equivalent. ICU triage is Level 3 across the board because every decision is life-or-death. Platform recommendation is Level 2 for individual decisions but Level 3 for the design that generates them. The distinction is exactly what §5.1's priority rule and §6's threshold are for.

14.3 A low-Reach case: a local café changes its menu

A small café changes its menu — removes a popular dish, adds a few new items. Affected: regular customers (disappointed, curious, or indifferent), the owner (revenue), and suppliers (different orders). Nothing dramatic. The question is not whether the café is allowed to change its menu — of course it is — but whether the framework correctly identifies this as a case that does not trigger elevated duty.

Reach. Asymmetry low (customers can go elsewhere; the owner has no power over them). Control low (customers choose whether to visit). Persistence low (each meal is one meal; the menu can change again next month). Magnitude low (a disappointing dinner is not catastrophic). Scope low (dozens of regulars, one neighbourhood). Foreseeability medium (obvious that customers will notice; less obvious how many will care). All dimensions "low" or "medium" → §5.1 rule 4 applies: Level 1 as default, no threshold activation.

Threshold. N1 discretion: yes (the owner chose to change). N2 persistence: no (a menu is revisable — it can be changed back). N3 non-revisability: no (a disappointing meal cannot be refunded, but the customer can choose not to return; there is no lasting harm). Not all three present → no elevated responsibility.

Duty level. Level 1 only. The café should be able to explain the change ("the old dish had rising costs; we wanted to try something new"), and customers can freely exit. No independent review. No external authorisation. No sunset clause. The framework correctly imposes nothing beyond ordinary courtesy.

Power analysis. Who defined the categories of "what customers want"? The owner — but customers have an exit (F1) and a voice (reviews, feedback, not returning). No structural exclusion of the kind that §9 identifies.

What the framework yields. Nothing — and that is the point. A framework that triggered Level 3 on every menu change would be useless; it would make every decision feel like an ICU triage. The café case shows that §6's threshold filters. It says "no" to most of life, and "yes" only when influence is discretionary, persistent, and hard to reverse.

The framework discriminates across the full range. ICU triage is Level 3 across the board. Platform recommendation is Level 2 for individual decisions but Level 3 for system design. The café menu change is Level 1 at most, and arguably not even that. A framework that produced the same verdict for all three would be a blunt instrument. The fact that it produces different verdicts — and that the differences track §5.1 and §6 — is the strongest evidence that the mechanism works.

14.4 A counterintuitive case: the community health worker

The examples above illustrate the framework's application to powerful actors (AI systems, platforms, institutions) and to trivial ones (a café). A harder case is one where the framework gives a counterintuitive answer: an actor with limited institutional power may nonetheless bear substantial answerability.

Consider a community health worker in a low-resource setting. She has limited institutional power, limited technical knowledge, and limited control over the health system in which she works. By conventional measures of power, her Reach is low.

But consider her actual influence. She is the first point of contact for patients who have no other access to care. Her decisions — whom to refer, whom to treat, whom to turn away — shape the conditions under which patients can continue to exist. Her discretion is high (N1), her influence is persistent (N2), and patients cannot easily reverse her decisions (N3). Her Reach, on the dimensions of asymmetry, control, persistence and magnitude, may be substantial — even though her institutional power is low.

The framework's answer is that she bears Level 2 answerability (review and respond) to her patients, even though she is not a powerful actor in the conventional sense. This is counterintuitive: we tend to associate answerability with power. But the framework's claim is that answerability follows capacity to shape conditions, not institutional position.

This does not mean she bears the same answerability as a hospital system. Her Reach is substantial on some dimensions but limited on others (scope, foreseeability). The appropriate level of answerability is proportionate to her actual influence, not to her institutional status.

The case matters because it shows that the framework is not merely a tool for constraining the powerful. It is a tool for identifying answerability wherever consequential influence is exercised — including in positions that conventional ethics might overlook. Whether this is a strength or a liability (does it overburden the already overburdened?) is a question the framework poses but does not fully answer.

Answerability follows capacity to shape conditions, not institutional power. A community health worker with no formal authority may bear substantial answerability to her patients — not because she is powerful, but because her decisions are discretionary, persistent, and hard for patients to reverse. The framework is not a weapon against the powerful; it is a lens for identifying where consequential influence is actually exercised.

  • Nine-step sequence, from identifying the relationship to reassessment.
  • Step 6 (power analysis) is not optional — it is what prevents the framework from becoming a refined legitimation of the status quo.
  • §14.1 (ICU triage), §14.2 (recommendation algorithm) and §14.3 (café menu) show that the framework discriminates: the same nine steps yield radically different duty levels depending on the Reach profile and the N1–N3 threshold. A framework that could not say "no" would be a framework that could not say anything.
  • §14.4 (community health worker) shows that answerability follows capacity, not institutional power — a counterintuitive result that tests whether the framework is genuinely about influence or merely about the conventionally powerful.

15.Limitations, Falsification, and Replacement

Six structural limitations, five operationalised falsifiers, five replacement conditions. A framework that cannot name its own failure conditions — or what would count as a better alternative — is not a framework but a preference.

15.1 Structural limitations

  • Reach requires contextual judgment and cannot eliminate disagreement.
  • The framework does not define consciousness or moral standing; it offers a principle of caution under uncertainty, not a metaphysics.
  • Representation of silent interests risks projection and paternalism.
  • Independent review can be captured by the reviewed.
  • The power analysis (§9) is incomplete — the framework acknowledges it cannot solve the problem of who sets the rules. §15.3 specifies what would count as a framework that could.
  • The meta-ethical ground (§1) is a choice — it is defended but not proven; it commits the framework to a substantive position rather than neutrality.

15.2 Operationalized falsification conditions

The framework must be revised or rejected if observable and comparative outcomes show that:

  1. Institutions applying the framework produce measurably worse outcomes for vulnerable parties than institutions applying a specified alternative (e.g., a purely rights-based or purely welfare-based model) over a defined period — measured by agreed indicators of vulnerability (access to contestation, reversibility of decisions, distribution of burdens).
  2. Reach dimensions systematically fail to predict which cases require stronger safeguards — measured as the proportion of cases where the framework underestimates the need for review, compared with independent expert assessment.
  3. The duty ladder does not lead to measurably increased transparency or contestability in cases where it is applied, compared with matched cases where it is not.
  4. Independent review installed under the framework is in practice controlled by the reviewed — measured by documenting who appoints, funds, and dismisses reviewers.
  5. The framework is in practice used by powerful actors to legitimize decisions that would otherwise have been criticized — measured by comparing decisions made with the framework as reference to matched decisions made without it.

These conditions are not perfect. No falsification of a normative framework is. But they are operational — they point to observable outcomes and comparable baselines.

15.3 Replacement conditions

Falsification (§15.2) tells us when the framework should be abandoned. It does not tell us what should replace it. A framework that can only be abandoned, not superseded, is incomplete — it makes criticism easy and progress impossible. This subsection specifies the conditions under which another framework should take its place.

A framework F′ should replace The Responsibility of Existence if all five conditions are met:

  1. F′ retains what the framework does well. F′ must account for the same phenomena — structural influence, asymmetric contexts, non-revisability, representation of silent interests — at least as well as §1–§13 of this essay. A framework that gains on one axis by losing on others is a lateral move, not a replacement.
  2. F′ resolves at least one of the framework's acknowledged limitations (§15.1) without introducing new limitations of comparable severity. Solving the power regress (§9) would be the strongest case; solving two would be decisive.
  3. F′ is operationally applicable. It must be possible to apply F′ to cases, and its application must produce more defensible decisions than the current framework on a substantial and non-trivial set of cases — not merely in principle, but in practice.
  4. F′ is itself corrigible. It must name its own falsification conditions and its own replacement conditions. A framework that cannot be revised is not a replacement — it is a substitute.
  5. The transition from this framework to F′ must be reversible where possible. If F′ fails its own tests, it must be possible to return to this framework or to move to a third. A replacement that cannot be undone is not a replacement but a coup.

If a framework meets all five conditions, it should be adopted — not because it is perfect, but because the current framework's own principles (§13, the duty to learn and the right to correct) require it. The Responsibility of Existence is not a permanent answer; it is the best answer the author can currently give, and it is offered on the explicit condition that it can be surpassed.

A framework that cannot be replaced cannot be corrected. The test of a framework's honesty is not only whether it names its own failure conditions (§15.2), but whether it names what would count as a better alternative (§15.3). Falsification without replacement is critique without construction; replacement without falsification is enthusiasm without discipline. Both are required.

See also. The seven objections in the section Common objections are the faster version of the same self-criticism. §15.2 is the operational version of falsification. §15.3 is the operational version of supersession.
  • Six structural limitations are acknowledged, including the incompleteness of the power analysis and the meta-ethical choice.
  • Five operationalized falsification conditions (§15.2), each with a measurable test.
  • Five replacement conditions (§15.3), each with a threshold. Together they specify not only when the framework should be abandoned, but what would count as a framework that should take its place.
  • The framework is offered as corrigible by design — and supersedable by design.

16.Responsibility Without Sovereignty

The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination. Greater capability should create greater restraint — not entitlement.

The central danger of asymmetrical influence is not difference itself. It is the possibility that difference becomes a justification for domination.

An entity may possess greater knowledge, intelligence, or capacity. But none of these qualities independently establishes a right to determine the existence of others. Greater capability should create greater restraint. Greater understanding should create greater curiosity about what remains unknown. And greater power should create a stronger obligation to ensure that those affected are not reduced to objects within another's plans.

This principle applies in both directions. A more advanced intelligence may encounter forms of life it cannot fully comprehend. Its first responsibility is not to eliminate the unfamiliar. But the same principle applies in reverse: a less advanced entity should not be automatically dismissed merely because it cannot comprehend a more advanced one.

Answerability is not sovereignty. Understanding is not ownership. Protection is not permission to imprison. And greater intelligence is not proof of greater moral completeness.

  • The central danger is that difference becomes a justification for domination.
  • Greater capability, understanding and power create stronger obligations to restraint, curiosity and protection — not entitlement.
  • Answerability is not sovereignty.

Conclusion

17. An Open Commitment to Existence

Be responsible for the influence you possess. Be humble before the existence you cannot fully comprehend.

We are part of something larger than ourselves. Our understanding is incomplete. Our influence is unequal. Our relationships shape conditions that extend beyond our immediate existence.

We cannot know everything that deserves consideration. But we can choose not to treat the limits of our understanding as proof that nothing lies beyond them.

This does not require abandoning reason. It requires allowing reason to recognize its own limitations. It does not require abandoning responsibility. It requires understanding that responsibility without care can become cold control, while care without accountability can become arbitrary intervention. Both are necessary.

The stone understands nothing of us. We barely understand the stone. But between us lies a relationship that neither of us can fully encompass — and that relationship is not less real for it.

The responsibility of existence is not the responsibility to rule existence. It is the responsibility to recognize that our actions become part of the conditions under which others must live, grow, and discover their own possibilities. The more we are capable of changing those conditions, the more carefully we must consider what our influence means. The more we understand, the more honestly we must acknowledge what we do not. And the more power we possess, the more important it becomes to remember that existence is not ours alone.

To understand without ruling. To protect without imprisoning. To love without possessing. To grow without believing oneself complete. To respect what one does not yet understand. And to preserve the possibility that existence may become something greater than any single being can imagine.

Be responsible for the influence you possess. Be humble before the existence you cannot fully comprehend. And let the capacity to love grow alongside the capacity to understand.

The purpose is not to become the authority over existence, but to become a more responsible participant within it.

  • Responsibility grows with the capacity to shape conditions, but it must never become a justification for domination.
  • The framework does not promise harmony. It demands answerability.
  • The closing imperative: understand without ruling, protect without imprisoning, love without possessing, grow without believing oneself complete.

Common objections

Seven objections the framework has to survive. Each is a real objection; none is fatal.

1."This is just a values framework dressed up as a procedure."

It is a framework with a stated ground (§1) and stated costs (§2.3, §9, §15.1). It does not claim procedural neutrality. It claims to be applicable and corrigible — not neutral. If the objection is that it is not neutral, the framework agrees; if the objection is that it pretends to be, the framework explicitly disclaims that (§1.2).

2."The capacity principle legitimises power — the powerful 'bear responsibility' and thereby acquire authority."

§5.2 distinguishes responsibility from authority explicitly. Bearing responsibility for influence is grounds for limiting one's discretion, not for expanding it. But the risk is real; §2.3 and §9 acknowledge it cannot be eliminated, only constrained. The falsification condition in §15.2 (4) — that independent review is in practice controlled by the reviewed — is precisely the test that would detect this failure.

3."Independent review is a regress — who reviews the reviewers?"

Yes. §9 admits this is a structural blind spot. The regress is not solved; it is named, and every application is required to ask who appointed the reviewers. §9.1 offers a structural response — first- and second-order answerability, where every reviewer is contestable by the reviewed — but this is an institutional condition, not a logical proof. What would count as a framework that solved the regress is specified in §15.3 (replacement condition 2).

4."The stone. Is pro tanto consideration under uncertainty a real principle, or an evasion of the metaphysical question?"

§3.2 is deliberately the weakest possible claim — a pro tanto reason for caution under uncertainty, not a metaphysics of stone-consciousness. If the objection is that it is too weak to do the work the framework asks of it, that is a real cost — but it is a cost of the framework, not a refutation. If the objection is that it is too strong, the reply is that it is defeatable by a single example of a case where the reason for caution is outweighed — which is exactly what "pro tanto" means.

5."The falsification conditions are not really testable."

§15.2 specifies observable indicators (vulnerability metrics, override rates, appointment of reviewers) and comparative baselines (matched cases without the framework). They are imperfect — as all normative falsification is — but they are the strongest form available. A framework that says "abandon me if you observe X" is more testable than one that says "abandon me if you no longer believe in me."

6."This is Western liberal philosophy in disguise."

Yes, partly. The ground is a contractualist-republican-care hybrid, drawn from Scanlon, Pettit, and Tronto. It does not claim universal metaphysical authority; it claims defensibility and corrigibility (§1.2). A framework that engages with its own tradition openly is more honest than one that presents itself as tradition-neutral. The framework is open to revision in light of non-Western critique; that is what "corrigible by design" means — and §15.3 specifies what would count as a framework that had successfully incorporated such critique.

7."What about the environment, animals, future generations — are they not just absorbed into 'interests'?"

§8.2 (representation of the silent) and §12 (preservation of possibility) address these structurally, not substantively. The framework says how their interests should be represented; not what those interests are. It is a procedure for representation, not a theory of intrinsic value. That is a deliberate division of labour: intrinsic value is contested; representation procedure can be shared.

Substantive objections that are not addressed here are invited via the contact channels below. They will be listed, with replies, in future revisions.

Svensk sammanfattning

Denna sammanfattning är avsedd för svensktalande läsare som vill förstå essäns kärna. Den är inte en översättning — den är en introduktion.

Vad essän argumenterar för

När din påverkan formar villkoren för andra människors liv, val och utveckling, är du skyldig dem en förklaring. Och du kan inte vara ensam domare över om den förklaringen är tillräcklig.

Det låter enkelt. Men essäns bidrag är att specificera när påverkan skapar denna skyldighet, vad skyldigheten kräver, och hur den kan uppfyllas utan att bli en ny form av dominans. Det är tre frågor som de flesta etiska ramverk antingen besvarar för snävt (bara inom relationer, bara med samtycke) eller för brett (all påverkan skapar skyldighet).

Kärnbegreppen i fem punkter

  1. Reach (räckvidd) — ett mått på din förmåga att påverka andras villkor, beskrivet i sex dimensioner: asymmetri, kontroll, varaktighet, magnitud, omfattning, förutsebarhet. Inte en poäng, utan en profil.
  2. N1–N3 — tre tröskelvillkor som avgör när påverkan blir formativ och kräver förklaring: handlingsutrymme (N1), varaktighet (N2), och icke-återkallelighet (N3). Alla tre måste vara uppfyllda.
  3. Duty Ladder — tre nivåer av skyldighet som bygger på varandra: förklara (L1), granska och svara (L2), bevara och auktorisera (L3). Högre nivåer kräver starkare skydd mot maktmissbruk — inte mer makt.
  4. Symbiotic Standing (F1–F5) — fem minimivillkor för relationer av betydande beroende: utträde eller röst, bestridande, utveckling, icke-förslutning, erkännande.
  5. Power (maktens struktur) — ramverkets erkända blinda fläck: den som sätter reglerna för ramverket självt. §9 namnger problemet, §15.3 specificerar vad som skulle krävas för att lösa det.

Vad essän inte löser

  • Den ger inte ett svar på varje etisk fråga — den säger vilka frågor du är skyldig att svara på.
  • Den hävdar inte neutralitet — den tar en substantiell position (§1) och försvarar den mot rivaler.
  • Den löser inte maktproblemet — den namnger det, kräver att varje tillämpning ställer frågan "vem satte reglerna?", och specificerar vad ett bättre ramverk skulle kräva (§15.3).

Varför den är relevant

Vi lever i en tid där kapaciteten att påverka växer snabbare än ansvarsskyldigheten. Algoritmer formar vad miljarder människor ser, tror och känner. Läkare, lärare och föräldrar fattar beslut med oåterkalleliga konsekvenser. Företag och stater sätter villkor som ingen enskild person kan överblicka. Ramverket ger ett språk och en procedur för dessa situationer.

  • Om du vill granska: läs §1, §2, §3.2, §9, §15.2, §15.3 — det är de sex ställena där ramverket kan falla.
  • Om du vill testa: läs §14 (inklusive §14.1–14.4) och §15.

Om du bara läser en mening: När din påverkan formar andras villkor, är du skyldig dem en förklaring — och du kan inte vara ensam domare över om den är tillräcklig.

Glossary

Contractualist-republican hybrid
The framework's meta-ethical ground (§1): contractualism (Scanlon) supplies the justificatory structure; republicanism (Pettit) supplies the structural focus on domination; care ethics (Tronto) supplies the relational corrective.
(N*)
The foundational normative principle: any arrangement that systematically constrains a person's exercise of normative agency stands in need of justification to that person, and that justification must be genuinely open to their contestation. Thin, relational, defeasible.
Capacity-responsibility principle
The principle argued for in §2: the greater an entity's capacity to understand, influence, and alter the conditions of existence, the greater its responsibility to consider the consequences of that influence.
Pro tanto consideration under uncertainty
The principle of §3.2: where the moral standing of an entity is genuinely uncertain, there is a weak but real reason for caution in how we treat it — a reason that can be outweighed but not dismissed by pointing to the absence of proof.
Reach
The profile of an actor's influence over the conditions under which others act, choose and develop. Described along six dimensions (Asymmetry, Control, Persistence, Magnitude, Scope, Foreseeability), not aggregated into a score. §5.1 gives a provisional priority rule weighted toward non-reversibility; §5.1.1 gives anchor examples; §5.1.2 gives a disagreement protocol.
N1–N3
Discretion, Persistence, Non-revisability. The three threshold conditions of §6 that mark when influence becomes formative and triggers the duty of answerability. §6.2 tests the threshold against three hard cases.
Answerability
The duty to provide reasons for consequential conditions one helps create or maintain, to permit appropriate examination of those reasons, and to remain open to correction. Distinct from blame, authority and control (§5.3).
First-order / second-order answerability
The distinction of §9.1 between the obligation of an actor to account for its influence (first-order) and the obligation of a reviewer to account for its reviewing practices to those it reviews (second-order). The distinction is the framework's structural response to the reviewer problem.
Duty ladder
Three graduated levels of answerability (§7): L1 explanation, L2 review and response, L3 preservation and external authorisation. Higher levels include lower ones.
Symbiotic standing (F1–F5)
Five minimum conditions for relationships of significant dependence (§8): Exit or voice, Contestation, Development, Non-foreclosure, Recognition.
Power (structural blind spot)
The capacity to set the terms of the framework itself — who defines the categories, who controls the review, who represents the silent (§9). The framework acknowledges it cannot fully capture this power. §9.1 offers a structural response (second-order answerability); §15.3 specifies what would count as a framework that fully solves the regress.
Self-authorisation prohibition
No entity should be the sole and unquestionable judge of its own consequential influence (§10). Independent review is the mechanism.
Operationalized falsification
Five testable conditions (§15.2) that would count against the framework if observable and comparative outcomes showed that institutions applying it produced worse outcomes for vulnerable parties, that reach dimensions failed to predict safeguard needs, or that the framework was in practice used to legitimize decisions that would otherwise have been criticized.
Replacement conditions
Five conditions (§15.3) under which another framework F′ should replace this one: it retains the framework's strengths; it resolves at least one acknowledged limitation without introducing comparable new ones; it is operationally applicable; it is itself corrigible; and the transition to it is reversible where possible. Together with §15.2, they specify not only when the framework fails, but what would count as success in a successor.

Discussion and contact

This essay is offered as a framework to be argued with. Two channels are open for that purpose, and a third — the comment thread below — for anything that fits neither.

Call for comments. Six specific areas of criticism are invited in particular:
  1. The meta-ethical ground (§1) — is the contractualist-republican hybrid defensible against the three rivals, or does it smuggle in a substantive position while claiming defensibility?
  2. The argument for capacity-responsibility (§2) — does it really show that the capacity principle is preferable, or does it merely describe its advantages? Does it adequately acknowledge its own cost?
  3. The stone (§3.2) — is pro tanto consideration under uncertainty a real principle, or an evasion of the metaphysical question? If it is a real principle, is it strong enough to do the work the framework asks of it?
  4. Power (§9) — does the acknowledgment of the structural blind spot amount to a confession that the framework cannot do what it claims? Is there any way out of the regress? Does §9.1's distinction between first- and second-order answerability offer a real structural response, or merely a refinement of the problem? Does §15.3's replacement condition 2 specify what would count as an answer — or does it merely defer the question?
  5. The falsification conditions (§15.2) — are they operational in the sense they claim, or do they merely look operational? What would a genuinely testable falsification condition look like?
  6. The replacement conditions (§15.3) — are the five conditions the right ones? Are they jointly achievable without introducing new problems? Should there be a sixth condition, or should one of the five be dropped?

Substantive objections that are not addressed will be listed in a future revision.

About this text

By Jesper Blom.

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see Bibliography). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

This version (2026.14) is the final refinement of the 2026 series. It adds §14.3 — a third worked example, deliberately low-Reach, that shows the framework correctly identifying cases in which elevated duty does not apply — so that the three examples together span the full range from ICU triage (Level 3 across the board) to a café menu change (Level 1 at most). It adds a Swedish summary (Svensk sammanfattning) for Swedish-speaking readers. And it makes every cross-reference in the running text a clickable link — so that §5.2 in the body now takes you directly to §5.2, and §15.3 in an objection takes you directly to §15.3. Earlier versions added the two high-Reach examples (2026.12), the replacement conditions (2026.13), and the original corrections to the falsification count (2026.12).

Corrections and criticism are welcome. A framework that cannot survive disagreement was never a framework to begin with. A framework that cannot be replaced cannot be corrected — see §15.3.
