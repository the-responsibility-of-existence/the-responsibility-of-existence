Abstract

The claim. Substantial, sustained, and consequential discretion over another party's conditions of agency generates distinctive obligations of justification, restraint, and accountability. The paper calls that relation reach, defends it as a normative account of responsibility, and shows that the obligations it generates have the characteristic structure of fiduciary relations.

The argument. The claim is defended through a normative interpretation of equal standing (§4.1–4.5), supported by a constitutive account of agency among agents (§4.6), constrained by an explicit account of the conditions under which reach does not generate such obligations (§5), and defended against six rival specifications of answerability (§7.6). The central contribution is the necessity argument: the fiduciary structure — principal, fiduciary, loyalty, discretion, accountability — is the minimal complete specification of what answerability requires under asymmetric reach.

An eighth concept. Where reach cannot be traced to identifiable agents, the framework introduces structural responsibility (§8.13) — a forward-looking, participatory obligation to contribute to reform of the structures one sustains. This recovers a determinate answer in cases that would otherwise defeat the analysis (§14.2), without displacing liability where liability applies.

What it is not. Not a complete moral theory, not a decision procedure, not a verdict on individual cases. It is a mid-level framework in Beauchamp and Childress's sense, offered as an instrument of analysis.

What remains open. Six failure modes (§A.1), four conditions for restriction (§A.2), a documented case in which the framework fails even with the eighth concept (§14.2), and a list of unresolved problems and known weaknesses (§A.4). These are not footnotes; they are the framework's honesty about its own limits.

In brief

What this paper tries to do. It provides a structured way of reasoning about responsibility when many agents, institutions, and time horizons are involved. It separates things that are routinely conflated: causing something, having control over it, having a task, being answerable, being blameworthy, and being liable. It adds an eighth concept — structural responsibility — for cases in which no identifiable agent can be singled out. And it states four conditions under which a responsibility claim should be limited.

What it is not. Not a complete moral theory. Not a finished decision procedure. Not a verdict on individual cases.

Why it is needed. In practical debates — climate, AI, healthcare, corporate conduct, migration — "responsibility" is used in at least eight distinct senses. This makes disputes undecidable, because the parties are often right in different senses at the same time.

How to use it. Test it against real cases, step by step (Appendix B.1 provides a template). The framework gains credibility by showing both where it works (§14.1) and where it fails even with its full conceptual apparatus (§14.2).

The argument in six steps

The whole argument of this paper can be summarised as a chain of six claims. Sections 4 and 7 defend them in detail; the steps below are the skeleton on which the rest of the text is flesh.

1. Agency is exercised by shaping the conditions under which others act (§4.1, P1).

2. Where one agent systematically shapes another's conditions, that shaping is systematic conditioning — not incidental causation (§4.1, P2; §5).

3. Equal standing requires that systematic conditioning either be attributable to the affected party's own agency or be answerable to them (§4.1, P3–P5).

4. Where attribution fails — because another has played a constitutive role in shaping the conditions — answerability is owed (§4.1, C).

5. Answerability under asymmetric reach requires the fiduciary structure: principal, fiduciary, loyalty, discretion, accountability (§7.1, §7.6).

6. Therefore: those with reach are fiduciaries of those whose conditions they shape. Greater capability generates greater obligation, not greater moral worth (§7.5).

Where reach cannot be traced to identifiable agents, steps 1–6 have no application. For those cases the paper adds an eighth concept — structural responsibility (§8.13) — which is forward-looking rather than backward-looking, participatory rather than causal, and shared rather than distributed. It fills a gap; it does not displace the six-step argument.

The remainder of the paper does three things. It defends steps 1–6 against objections (§5, §7.6, §15), distinguishes the concepts on which they depend (§8), and applies them to concrete cases (§14). Appendices A and B contain the self-assessment and the case-analysis tools — material that matters, but that would obscure the argument if it were woven into it.

1.Introduction

1.1 The problem

Contemporary normative ethics has been organized, to a considerable degree, around questions of rights: what is owed to whom, and on what grounds (Scanlon 1998; Pettit 1997). This approach has generated substantial progress. Yet it leaves an important question insufficiently developed: what follows when an agent has the capacity to shape the conditions under which others live and act?

The problem becomes especially visible where consequences are produced through complex arrangements rather than by a single identifiable actor. Climate change, autonomous systems, and informational infrastructures are not merely cases in which particular rights are violated. They are also cases in which the capacity to produce consequences has expanded beyond the institutions and practices capable of understanding, distributing, and accounting for those consequences (Jonas 1984; Matthias 2004).

This paper's central claim is that responsibility, understood in this context, is not simply a fixed property that a being either possesses or lacks. It is a relation between an agent's capacity to understand, foresee, and influence its surroundings and the consequences that follow from exercising that capacity.

The relation is:

  • Asymmetric: answerability runs toward those whose conditions are shaped, not over them.
  • Graded: its strength varies with the extent, systematicity, and foreseeability of the conditioning.
  • Structural: it can hold whether or not the agent acknowledges it.
  • Relational: it concerns the connection between an agent's reach and the conditions of others.
  • Fiduciary in form: the answerability that arises under asymmetric reach has the five characteristic features of fiduciary relations (§7).

1.2 Methodology

This is a mid-level normative framework in the sense used by Beauchamp and Childress (2019). It does not claim to be a complete moral theory, nor does it claim to settle every particular case. It offers conceptual distinctions, a normative argument, a partial formalization, and a decision heuristic intended to discipline reasoning where capability and accountability have diverged.

Three methodological commitments guide the argument.

First, the framework is coherentist rather than foundationalist. It seeks reflective equilibrium between three considerations: the structural features of agency, the normative importance of equal standing, and considered judgments about cases. None is treated as an unquestionable foundation.

Second, the framework makes claims that can be challenged. Its arguments can be tested against counterexamples, competing accounts, and cases in which its conclusions appear implausible. Its normative argument is vulnerable to counterexamples in which systematic conditioning appears not to generate answerability — a vulnerability addressed directly in §5.

Third, the framework states its limits. It identifies its failure modes (§A.1), conditions under which its application should be restricted (§A.2), unresolved problems (§A.4), and the difference between answerability as a normative relation and answerability as an institutional practice (§8.10).

1.3 Scope and terminology

The paper uses capability rather than intelligence as its central term. Intelligence may contribute to capability, but capability is broader. Institutions, technological systems, collective agents, and ecological processes can shape conditions in ways that matter to the framework without being appropriately described as intelligent in the ordinary cognitive sense.

The framework's scope is normative rather than descriptive. It does not claim that agents actually acknowledge their answerability; it argues that answerability can be owed whether or not it is acknowledged. Nor does it claim that answerability is always enforceable.

2.Philosophical context

Seven traditions are sufficiently close to this framework that failing to distinguish it from them would undermine its claim to make a distinct contribution.

2.1 Jonas

Jonas (1984) grounds responsibility for the future conditions of human existence in an account of the value and purpose of life. The present framework does not depend on that teleological foundation. It grounds answerability in the relationship between agents and those whose conditions they systematically shape. This makes the framework available to readers who reject Jonas's metaphysics, but leaves it exposed to a problem Jonas sought to address: why an agent should care about distant consequences and future beings (§4.6, §15.7).

2.2 Goodin

Goodin (1985) grounds responsibility in vulnerability and dependency. This is close to the present framework, but the emphasis differs. Goodin's account begins with the vulnerable party; the present framework begins with the reach of the agent whose actions shape another's conditions. Reach may capture cases involving diffuse, numerous, or temporally distant affected parties who are not dependent in the ordinary interpersonal sense.

2.3 Young

Young (2011) grounds responsibility in participation in structural processes that produce injustice. The present framework grounds answerability in systematic reach, whether or not injustice has already occurred. The cost is that the framework cannot simply borrow Young's political diagnosis to explain why structural participation generates responsibility. It must provide its own normative argument (§4). Where reach cannot be located in identifiable agents, the framework introduces structural responsibility as an eighth concept (§8.13), whose relation to Young's social-connection model is defended at length there.

2.4 Care ethics

Tronto (1993), Held (2006), and Noddings (1984) locate important aspects of responsibility in relational responsiveness and care. The present framework does not require an agent to experience care or emotional responsiveness; it can therefore apply to institutions and systems that cannot care in the ordinary psychological sense. Its debt to care ethics lies primarily in the content of responsibility. §7.6.2 shows that care ethics, without the fiduciary structure, is incomplete as a specification of answerability under asymmetric reach.

2.5 Fiduciary theory

Fox-Decent and Criddle (2016) argue that authority is legitimate only when exercised in a fiduciary manner on behalf of those subject to it. The present framework accepts much of the fiduciary structure but seeks to extend its relevance beyond formal legal and political authority. The proposed contribution is not the invention of fiduciary responsibility; it is the derivation of its characteristic structure from reach-based answerability (§7).

2.6 Stewardship

Stewardship models often locate responsibility in a steward who holds something in trust for a beneficiary but answers to an owner, sovereign, or community. The present framework rejects any arrangement in which accountability to a third party entirely replaces accountability to the affected party. §7.6.2 shows that stewardship either fails this requirement or collapses into the fiduciary structure.

2.7 Duty of care

Tort-based duties of care are often negative and episodic. They prohibit certain foreseeable harms and are commonly triggered by particular acts or relationships. Reach-based answerability is broader and potentially ongoing. §7.6.2 shows that duty of care, when expanded to meet those requirements, becomes the fiduciary structure.

2.8 Non-domination

Pettit's (1997) republicanism identifies arbitrary power as a central threat to freedom. Reach-based answerability shares this concern but adds a positive requirement. §7.6.2 shows that non-domination is a necessary condition of the fiduciary relation, not an adequate substitute for it.

2.9 The philosophical movement

The framework's proposed contribution is not simply that it integrates ideas from several traditions. It is that it attempts a particular philosophical movement:

The fiduciary structure is treated not as an independent normative commitment imposed upon the relationship, but as a structure that follows from the requirements of answerability under asymmetric reach — and as the minimal complete structure that any adequate specification must contain.

The derivation is developed in §7. It does not merely establish that the fiduciary structure is available as a specification of answerability. It establishes that the fiduciary structure is the minimal complete specification. §7.6 defends this stronger claim.

3.Existence, moral status, and the grounds of consideration

3.1 Existence is not identical to moral status

The fact that something exists does not establish that every entity possesses identical moral standing. A conscious being capable of suffering presents a different moral case from an inanimate object. Rejecting identical moral status does not require treating entities without established moral status as morally irrelevant.

3.2 Four grounds of value

Experiential value
A being capable of suffering, well-being, fear, or other forms of experience has interests that can be affected from its own perspective.
Agency-based value
Capacities for choice, self-direction, understanding, or the pursuit of one's own purposes. Where such capacities exist, preserving meaningful opportunities for their exercise becomes an important moral consideration.
Ecological value
Organisms, species, habitats, and ecological systems may possess value through their contribution to the continuity, diversity, resilience, and functioning of the living world.
Relational and historical value
Significance acquired through relationships, history, role in a community, or contribution to the conditions under which other forms of life and agency become possible.

These grounds can overlap. A forest may support sentient life, sustain human communities, contribute to ecological resilience, and possess historical significance. The framework therefore resists reducing all moral consideration to a single measure.

3.3 Three mistaken inferences

Wrong inference 1: "It exists, therefore it matters." Existence alone does not settle moral status.

Wrong inference 2: "It matters, therefore it may decide." Moral consideration does not automatically confer authority.

Wrong inference 3: "It can, therefore it should rule." Capability does not create entitlement to govern others.

3.4 Uncertain moral status

Some entities may possess forms of experience or agency that are difficult to recognize. The absence of established evidence for moral status is not equivalent to proof that no morally relevant capacities exist. A defensible approach is proportional caution. The more severe or irreversible the possible harm, the stronger the case for investigation and safeguards.

3.5 Future possibilities

An ecosystem may be valuable not only because of the organisms currently inhabiting it, but because it sustains possibilities for future forms of life. Knowledge, cultural continuity, biodiversity, and environmental stability may matter because they preserve opportunities that present generations cannot fully anticipate.

4.The normative bridge: from reach to answerability

4.1 The structural argument

(P1) Agency is exercised by acting on conditions. To act is to shape the space of possibilities in which one acts and in which others may act. Agency is not merely the production of effects; it can involve determining the conditions under which choices become available, meaningful, or possible.

(P2) Systematic social conditioning. When A's actions systematically shape the conditions under which B exercises agency, B's possibilities are partly determined by A. B's agency is thereby heteronomously conditioned in the relevant domain. Systematic conditioning must be distinguished from incidental causal effects.

(P3) Equal standing. Where one agent systematically determines the conditions under which another exercises agency, that determination must either be attributable to the affected agent's own agency or be answerable to that agent in a form that permits meaningful scrutiny and contestation.

(P4) The failure of attribution. Where A systematically determines B's conditions, those conditions cannot be attributed entirely to B's own agency. A has played a constitutive role in shaping them.

(P5) The requirement of answerability. If the conditions cannot be attributed entirely to B's agency, equal standing requires that A be able to give an account of the determination to B. Such an account must be capable, in principle, of being received, examined, contested, and revised.

(C) Conclusion. Where A's reach systematically shapes the conditions under which B exercises agency, A is answerable to B for that shaping.

4.2 Why this interpretation of equal standing?

Four alternative interpretations are available, and each fails.

  • Only non-interference. Too weak: an agent may avoid interfering with any particular decision while still determining the broader conditions under which another makes decisions.
  • Substantive equality of outcome. Too strong: introduces a substantive theory of distribution the framework does not seek to establish.
  • Only attribution to one's own agency. Fails where another agent has substantially shaped the relevant conditions.
  • Fully reciprocal justification. Assumes a symmetry that does not exist in relationships of unequal reach.

The proposed interpretation is a minimal normative position for cases of asymmetric conditioning.

4.3 Why affected parties have a special claim

The people whose agency is being shaped occupy a distinctive position: they bear the consequences of decisions over which they may have little control. Their claim is not necessarily a claim to control the decision. It is a claim that their position must count in its justification.

  1. Being affected establishes a possible interest in the consequences.
  2. Having interests establishes reasons that may deserve moral consideration.
  3. Being systematically subject to another's consequential discretion establishes a stronger claim to justification.
  4. Being deprived of meaningful means of contestation strengthens the need for independent accountability.

4.4 Three features of the argument

First, it is not an argument from causation directly to obligation. P2 concerns systematic conditioning, not mere causal efficacy.

Second, the argument is asymmetric. It establishes answerability to those whose conditions are shaped, not authority over them.

Third, it is graded. The strength and scope of answerability vary with the extent, persistence, foreseeability, control, and asymmetry of the conditioning (§6).

4.5 Three objections

Objection 1: benign imposition. Parents, teachers, and physicians systematically shape the conditions under which others act. Does the framework imply that these relationships are wrong? Reply: No. The framework distinguishes between being answerable and having acted wrongly.

Objection 2: mutual conditioning. It makes each party answerable within the domain of their respective reach. The framework does not imply that every interaction creates unlimited responsibility.

Objection 3: institutional mediation. Individual responsibility must track each agent's actual reach, control, and position within the arrangement. Institutions may themselves be answerable (§11).

4.6 Agency and the practice of giving reasons

Sellars (1956) and Brandom (1994) describe the space of reasons as a practice in which claims, commitments, and actions can be challenged and defended.

(P1') Agents capable of acting for reasons participate, to some degree, in practices of giving and receiving reasons. (P2') Such practices involve commitments that can be questioned, defended, and revised. (P3') An agent who exercises reach over another's conditions makes decisions that can become objects of legitimate scrutiny. (C') The practices through which agents give and receive reasons provide a natural setting for exercising answerability.

The constitutive argument is important but limited. It establishes that answerability is closely connected to agency and reason-giving; it does not prove the full normative argument, identify every affected party, or establish that every agent will recognize or fulfil its answerability.

4.7 The silent-principal objection

Does the framework require answerability to be demanded before it can be owed? No. The framework treats answerability as a normative relation that may exist whether or not the affected party can articulate or enforce a demand. The inability to demand an account does not automatically diminish the significance of the conditions being shaped. It does, however, make representation more difficult (§10.6).

5.The limits of reach

Almost every action affects conditions beyond the agent's immediate intentions. If every such influence generated a special relationship of answerability, the concept would become excessively broad.

5.1 Incidental influence

Where influence is incidental, limited, and not reasonably subject to the agent's control, the grounds for special answerability are correspondingly weak. General moral obligations may still apply.

5.2 Insufficient foreseeability

An outcome may be connected to an agent's actions without having been reasonably foreseeable. But unforeseeability is not a permanent exemption. As evidence accumulates and systems become better understood, the grounds for answerability may change.

5.3 Lack of meaningful control

Responsibility should be proportionate to the actual scope of available action. Lack of total control does not eliminate responsibility for the parts that remain within discretion.

5.4 Diffuse interdependence

Participation is not identical to governance. Stronger answerability arises where an agent has a substantial role in determining the conditions under which others must operate.

5.5 Beneficial influence and legitimate authority

Not all asymmetry is wrongful. But beneficial intentions do not remove the need for answerability. Legitimate authority is not authority without accountability. It is authority whose exercise can be justified in light of the relationship and its purposes.

5.6 A threshold for distinctive answerability

Distinctive answerability is most strongly established where several conditions converge:

  1. The agent substantially shapes another party's conditions of agency.
  2. The influence is sustained, repeated, or sufficiently consequential.
  3. The agent possesses relevant discretion or practical control.
  4. The consequences are reasonably foreseeable, or become foreseeable through information the agent ought to consider.
  5. The affected party has limited ability to contest, influence, or escape the conditions being shaped.

These are not rigid necessary-and-sufficient conditions. The threshold is contextual and proportional.

6.Reach: the six dimensions

The normative argument of §4 depends on the claim that A's reach over B's conditions is systematic. The framework proposes six dimensions.

Magnitude

How substantially can A change B's conditions? The degree of material change in B's relevant possibilities, compared against a defensible baseline. Both expansion and restriction may constitute significant reach.

Duration

How long do the consequences persist? Temporary effects and consequences extending across generations create different forms of responsibility.

Scope

How many individuals, communities, species, or systems may be affected? Scope is relevant but not decisive. A larger number does not automatically outweigh the interests of a smaller group.

Foreseeability

How much could A reasonably understand about likely consequences? Failure to investigate can itself be a failure where the agent had the capability and responsibility to investigate.

Control

How much practical influence does A possess? Formal authority and practical control are not always the same. The framework is concerned with actual capacity to shape or alter conditions.

Asymmetry

How limited are the affected parties in their ability to refuse, exit, contest, or alter the relationship? Asymmetry connects reach to non-domination (§9).

A single measure of reach would conceal important differences. The framework's claim is that responsibility must be assessed through the profile as a whole.

7.Why responsibility is fiduciary

7.1 The derivation

(P1, from §4) A's systematic reach over B's conditions generates answerability to B.

(P2, structure of agency) B's conditions include the conditions under which B pursues interests and exercises agency.

(P3, from P1 + P2) A is answerable to B for the effects of A's reach on those interests and conditions.

(P4, the prohibition on self-serving substitution) If A were free to disregard B's interests and substitute A's own interests within the very domain in which A exercises reach, then answerability to B would become largely nominal.

(P5, the positive requirement) Answerability therefore requires more than the absence of deliberate self-interest. A must take B's interests and capacity for self-direction seriously when exercising the reach that shapes B's conditions.

(C, fiduciary structure) Under conditions of asymmetric reach, answerability takes on five characteristic features:

Principal
B, whose conditions and interests are affected.
Fiduciary
A, whose reach creates the relevant responsibility.
Loyalty
A must not use the relationship merely to advance A's own interests at B's expense.
Discretion
A may have to make decisions that B cannot specify fully in advance.
Accountability
A must be able to explain and justify the exercise of that discretion to B.

7.4 The circularity objection

Does the derivation presuppose what it seeks to prove? No. The argument proceeds in stages: (i) equal standing supports answerability; (ii) answerability requires explainability; (iii) where conditions affect another's interests under asymmetric reach, answerability requires constraints on discretion; (iv) those constraints resemble the characteristic features of fiduciary relations. Giving an account is not itself identical to a fiduciary relationship. The fiduciary structure is a proposed specification of what answerability requires under conditions of asymmetric reach.

7.5 A consequence worth stating

Greater capability does not generate greater moral worth. A being with vast capability is not, for that reason, worth more than a being with modest capability. It is capable of affecting more, and therefore may have more extensive obligations. Its responsibility is greater because its reach is greater, not because its interests count for more.

7.6 The necessity objection: why the fiduciary structure is the minimal complete specification

If the fiduciary structure is merely one possible specification of answerability among several equally adequate ones, then the derivation in §7.1 establishes at most that the fiduciary structure is available, not that it is required. This section argues that the five requirements cannot be jointly satisfied by any specification that lacks the fiduciary structure.

7.6.1 The requirements restated

  • (R1) A principal whose conditions and interests are affected.
  • (R2) Loyalty — a prohibition on substituting the agent's own interests for the principal's.
  • (R3) Discretion — the agent must act within a range the principal cannot fully specify.
  • (R4) Accountability — the agent must explain and justify the exercise of discretion to the principal.
  • (R5) Positive orientation — the agent must take the principal's interests and capacity for self-direction seriously.

7.6.2 Six rivals considered

Stewardship

Locates accountability upward — to an owner, sovereign, community. Fails R4. A steward who is also accountable to the principal has become a fiduciary.

Duty of care

Negative and episodic. Fails R5 (no positive orientation) and R3 (no ongoing discretion). When expanded to include positive orientation, becomes fiduciary.

Non-domination

Specifies what must be avoided, not what must be done. Fails R5 and R2. Adding positive orientation and loyalty produces the fiduciary structure.

Care ethics

Grounds in responsiveness and affect. Fails institutional application and R3. Supplemented structurally, becomes the fiduciary structure.

Kantian justification

Justifies to an idealized rational agent, not the specific affected party. Fails R1 and R5. Specified to the actual party, becomes fiduciary.

Virtue ethics

Specifies what a good agent is like, not relationships. Fails R1 and R4. Can complement but cannot replace the structural account.

7.6.3 The pattern

Each rival fails at least one requirement outright, or meets all requirements only by adding the features that constitute the fiduciary structure. The fiduciary structure is therefore not one specification among many equally adequate ones; it is the minimal complete specification that satisfies all five requirements simultaneously.

This is not a claim that the rivals are worthless. Each captures something important. What the framework denies is that any of them, taken alone, is an adequate specification of answerability under asymmetric reach. Each is a partial specification; the fiduciary structure is the minimal complete one.

7.6.4 Three limits

First, the argument establishes that the fiduciary structure is the minimal complete specification, not that it is the only possible one. A more complex structure meeting all five requirements would not be ruled out.

Second, the argument depends on R1–R5 being the right requirements. If a critic rejects one, the argument fails for that critic.

Third, the argument is a comparative claim about rival traditions as they are standardly formulated. A version of stewardship, duty of care, or non-domination that already includes the features I claim they lack is either already a fiduciary structure under another name, or a specification the framework should incorporate.

The fiduciary structure is not merely available as a specification of answerability. It is the minimal complete specification — the structure that any adequate specification must at least contain.

8.Responsibility, answerability, accountability, and culpability

8.1 Why distinctions matter

The language of responsibility often combines several different ideas. An agent may cause an outcome without being blameworthy. It may owe an explanation without having acted wrongly. It may possess a duty to repair without having been the sole cause of the harm.

8.2 Causal responsibility

Whether an agent contributed to producing an outcome. Relevant but not sufficient for blame or repair.

8.3 Control responsibility

The agent's practical capacity to initiate, modify, prevent, or reverse relevant conditions. May be partial or distributed.

8.4 Responsibility

Obligations attached to an agent's position, actions, capacities, and relationships. May be prospective or retrospective.

8.5 Answerability

The obligation to provide reasons, justify decisions, and remain open to examination and revision.

8.6 Accountability

Institutional or social arrangements through which answerability can be examined and enforced.

8.7 Culpability

Blameworthiness in light of knowledge, intentions, alternatives, and reasonable opportunities.

8.8 Reparative responsibility

Obligations to prevent, correct, or remedy adverse outcomes. Do not always correspond to degrees of blame.

8.10 Answerability as relation and as practice

Relation: A is answerable to B. Practice: something exercised through institutions, procedures, explanations, hearings, review, and opportunities for contestation.

The relation can exist without an adequate practice. Institutions can participate in answerability without possessing individual agency in the same sense as persons. And the distinction clarifies the framework's limits: it specifies what answerability requires, not the complete institutional design for exercising it.

8.11 Capability borrowed is responsibility retained

A system may produce consequential outcomes without possessing the intentions or understanding required for ordinary moral blame. Responsibility must therefore be examined across the wider arrangement of designers, operators, institutions, and decision-makers. The existence of a system does not automatically eliminate the responsibility of those capable of governing its use.

8.12 Summary table: eight concepts and their test questions

The distinction developed in this section is not merely verbal. Each concept carries its own criteria, and each can be applied without the others. The table below summarises the operative difference. An answerability claim that cannot be restated in one of these rows is, on the framework's account, under-specified.

Concept Definition Test question
Causal responsibility Contribution to producing an outcome. Would the outcome have differed without the contribution?
Control responsibility Practical capacity to initiate, modify, prevent, or reverse relevant conditions. Could the agent have done otherwise, given its position?
Responsibility (as task) Obligations attached to a position, action, capacity, or relationship. Who has the task of handling this?
Answerability Obligation to provide reasons, justify decisions, and remain open to revision. To whom is an account owed, and by whom?
Accountability Institutional or social arrangements through which answerability is examined and enforced. Is there a forum, procedure, or addressee for the account?
Culpability Blameworthiness in light of knowledge, intentions, alternatives, and opportunities. Is the agent sufficiently free, informed, and capable to be blamed?
Reparative responsibility Obligations to prevent, correct, or remedy adverse outcomes. Is there a basis for repair, independent of blame?
Structural responsibility Forward-looking obligation to contribute to reform of a structural process in which one participates. Does the structure systematically shape conditions, and does the agent have capacity to contribute to reform?
Rule of application. No responsibility ascription is complete until it specifies which of the eight concepts is being claimed. A claim that conflates two of them is, in the framework's terms, undecidable. This is the single most practically useful output of the paper.

8.13 Structural responsibility

The seven concepts above assume that responsibility can be located in an identifiable agent or in an institution with identifiable decision procedures. Where it cannot — where reach is distributed across many agents, none of whom has a determinable share, and there is no addressee for answerability — the framework lacks a category. §14.2 documents this directly. This section proposes the missing category.

Structural responsibility
The forward-looking obligation to contribute to the reform or mitigation of a structural process in which one participates, where that process systematically shapes the conditions of agency of others in ways that cannot be traced to identifiable individual contributions.

Five features

  1. Forward-looking, not backward-looking. The question is what should be done about this structure?, not who caused this?
  2. Participatory, not causal. It applies to those who sustain or benefit from the structure, whether or not their contribution is causally traceable.
  3. Shared, not distributed. It cannot be portioned out. It is held jointly, with each participant having an obligation to contribute to reform proportioned to capacity.
  4. Does not generate culpability. Participants in structural responsibility are not blameworthy for the structure.
  5. Generates answerability only at decision points. Where the structure has identifiable decision-makers, those decision-makers become answerable in the ordinary way (§7). Where it does not, structural responsibility is an obligation without a specific addressee — which is exactly what §8.10 predicts a normative relation can be.

Five conditions for application

  1. The structural process must systematically shape conditions of agency.
  2. Participation must be non-trivial — the agent must sustain or benefit from the structure.
  3. The structure must be identifiable as a structure — there must be some way of describing it that is not a mere aggregate of individual actions.
  4. The agent must have capacity to contribute to reform.
  5. No ordinary reach-based answerability may already cover the case. Structural responsibility fills a gap; it does not displace the seven other concepts.

Three limiting conditions

  1. The category must not be used to obscure ordinary reach-based responsibility.
  2. It must not be used to attribute blame.
  3. It must not be used to demand unlimited forward-looking obligation — the obligation is to contribute to reform, proportioned to capacity.

Relation to Young

Young's (2011) social-connection model is the primary source for this category. Her central claim is that responsibility for structural injustice is not about blame or liability but about participation. The framework's structural responsibility belongs to the same family. This has led to the obvious objection — that §8.13 is Young's model under another name — and the objection deserves a direct answer.

The objection. Structural responsibility, as defined above, adds nothing to Young's social-connection model. All five features and all five conditions are already present in Young. Calling it an "eighth category" is terminological repackaging, not a new contribution.

Reply, first layer: what Young does not do. Young's model is a free-standing political theory of responsibility for structural injustice. It stands alone: it replaces the liability model for a certain class of cases and claims to be the correct understanding of those cases. §8.13 is not free-standing. It is one of eight categories, with an explicit transition rule (§11.11) and an explicit relation to the seven other concepts of §8. The difference is not cosmetic: a free-standing model must carry the whole analysis itself, whereas a situated category need only carry the part of the analysis the other seven do not cover.

This is the weakest of the three replies, and it should be said plainly. A critic may reply that architectural situation is a property of this paper, not of the category — Young's model could be situated in the same way. That reply is partly correct, which is why a second layer is needed.

Reply, second layer: what Young cannot do. Young's model says nothing about what happens when the structure has identifiable decision points. She is not uninterested in the question, but her model is not built to answer it — it is built to explain why participation in structures generates responsibility even when no individual is at fault. Feature 5 of §8.13 does something Young's model does not: it ties structural responsibility to the framework's account of answerability. Where the structure has decision points, decision-makers become answerable in the ordinary way (§7). Where it does not, structural responsibility is an obligation without a specific addressee — which §8.10 has already predicted a normative relation can be.

This is a real difference, not a terminological one. Young's model treats structural injustice as a homogeneous category. §8.13 treats structural responsibility as a category with internal structure — one that can coexist with ordinary answerability in the same case, and that can therefore be used in cases where Young's model can only say "this is structural".

Reply, third layer: compatibility, not competition. §8.13 is not formulated as a competing model to Young. It is formulated as a complement: a situating of what Young has already shown, within a framework that needs to distinguish eight different concepts. If Young is right — and she probably is — then what she says about structural injustice is correct. §8.13 does not add a new theory of injustice. It adds a place in a framework where Young's insight can coexist with reach-based responsibility without either swallowing the other.

This is not a retreat. It is a clarification of what the category claims. A critic who says "this is Young" is right on the substance and wrong on the conclusion: yes, it builds on Young, and no, that does not make it redundant — because the framework needs a place where Young's insight can live together with the other seven concepts.

What falls if the critic is right. If a critic still holds that §8.13 adds nothing to Young, what is lost?

  • §14.2's recovery falls in part. If §8.13 is Young under another name, migration remains a case in which the framework fails with its own categories — the eighth concept does not rescue §14.2, it merely borrows an answer from another tradition.
  • §11.11 stands. The transition rule — §11.1 applies when agents are identifiable, §8.13 when they are not — is independent of whether §8.13 is original. It is an architectural observation about the framework, not a theoretical contribution.
  • The §8.12 table stands. That structural responsibility is one of eight concepts with its own test question is an operational point, not a theoretical one.

The defence of §8.13 is therefore partly independent of whether the category is original. A critic who is right that it is Young is wrong that it is redundant.

Verification required before publication. The characterisation of Young in §2.3 and in this subsection is based on the standard reading of Responsibility for Justice (2011), not on a fresh reading of the primary source. Four claims in particular require verification: (i) that Young grounds responsibility in participation in structural processes; (ii) that her model is free-standing in the sense claimed above; (iii) that she does not make the answerability connection attributed to §8.13; (iv) that "social connection" is her own term. Claim (iii) is the most vulnerable, because it is a claim about the absence of an argument, and absence is harder to establish than presence.

9.Protection, freedom, and non-domination

Protection is not automatically benevolent. An agent may claim to act for another's benefit while unnecessarily restricting that being's ability to make choices. A protective relationship can become one of domination when the protected party is permanently denied meaningful self-direction.

9.5 Protection without captivity

The framework proposes five constraints on legitimate protective action:

  1. Proportionality. The intervention must be proportionate to the seriousness of the risk.
  2. Minimality. It must be limited to what is necessary.
  3. Scrutiny. It must be open to independent review.
  4. Revisability. It must be reconsidered when circumstances change.
  5. Orientation. It must seek to preserve or restore self-direction wherever possible.

Protection that persistently fails these constraints risks becoming domination, regardless of how benevolent its stated purpose may be.

9.6 The right to develop

A powerful agent should not automatically prevent another from taking every risk. The relevant question is whether the risk is sufficiently serious and unjustified to warrant intervention, and whether less restrictive alternatives exist.

10.Cosmic symbiosis: a regulative ideal of coexistence

10.2 Symbiosis as a regulative ideal

Cosmic symbiosis is not presented as an established scientific description of universal harmony. It expresses the aspiration to preserve the conditions under which different forms of existence can continue, develop, and coexist without unnecessary domination or destruction. It performs three functions:

  1. Specifies the orientation of fiduciary responsibility. The fiduciary is answerable not merely for another's survival, but for the conditions under which the other can continue to develop as a distinct form of existence.
  2. Rejects certain resolutions. The destruction or erasure of one party cannot be treated as an ordinary solution to a conflict.
  3. Directs attention toward the conditions of difference. Symbiosis is active rather than passive.

10.3 Two contrasts

Symbiosis is not fusion. It does not aim at absorbing the other into oneself, even where the absorption is described as benevolent.

Symbiosis is not indifference. It does not mean leaving the other alone regardless of the consequences.

10.6 The silent principal problem

Future generations cannot directly express their interests. Ecosystems cannot provide conventional consent. Possible artificial agents may present unfamiliar forms of agency or experience. The absence of direct participation does not make their interests or value irrelevant.

Three disciplines are necessary: revisability (the interpretation must be open to revision as evidence changes), contestation (it must be open to criticism by those who can speak from relevant positions), and self-direction (it must be oriented toward preserving the principal's future capacity for agency).

These disciplines do not eliminate the problem. They establish minimum conditions for responsible representation.

11.Distributed responsibility

11.1 The constraint

Where an outcome is produced by multiple agents, a distribution of answerability is justifiable only if each agent's share is sensitive to that agent's reach profile across the six dimensions (§6).

11.4 A partial metric

The six dimensions can be represented as a profile: r_i = (m_i, d_i, s_i, f_i, c_i, a_i), where each value is assessed on a normalized scale from 0 to 1. The numbers are not direct measurements of moral responsibility. They are structured assessments of relevant features of reach.

11.6 A provisional distribution rule

A weighted representation can be used to compare reach profiles:

q_i = Σ w_k · r_ik

Where a proportional allocation is appropriate, a provisional share can be represented as σ_i = q_i / Σ q_j. This is not a universal formula for moral or legal responsibility. It is a way to make assumptions visible.

The weights require justification in relation to the purpose and context of the assessment. A defensible application should state the chosen weights and explain why they are appropriate, test whether the result changes substantially under reasonable alternative weights, identify dimensions that cannot be meaningfully quantified, and avoid presenting numerical output as a substitute for normative judgment.

11.7 A worked example

Consider three agents involved in the deployment of an algorithmic hiring system. Suppose the following profiles are assessed:

Agent m d s f c a
Designer0.70.60.80.90.80.7
Deployer0.80.50.60.70.90.6
Beneficiary0.40.40.50.40.30.5

With weights favoring control and asymmetry: w = (0.15, 0.10, 0.10, 0.20, 0.25, 0.20), the scores are approximately 0.765 (designer), 0.715 (deployer), and 0.405 (beneficiary). Normalized shares are approximately 40.6%, 37.9%, and 21.5%.

This example does not establish that the framework's distribution is correct. It shows that the formalization produces determinate, testable, and contestable outputs. That is what a partial formalization is supposed to do.

11.10 A gesture toward systemic responsibility

An institution is not merely an aggregate of individual agents. It is a structured arrangement of roles, procedures, information flows, and decision points. When such an arrangement systematically shapes the conditions of others, the institution itself can be treated as a locus of answerability — not because it has intentions or experiences, but because its structure determines the conditions under which individuals act.

The conditions of this claim are strict. An institution can be a locus of answerability only if it has identifiable decision procedures, can give an account of those procedures through identifiable spokespersons or records, and can revise those procedures in response to contestation. Where these conditions fail, the framework's account of institutional answerability is incomplete.

11.11 How structural responsibility extends distributed responsibility

§11.1–11.10 concern cases in which multiple identifiable agents contribute to an outcome, and in which the framework's task is to distribute answerability among them. §8.13 introduces a different case: where the relevant reach cannot be distributed because it cannot be traced, but where participation in a structure nonetheless generates obligation.

The two are not in competition. Distributed responsibility (§11.1) applies where the agents and their contributions are identifiable. Structural responsibility (§8.13) applies where they are not. The distinction is not a matter of degree — it is a difference in kind. A case that cannot be handled by §11.1 is not a harder case of distribution; it is a case of a different type.

The consequence for practice is that a framework user should ask, at step 3 of the template (Appendix B.1), whether causal contribution can be identified at all. If it can, §11 applies. If it cannot, §8.13 applies. The two answers will often coexist in the same case: identifiable decision-makers may be answerable in the ordinary way while the wider structure in which they operate is a matter of structural responsibility.

12.Tragic conflict, irreversibility, and existential risk

12.1 The limits of moral resolution

Not every conflict admits of a solution in which all legitimate interests are preserved. Some decisions involve tragic loss: a situation in which every available option carries morally significant costs.

12.3 Irreversibility

Irreversible decisions deserve particular scrutiny. Where two options offer comparable protection, preference should ordinarily be given to the option that preserves the greatest possibility of correction. Reversibility is not an absolute requirement. Some emergencies may demand irreversible action. Such action requires stronger justification.

12.5 Tragic responsibility

Where no option avoids serious loss, responsible action requires more than selecting a preferred outcome. It requires acknowledging the costs imposed, explaining why alternatives were insufficient, limiting avoidable harm, and accepting accountability for the decision.

12.6 Annihilation and existential threat

Cosmic symbiosis rejects the idea that destroying one party is an ordinary or acceptable way to settle a conflict. It does not, however, establish that every threat must be tolerated or that self-defense is impermissible. Where no non-destructive alternative exists, the framework recognizes a tragic conflict rather than pretending the decision is morally uncomplicated.

13.Conflicting claims: priority, proportionality, and legitimate intervention

13.3 A structured order of consideration

When legitimate claims conflict, deliberation should proceed through the following considerations.

  1. Serious threats. Identify threats to life, fundamental interests, meaningful self-determination, and the conditions necessary for continued existence.
  2. Domination and irreversibility. Particular caution is required where intervention may permanently remove an individual's capacity for independent action or destroy irreplaceable ecological systems.
  3. Maximum feasible coexistence. Where several options can prevent serious harm, prefer the option that preserves the greatest meaningful freedom and broadest conditions for continued coexistence.
  4. Fair distribution of burdens. Examine who bears the burdens, whether those parties are already disadvantaged, and whether the burdens can be reduced or shared more fairly.
  5. Review and revision. Decisions should remain open to reassessment when evidence changes or assumptions prove mistaken.

13.5 Catastrophic risk

A credible threat of catastrophic harm may require exceptional intervention. However, the language of catastrophe is vulnerable to abuse. Exceptional intervention should require exceptionally severe threatened harm, credible supporting evidence, sufficient urgency to make ordinary procedures inadequate, examination of less restrictive alternatives, proportionality, limits on duration and scope wherever possible, and independent review as soon as circumstances permit.

13.6 No single decision-maker

No individual, institution, government, or artificial intelligence should possess unquestionable authority to determine that catastrophic intervention is necessary. The greater the power exercised, the stronger the requirement for accountability beyond the decision-maker's own judgement.

14.Applications

A framework earns its keep by what it can do with cases. The three below are chosen for a specific reason: the first shows the framework working as intended, the second shows it initially failing and then partially recovering through the eighth concept, and the third shows it extending to a domain for which it was not designed. Additional worked cases, together with the template used to produce them, are in Appendix B.

14.1 A case in which the framework works: healthcare prioritisation

The case. A regional health authority must allocate scarce critical-care capacity between two patient groups during a sustained surge. Politicians set funding. Officials draft guidelines. Clinicians apply them at the bedside. No single agent controls the outcome.

Reach. Politicians have high reach over capacity, low reach over bedside application. Officials have moderate reach over both. Clinicians have low reach over capacity, high reach over the specific decision. Asymmetry is high for patients, low for politicians.

Answerability. Politicians answer to citizens through elections; officials to ministers through administrative hierarchy; clinicians to patients and professional bodies. Each form of answerability has a distinct addressee and a distinct forum — which is exactly what §8.10 requires.

Culpability. A clinician following a legitimate guideline should have low culpability. The framework cleanly separates culpability from responsibility as task, and locates accountability at the level where discretion is actually exercised.

Verdict. The framework works. The clinician should not bear the moral weight of a system-level allocation decision she did not design; the politician should not evade the democratic accountability of the framework she set. This case is the framework's benchmark — the strongest application in the paper.

14.2 A case in which the framework initially fails: migration

The case. A regional refugee crisis requires coordinated reception across several states. No single state can absorb the flow alone; each has partial capacity and partial willingness.

Reach. Distributed and non-traceable. No state is the sole cause of the crisis; many contribute through foreign policy, arms exports, and economic arrangements. The relevant reach cannot be assigned to identifiable agents in identifiable proportions.

Answerability. Diffuse. There is no single addressee to whom an account is owed, and no forum capable of enforcing it. The regional body is weak; the international legal framework is under-enforced.

Initial verdict. With the seven concepts of §8, the framework fails here. Its categories assume that reach and answerability can be located in identifiable agents. Where they cannot, the framework has nothing determinate to say.

Recovery through §8.13. The structural-responsibility category recovers a partial answer. States participate in the international structure that produces refugee crises: through arms exports, economic arrangements, foreign policy, and the maintenance of a weak regional framework. Each has capacity to contribute to reform. Each therefore has a forward-looking obligation to contribute to reform, proportioned to capacity. No state is culpable for the crisis; each is structurally responsible. The regional body, to the extent it has identifiable decision points, becomes answerable in the ordinary way.

What remains unresolved. The obligation is diffuse. "Contribute to reform" is not a determinate share. Structural responsibility does not generate liability, and it does not resolve the question of what a fair distribution of reception would be. §14.2 therefore remains a partial failure: the eighth concept recovers a determinate answer at the level of obligation, but not at the level of allocation. This is acknowledged in Appendix A.4.

What this shows. The framework's categories are not fixed. Where a case defeats them, the right response is to ask what category is missing — not to declare the case unanalysable. §8.13 is the result of asking that question about §14.2.

14.3 A case in which the framework is tested: AI systems and the trolley problem

The case. An autonomous vehicle faces an unavoidable collision. The classical trolley-problem question — which trade-off is correct — is not what the framework addresses. The framework addresses a different question: who is answerable for the conditions under which the decision is made?

Reach. The designer has reach over the decision architecture. The deployer has reach over operating conditions. The beneficiary may have reach through decisions to purchase, commission, or deploy. None of them owns the entire outcome.

Answerability. The designer is answerable to the deployer and, indirectly, to affected parties. The deployer is answerable to the operator and, via regulation, to the public. The beneficiary's answerability is mediated by the terms on which it acquires the system.

Verdict. The framework does not resolve the underlying moral dilemma. It resolves a different question: who must be able to answer for the conditions under which this trade-off was made, and to whom? That question can have a meaningful answer even where the dilemma remains unresolved.

What this shows. The framework is not a moral algorithm; it is a discipline for locating accountability when substantive moral questions remain open. That is not a weakness — it is the appropriate scope of a framework of this kind.

15.Objections and replies

Three objections are strong enough that the framework cannot proceed without answering them. The epistemic-justice objection concerns who is entitled to make the relevant judgments; the consequentialist objection concerns whether the framework is answering the right question; the political-realist objection concerns whether normative analysis of this kind can matter at all. A further set of objections — care ethics, virtue ethics, non-ideal theory — is considered in Appendix A.3, together with the failure modes (§A.1) and the four conditions under which the framework's application should be restricted (§A.2).

15.1 The epistemic-justice objection

Objection. The framework depends on judgments about capability, foreseeability, and reach. These judgments will be made by those already in positions of power, and they will tend to reproduce the power structures they purport to examine. The framework is therefore structurally biased toward the powerful.

Reply. The objection is correct as far as it goes, and it is why the framework treats contestability as a structural requirement rather than a procedural nicety. Judgments about capability and reach must remain open to challenge; where they cannot be, the framework's application should be suspended (§A.2). The asymmetric caution principle (Glossary) is the further requirement that capacity should be presumed where it is uncertain, because denial of capacity has historically been the mechanism of exclusion.

Residual cost. Contestability does not eliminate the risk of circularity; it mitigates it. The framework's claim is not that epistemic justice is achieved, but that it is a condition of legitimate application.

15.2 The consequentialist objection

Objection. The framework distributes responsibility by relations and roles but ignores aggregate outcomes. In practice it is the sum of consequences that matters, not the assignment of formal answerability. A framework that does not weight outcomes cannot guide action.

Reply. The framework is not a decision procedure for cases; it is an instrument for allocating answerability when cases are contested. It can be combined with consequentialist analysis at step 4 of the template (Appendix B.1), where control and capacity are assessed. The combination is not a concession; it is what a mid-level framework is for.

Residual cost. The framework must say explicitly how it is to be combined with consequentialist reasoning. If it does not, it will function in practice as a normative ritual that has no purchase on the cases that matter most. §A.1 identifies this as one of the six failure modes.

15.3 The political-realist objection

Objection. Responsibility is already distributed by power. A normative framework that does not analyse power risks legitimising existing distributions by redescribing them as "reach". On this view, the framework's claim to locate answerability is illusory: the powerful will always be able to shape the criteria by which their own reach is assessed.

Reply. This is the framework's weakest point with respect to the social sciences, and it should be acknowledged as such. But the reply is not empty. First, the framework is explicitly normative: it is a proposal about what should be answerable, not a description of what currently is. Second, the framework incorporates a power criterion into its account of reach — asymmetry (Dimension 6) is a measure of power. Third, §A.2's conditions for restriction are designed precisely to prevent the framework from being used to legitimise existing arrangements.

Residual cost. The reply is partial. A full response would require the framework to integrate an account of how normative claims about reach are themselves shaped by existing power — a task beyond the scope of this paper, and acknowledged as one of the three unresolved problems (§A.4).

15.4 Falsifiability: how the framework can be tested

The framework is not an empirical theory, and it cannot be falsified in the way a scientific hypothesis can. But it can be tested, in the weaker sense that matters for normative proposals. Three kinds of test are available:

  1. Counterexample tests. Cases in which the framework yields a verdict that conflicts with a broad and stable considered judgment.
  2. Discrimination tests. Cases that ought to be distinguished but that the framework treats identically, or cases that ought to be treated identically but that the framework distinguishes.
  3. Failure-mode tests. Cases in which one of the six failure modes (§A.1) is triggered in practice, and in which the framework has no internal resource for detecting the failure.

§14.2 documents a case of the first and second kind (migration). Appendix B.3 documents a case of the third (market power in supply chains). The framework's own claim to self-awareness depends on this kind of documentation being undertaken openly rather than being avoided.

15.5 What would count as a refutation

The framework would be seriously undermined if:

  • A systematic class of cases could be shown in which sustained, consequential, and asymmetrical reach does not generate any answerability whatsoever; or
  • It could be shown that the fiduciary structure is not the minimal complete specification, and that a rival that genuinely lacks the five requirements nevertheless satisfies all of them under another name; or
  • It could be shown that the framework cannot, even in principle, be applied in the second-best scenarios in which responsibility claims are actually made.

None of these has been shown. But the framework's defence is therefore conditional, not absolute, and should be stated as such.

16.Conclusion

The framework can be stated in a sentence: capability extends reach, reach shapes conditions, and where those conditions are systematically shaped by another, answerability arises — in a fiduciary form.

Responsibility is not a measure of worth. It is a normative consequence of determining, to a significant degree, the conditions under which others exercise agency.

The framework is not a complete solution to the many-hands problem. It offers a constraint, a partial formalization, and a gesture toward institutional answerability. Where reach cannot be located in identifiable agents, it adds an eighth concept — structural responsibility — which is forward-looking rather than backward-looking, participatory rather than causal, and shared rather than distributed. That concept does not resolve allocation questions; it recovers a determinate answer at the level of obligation.

Its central demand is modest but consequential:

Those who possess greater power to shape the conditions of others should be prepared to answer for that power.

Their answerability should take a form that preserves rather than replaces the agency of those affected. Protection should not become captivity. Capability should not become entitlement. Difference should not be treated as a problem that must always be eliminated.

The guiding ideal is not control over existence, but responsibility within coexistence. Cosmic symbiosis names that orientation: sustaining the conditions under which different forms of existence can continue and develop, while respecting their distinctness.

Preserve the conditions of coexistence. Respect the agency of those capable of exercising it. Approach what remains unknown with humility rather than indifference.

Appendix A. Limits and self-assessment

This appendix collects material that belongs to the paper's honesty about itself: the failure modes, the conditions under which it should not be applied, the objections not strong enough to require a full section, and the problems it does not solve. It is placed here rather than in the main argument because the main argument should not be interrupted by its author's misgivings. But the misgivings are real, and they should be read.

A.1 Six failure modes

  1. Holism abused. Talk of symbiosis and interdependence can justify control in the name of a larger whole.
  2. Capability hard to assess. Judgments about understanding, control, and foreseeability are uncertain and may reflect the interests of those making them.
  3. Not fully operationalized. The partial formalization makes choices explicit and demonstrates them on a test case, but it has not been validated across a broad range of cases.
  4. Licensing control as care. Historically, paternalistic power has often justified itself through claims of superior knowledge or capability. The framework's response is the constraints on protection (§9.5).
  5. Privileging cognition. By emphasizing understanding and foreseeability, the framework may undervalue forms of influence that operate through ecological, collective, or distributed processes.
  6. Emergent systems. Where an outcome is produced by a sociotechnical system whose behavior cannot be traced to identifiable agents, the framework lacks a complete account. (Structural responsibility, §8.13, is the partial response.)

A.2 Four conditions for restriction

The framework's application should be restricted under the following conditions. These are not exceptions to the argument; they are markers of where the argument cannot yet be responsibly extended.

  1. Where epistemic contestation is impossible. If the judgments on which the framework depends cannot be challenged by those affected, the framework becomes an instrument of domination rather than a discipline of answerability.
  2. Where the interests of a silent principal are genuinely unknowable. Where future generations or non-human entities are affected but their interests cannot be responsibly inferred, the framework should not be used to claim authority over them.
  3. Where reach analysis is used to displace legitimate political contestation. The framework is an instrument of analysis, not a substitute for democratic deliberation.
  4. Where people are reduced to units in a distribution. The partial formalization in §11 is a tool, not a measure of human worth. Applications that treat numerical output as the answer have failed to use the framework as intended.

A.3 Further objections

Care ethics

Objection. The framework formalises relations that in practice are dependencies, forms of care, and asymmetries. It risks making care responsibility invisible, or treating it as a special case of a general structure that was built without it.

Reply. Care ethics is treated seriously in §2.4 and §7.6.2. The framework does not deny that relational responsiveness matters. It claims that the fiduciary structure contains the requirements that care ethics rightly insists on, and adds the institutional conditions that care ethics alone cannot supply. A framework that tried to capture the phenomenology of care would cease to be a framework and become a phenomenology; the two are complements, not substitutes.

Virtue ethics

Objection. Responsibility is a virtue — a form of excellence in the exercise of agency — not a structural relation. On this view the framework has mistaken a character trait for a juridical structure.

Reply. The framework accepts the virtue dimension. Answerability can be understood both as an obligation and as a virtue: an agent who anticipates consequences, prepares an account, receives contestation without defensiveness, and revises in response exercises a form of excellence. The virtue response does not establish that every agent will be motivated to develop it; it claims only that the exercise of the virtue is a form of excellence. The two accounts are complementary.

Non-ideal theory

Objection. The framework assumes rationality, transparency, and institutional capacity that are frequently absent. It is a theory for well-ordered societies, not for the world in which responsibility claims are actually made.

Reply. The four limiting conditions (§A.2) are designed precisely for this. The framework is intended to be applied in second-best scenarios, and its normative claims are graded, not absolute. If the framework turns out to work only under good institutional conditions, its practical reach is limited — and this should be acknowledged as a genuine limitation.

A.4 Unresolved problems and known weaknesses

The framework does not solve the following problems. They are stated here rather than in the main text so that the argument can be assessed on its own terms.

  1. Collective, non-localisable responsibility. §8.13 introduces a category — structural responsibility — that addresses the gap documented in §14.2. But the category is narrow: it generates forward-looking obligation, not liability, and it does not resolve allocation questions. The problem is therefore addressed, not solved. A fuller account would need to say how structural obligations interact with ordinary reach-based obligations where both apply, and how "capacity to contribute to reform" is to be assessed. The category's relation to Young's social-connection model is defended in §8.13; the defence is partly independent of whether the category is original, but the Young-referencing must be verified against the primary source before publication.
  2. Market power and informal relations. The fiduciary structure tends to over-weight formal contractual relations and under-weight informal market power. Appendix B.3 proposes a market-power criterion; it is not yet integrated into the main argument.
  3. Second-best scenarios. The framework's applicability outside well-ordered institutions is untested. The four limiting conditions (§A.2) are a placeholder, not a solution.
  4. Motivation. The framework explains why answerability arises but not why agents should be motivated to fulfil it. The virtue of answerability (§A.3) is offered as a partial response, not as a solution.
  5. Empirical validation. The partial formalization in §11 produces determinate outputs on a single test case. It has not been validated across a range of cases, and it may not generalise.
  6. Power and legitimacy. The political-realist objection (§15.3) is acknowledged but not fully answered. A framework that does not analyse power risks legitimising existing distributions.

Appendix B. Case analysis template and additional cases

The template below is a working tool, not a normative claim. The cases that follow are applications of it. Together they extend the three cases of §14 to other domains, and they show both where the framework is useful and where it strains.

B.1 Template for case analysis

  1. Case description. State the case in plain terms, without prejudging the verdict.
  2. Agents and institutions. Identify every party whose actions or structures are relevant.
  3. Causal contribution. Who contributed to producing the outcome, and in what way? (If no identifiable contribution can be found, skip to §8.13's structural responsibility test.)
  4. Control and capacity. Who could have acted otherwise, and at what cost?
  5. Responsibility as task. Who has the task of handling this, and on what basis?
  6. Answerability and accountability. Who must give an account, and to whom?
  7. Culpability and liability. Is there a basis for blame, or for repair independent of blame?
  8. Limiting conditions. Do any of §5.6's criteria fail?
  9. Intuitive verdict. What does a considered, pre-theoretical judgment say?
  10. Framework verdict. What does the framework say?
  11. Deviation and revision. If the two diverge, which should be revised, and why?

Steps 9–11 are the most important. A framework that never diverges from intuition is not adding anything; a framework that diverges without argument is not earning its keep. The most valuable case is one in which the framework fails, because that is where it shows its limits.

B.2 Climate responsibility

1. Case description. A mid-sized industrial state has historically contributed a moderate share of cumulative emissions, currently has high per-capita emissions, and possesses limited sovereign control over its energy system because of regional grid integration.

2. Agents and institutions. The state, its government, its utilities, its citizens, regional grid authorities, and the international bodies with which it negotiates.

3. Causal contribution. Moderate historically; high per capita currently. Causation is real but shared.

4. Control and capacity. High fiscal capacity; low unilateral control over grid decarbonisation. Practical control is much lower than formal authority suggests.

5. Responsibility as task. The state has the task of decarbonising its own system and of negotiating in good faith with regional partners.

6. Answerability and accountability. Answerability to its own citizens (democratic) and to affected states (weaker institutional basis).

7. Culpability and liability. Culpability is weakened by collective decision-making and long time horizons. Liability is possible via international agreement.

8. Limiting conditions. Shared sovereignty; historical ignorance of some consequences; proportionality of burden.

9. Intuitive verdict. The state should bear a fair share, but not the entire burden.

10. Framework verdict. Strong answerability to citizens; weak answerability to affected foreign nationals; liability conditional on a just international scheme.

11. Deviation and revision. If the framework assigns identical shares to a small state with no historical emissions and a large historical emitter, its reach category is under-theorised: it needs an explicit historical weighting. This is a revision point, not a defence of the framework.

B.3 Supply-chain responsibility

1. Case description. A multinational retailer discovers credible evidence of labour-rights abuses at a tier-two supplier, with which it has no direct contractual relationship.

2. Agents and institutions. The retailer, its direct supplier, the tier-two supplier, the local state, the affected workers, and the NGO sector that surfaced the evidence.

3. Causal contribution. The retailer contributes indirectly through purchasing practices, price pressure, and delivery deadlines.

4. Control and capacity. The retailer has substantial economic leverage but limited legal jurisdiction. Its capacity to act is real but bounded.

5. Responsibility as task. The retailer has a task of due diligence, monitoring, and remediation support. It does not have a task of direct employment.

6. Answerability and accountability. Answerability to investors, consumers, and workers; accountability via reporting requirements, certifications, and reputational mechanisms.

7. Culpability and liability. Culpability depends on knowledge and capacity to influence. Liability is contractual and jurisdictional, not general.

8. Limiting conditions. Causal distance; jurisdictional fragmentation; the possibility of exit by the supplier.

9. Intuitive verdict. Responsibility exists, but it should be proportionate to influence, and should not collapse into full ownership of the supplier's conduct.

10. Framework verdict. The framework's fiduciary structure captures the retailer-supplier relationship well: loyalty to the affected workers, discretion in purchasing, accountability to consumers and regulators.

11. Deviation and revision. The framework tends to over-weight formal contractual relations and under-weight informal market power. Revision proposed: the fiduciary structure should be complemented by an explicit market-power criterion when the formal relation is thin. This revision is not yet integrated into the main argument (§A.4, problem 2).

Why this appendix is separated. The three cases in §14 are the ones that carry the argument: a case where the framework works, a case where it initially fails and then recovers, and a case where it is tested. The cases here are extensions, not load-bearing. They belong in an appendix because they show the framework's reach without requiring the reader to accept it in order to follow the main argument.

Glossary

Answerability
The obligation to provide reasons, justify decisions, and remain open to examination and revision. Broader than culpability, narrower than responsibility.
Accountability
The institutional or social arrangements through which answerability can be examined and, where appropriate, enforced.
Asymmetric caution
The default of presuming capacity where it is uncertain, rather than denying it — because denial of capacity has historically been the mechanism of exclusion.
Cosmic symbiosis
A regulative ideal of coexistence in which different forms of existence preserve the conditions for one another's continued existence and development while remaining distinct.
Culpability
Blameworthiness in light of knowledge, intentions, alternatives, and reasonable opportunities to act differently. Narrower than responsibility.
Fiduciary responsibility
Responsibility held for another rather than over them, exercised in the other's interest and structured by the five features of principal, fiduciary, loyalty, discretion, and accountability.
Graded responsibility
The view that responsibility comes in degrees and can be distributed across multiple agents, rather than being binary.
Moral status
That something's interests count, or would count if it had them. A claim about consideration, marked by susceptibility, direction, and standing.
Reach
The extent to which an agent's actions can systematically shape the conditions under which others exercise agency. Assessed through six dimensions: magnitude, duration, scope, foreseeability, control, and asymmetry.
Reparative responsibility
Obligations to prevent, correct, or remedy adverse outcomes. May arise from causal contribution, control, benefit, institutional position, or special capacity to repair.
Structural responsibility
The forward-looking obligation to contribute to reform or mitigation of a structural process in which one participates, where that process systematically shapes the conditions of agency of others in ways that cannot be traced to identifiable individual contributions. Distinguished from responsibility-as-task by its participatory (rather than positional) basis, and from liability by its forward-looking rather than reparative orientation.
Systematic conditioning
The ongoing structural shaping of another's conditions of agency, distinguished from incidental causal effects.

About this text

This is an independent philosophical essay. It has not been peer reviewed, and it does not claim the authority of an established tradition. It is offered as a framework — a set of distinctions that may be useful for reasoning about responsibility in contexts where capability is growing faster than accountability.

The text is deliberately self-contained in its argument, though it engages with the work of others (see References). Readers who wish to test the framework are invited to apply it to cases the essay does not discuss, and to look for the places where it strains.

Editorial note: what changed in this edition

Version 19 builds on the argumentative restructuring introduced in v18 and adds one substantive change: an eighth concept, structural responsibility (§8.13). The concept fills a gap documented in §14.2 — where reach cannot be traced to identifiable agents, the framework previously had nothing determinate to say. §8.13 proposes a forward-looking, participatory obligation to contribute to reform of the structures one sustains, and defends its relation to Young's (2011) social-connection model in three layers.

The reason for the change is that a framework which fails on a documented case should ask what category is missing, not simply record the failure. §8.13 is the result of asking that question about §14.2. But the category is narrow — it does not generate liability, and it does not resolve allocation — and §14.2 therefore remains a partial failure rather than a full recovery.
