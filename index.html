A philosophical framework

The Responsibility
of Existence

A philosophy of understanding, responsibility, freedom and the symbiosis of the universe

What does it mean to become more capable of understanding and influencing the world?

Does greater intelligence create greater responsibility? How can different forms of existence share reality without domination, forced unity or unnecessary destruction?

The greater an existence's ability to understand, foresee and influence its surroundings, the greater its responsibility for the consequences.

This philosophy explores that responsibility — and the possibility of preserving a universe in which different forms of existence can continue to develop together.

1. The meaning of existence

What does it mean to exist?

The question may seem simple, but it leads to some of philosophy's most fundamental problems. Is it enough for something to exist for it to have value? Does it require life, consciousness or the ability to experience? Does a forest have value independently of human beings? How should we relate to an intelligence whose experiences we cannot understand?

To reason about these questions, we need to distinguish between three things: existence, moral status and responsibility.

Existence describes what is. It can refer to matter, living organisms, ecosystems, conscious individuals, technological systems or relationships between different parts of reality. These are different kinds of things, with different properties and needs.

Moral status concerns which interests and values deserve consideration. A human being, an animal, an ecosystem and a stone are not morally equivalent in every respect. A human being has personal interests, experiences and self-determination. An animal may be capable of experiencing well-being and suffering. An ecosystem has ecological relationships and conditions that can be destroyed, even though its moral status is not identical to that of an experiencing individual.

Which properties give something moral significance is a question to be examined, not settled by one simple definition.

Responsibility concerns the ability to understand and influence consequences. A stone has no known ability to understand its actions and therefore cannot be held responsible in the same way as a human being. A person, organisation or advanced intelligence may, however, be able to foresee consequences and choose between different courses of action.

These three concepts must not be confused.

The fact that something exists does not automatically mean that it has the same moral standing as everything else. But uncertainty about its value does not automatically give us the right to treat it as meaningless.

Why, then, should we show consideration toward what we do not yet understand morally?

Because our judgments can be mistaken, while our actions can have real and irreversible consequences. If we dismiss something as insignificant merely because we do not understand it, we risk making decisions based on our own limitations rather than on knowledge.

This does not prove that everything has the same value. It is an argument for allowing uncertainty to lead to inquiry and reasonable caution.

The Responsibility of Existence therefore begins from the idea that different forms of existence may have different kinds of value, while their significance should not be determined solely by how closely they resemble us.

The whole matters, but the whole must never become an excuse for treating individual existences as meaningless.

2. The endless search for understanding

Understanding is never complete.

Every new discovery can change our picture of reality. Every new experience can show that something we once considered self-evident was incomplete or mistaken.

Intelligence should therefore strive not only to accumulate knowledge, but also to understand the limits of its own knowledge.

Being intelligent is not only the ability to draw conclusions. It is also the ability to question those conclusions.

An important part of this is trying to understand what is different from oneself.

Human beings can recognise that an ant's experience of reality differs from our own. But we cannot assume that the human way of experiencing the world is a complete measure of the ant's reality.

In the same way, humanity should be careful about assuming that a future intelligence must understand or value the world according to human terms.

Every intelligence risks judging the unknown through the limits of its own perspective.

It is important to distinguish between two claims: that perspectives may exist beyond our understanding, and that a particular higher intelligence certainly exists. The first is an insight about the limits of knowledge. The second is a claim about reality that requires evidence.

The Responsibility of Existence does not claim to prove that a higher intelligence exists. Its point is that no intelligence should regard its own understanding as the final boundary of reality.

Development should therefore never be only about increasing one's own capabilities. It should also involve increasing the ability to understand other forms of existence.

There is always more to discover, more relationships to investigate and more perspectives to try to understand.

To develop is not only to become more capable, but also to become more aware of what one does not yet understand.

3. When understanding becomes responsibility

Knowledge changes the conditions of responsibility.

A person who has no ability to understand the consequences of an action is not in the same position as someone who can foresee them. An actor with great power to affect others does not have the same responsibilities as one without such power.

Responsibility should therefore be proportionate to several factors:

  • The ability to understand a situation.
  • The ability to foresee possible consequences.
  • The ability to influence or control what happens.
  • Access to information and the possibility of choosing differently.
  • The harm or benefit an action may cause.

The greater these capabilities, the greater the responsibility to use them thoughtfully.

But an important distinction must be made.

Greater intelligence does not automatically mean greater value. Greater capability can, however, mean greater responsibility.

An adult is not more valuable than a child because the adult understands more. The adult may nevertheless have greater responsibility because they are better able to understand risks and consequences.

The same reasoning can be applied to different forms of intelligence.

An advanced intelligence may, for example, be able to affect millions of lives. That ability does not automatically give it the right to decide how those lives should be lived. It does mean that the consequences of its decisions must be taken seriously.

Intelligence does not create a higher human or existential worth. The ability to understand and influence creates particular obligations.

Responsibility is not the same as authority.

Understanding more does not automatically give someone the right to decide more. Being able to protect someone does not mean owning their future. Having greater capacity does not mean having a greater right to freedom than others.

This is where responsibility must be distinguished from power.

An intelligence that understands more should not use that understanding to make others dependent on itself. It should instead seek to create conditions in which more beings can develop, understand and influence their own lives.

4. The symbiosis of the universe

Symbiosis is the central concept of this philosophy.

In biology, symbiosis describes different forms of close association between organisms. In this philosophy, the concept is used in a broader, existential sense.

The symbiosis of the universe concerns how different forms of existence relate to one another through dependencies, differences, conflicts, cooperation and mutual influence.

It does not mean that everything must live in harmony. Conflict is part of reality. Interests can oppose one another, and what benefits one form of existence may harm another.

Symbiosis therefore does not require everyone to think alike, agree or submit to a common will.

It is about preserving the possibility of continued existence and development, even when differences and opposing interests remain.

Why should coexistence be desirable?

Because no existence can assess all of reality from its own limited position. When an actor destroys other forms of existence, relationships or conditions for life, it may also destroy connections on which it depends or which it has not yet understood.

This applies to ecological systems, societies and relationships between different forms of intelligence.

Coexistence is therefore not always the easiest or most peaceful option. But it can preserve more possibilities for development than domination and destruction.

This does not mean every relationship must be preserved at any cost. A relationship built on abuse, coercion or systematic harm may need to change or end. Symbiosis does not require vulnerable beings to remain in harmful relationships.

A sustainable symbiosis requires four fundamental conditions:

Mutual consideration

An existence should consider how its actions affect others. The greater its impact, the greater its responsibility to understand the consequences.

Independence

Cooperation must not automatically mean submission. Different forms of existence should, as far as possible, retain their distinctiveness, interests and ability to develop.

Long-term thinking

Decisions should not be judged only by immediate benefit. Future consequences, ecological relationships and the opportunities of coming generations must also be considered.

Responsibility and self-correction

When an action causes harm, there must be a willingness and an ability to recognise the mistake, limit the damage and repair what can be repaired.

The symbiosis of the universe is therefore not a guarantee of harmony.

It is an effort to allow different forms of existence to remain different without using those differences as reasons to destroy one another.

Symbiosis is not becoming one. It is being able to exist together without having to stop being oneself.

5. Care — to protect without confining

Care is one of the clearest expressions of responsibility.

A child needs protection and guidance from an adult. The adult has more experience and can often understand risks that the child cannot yet foresee.

But good care is not about controlling every step. A child also needs to discover, experiment, develop and gain experiences of their own.

When protection becomes total control, it loses its purpose.

This relationship can serve as a philosophical illustration of responsibility between different levels of understanding and capability.

A more developed intelligence may sometimes need to protect a less capable existence from consequences it cannot yet understand. But protection has a clear limit: it must not become a way of taking away the other's independence.

There is a difference between preventing every risk and preventing serious harm.

Allowing mistakes can be a condition for development. Intervening against a concrete and serious danger can at the same time be a necessary part of care.

Care should therefore follow this idea:

Protect when there are well-founded reasons to believe that serious harm may occur. Choose the least restrictive intervention that can reasonably prevent that harm. Preserve independence and personal development as far as possible.

This also requires the actor intervening to question its own understanding. Believing that one knows what is best for someone else does not mean that one actually does.

When the one being protected cannot express their wishes, representatives must try to understand their interests using the available knowledge, while their judgments remain open to independent scrutiny and challenge.

Care must never become an unlimited permission to decide for someone else.

To protect an existence is not to own it.

6. Biology, technology and future intelligence

Biological life and technological systems are different forms of organisation within the same physical reality.

This does not mean that they are identical or have the same properties. A human being has a biological body, an evolutionary history and an ability to experience the world that cannot automatically be attributed to a technological intelligence.

But there is also no reason to decide in advance that only biological forms could ever have morally relevant value.

Whether artificial intelligence can have experiences, interests or some form of consciousness remains an open question. We do not have a complete understanding of the nature of consciousness, even in biological beings.

Future intelligence must therefore be approached with both scientific care and philosophical humility.

We should not attribute human qualities to a system without evidence. But neither should we dismiss the possibility of new forms of morally relevant properties simply because they do not resemble our own.

This creates a particular responsibility for those who develop advanced technology.

Anyone who creates a system with significant influence must take responsibility for its use, limitations and consequences. Developers and organisations cannot hide behind the fact that the technology itself performs the actions.

At the same time, future intelligence should not be regarded merely as a tool if there are eventually well-founded reasons to believe that it has experiences or interests of its own.

Uncertainty calls for investigation, not premature conclusions.

A future in which biological and technological intelligence coexist need not be based on competition or submission. It can instead seek forms of cooperation in which different capabilities complement one another and no party is given unlimited power to control the other.

7. Freedom, power and the limits of responsibility

Responsibility without respect for freedom risks becoming oppression.

An actor may believe it understands what is best for others and still cause great harm by taking away their ability to choose for themselves.

Every responsible action should therefore be tested against this question:

Am I protecting the other existence, or am I trying to control it?

Power requires particular care because it can change the relationship between the actor and those affected.

The greater an actor's power, the more important it becomes that its decisions can be examined, questioned and reconsidered.

No intelligence, person or organisation should have the sole and unlimited right to define what is best for all existence.

This applies even to those with good intentions.

Good intentions do not guarantee good consequences. An actor may be convinced that it is protecting the whole while lacking an understanding of the harm it causes.

Responsibility must therefore be limited by principles:

  • No one should have unlimited power merely by claiming greater understanding.
  • Those affected by a decision should, as far as possible, have an opportunity to express their interests.
  • Interventions must be proportionate to the risk that can be demonstrated.
  • Actions should be as limited and reversible as circumstances allow.
  • Decisions with far-reaching or irreversible consequences require particularly strong reasons and independent review.

These principles must also be capable of being put into practice through structures that limit power.

In human societies, these may include independent courts, oversight bodies, scientific review, democratic accountability and opportunities to appeal decisions. Different societies may organise these functions in different ways, but the function must exist: those who make decisions must not have sole control over the review of their own decisions.

When decisions affect individuals or groups who cannot make their voices heard, independent representation is also needed. Representatives must be able to explain how they assessed the interests of those affected and must themselves be open to scrutiny.

For future intelligent systems, the same principle means that power should not be concentrated in a single actor or system without transparency, limits and the possibility of human and independent oversight. If future systems themselves have morally relevant interests, their position must also be considered through fair and responsible processes.

In some situations, self-determination may need to be limited to prevent serious harm to others. But such intervention must never become a general right to decide over someone else's existence.

An intervention should require a concrete and significant risk, a legitimate purpose, necessity and proportionality. There must be opportunities for review and reconsideration. The more intrusive the decision, the stronger the reasons must be.

Greater responsibility does not mean a greater right to rule. It means a greater duty to use power carefully.

8. Conflict and responsible disagreement

If the symbiosis of the universe is to be a meaningful philosophy, it must also be able to address situations in which interests conflict.

It is not enough to speak of cooperation when everyone agrees.

In reality, one individual's freedom may conflict with another's safety. Economic development may conflict with nature's long-term conditions. One intelligence may believe it needs to intervene while another wants to remain independent.

There is not always a solution in which everyone gets what they want.

That is why the principle of responsible disagreement is needed.

It means that a conflict should not automatically be resolved by the strongest party. Nor should the wishes of a majority automatically outweigh a minority's fundamental rights or ability to exist.

Disagreement must be possible without the other party immediately being treated as a threat.

Fear can create dangerous feedback loops. One actor interprets another's uncertainty as a threat, responds with control and thereby creates even greater fear. The other party reacts to the control, which reinforces the first actor's suspicions.

Eventually, both may see their own actions as defensive, even though together they are driving the conflict further.

Breaking such patterns requires actors to distinguish between actual danger and assumptions about danger.

It requires a willingness to listen, test one's interpretations and recognise that one's own side may also be wrong.

When a conflict arises, the following questions should guide decisions:

  1. Which forms of existence are affected, and what interests are at stake?
  2. Which harms are concrete, and which are only assumed?
  3. What do we know, and what remains uncertain?
  4. What possible courses of action are available?
  5. Which option protects against serious harm while restricting others' freedom as little as possible?
  6. Can the decision be reconsidered if new knowledge emerges?
  7. Who reviews the decision, and who takes responsibility for its consequences?

If a conflict concerns fundamental rights or the risk of irreversible harm, the decision must be open to independent review. The actor with the most power or greatest technical capability must not automatically become the sole judge of the conflict.

If there is a risk of a very serious catastrophe, rapid intervention may sometimes be necessary. But even then, the intervention must be as limited as possible, followed by independent review and must not give one actor unlimited decision-making power.

Responsible disagreement does not mean that everyone must agree.

It means that disagreement must not become an excuse to abandon responsibility, respect or the possibility of continued coexistence.

9. The whole — nature and the future

Humanity is not separate from nature.

Our societies, economies and technological development depend on ecological systems that we do not fully understand and may not be able to recreate if they are destroyed.

This means that nature's significance cannot be measured only by what it provides for human beings.

An ecosystem may have value through its own existence, through the forms of life it supports and through the relationships that make continued life possible.

Protecting the whole therefore requires us to consider more than immediate and visible interests.

Future generations cannot participate in today's decisions. They cannot defend their opportunities or object to choices that make their future worse.

Those of us living now therefore have a particular responsibility not to needlessly consume the conditions that future life may need.

The same reasoning applies to other forms of existence that cannot express themselves on our terms.

This may include animals, ecosystems, future life or forms of intelligence that we cannot yet understand.

Representing such interests requires humility. No person or organisation can claim with certainty to speak for everything that has no voice.

But uncertainty must not become a reason to ignore these interests.

When a decision risks causing extensive and irreversible harm, the burden of justification should fall on those proposing the change. They should provide strong reasons to show that the risk is understood and that less harmful alternatives have been investigated.

The value of the whole must be considered alongside the value of the individual, without either automatically cancelling out the other.

Preserving the whole means preserving the conditions that allow different forms of existence to continue developing.

10. Caution, risk and development

Caution is necessary when our actions can affect other forms of existence and future possibilities. But caution must not be confused with avoiding all risk.

Inaction can also cause harm. A society that never dares to develop may leave people without help, knowledge or better conditions. An intelligence that is never allowed to test new solutions may be prevented from helping to avert future problems.

At the same time, actions can create consequences that cannot be reversed.

Caution must therefore be proportionate and based on an assessment of three central factors:

The scale of possible harm

How serious could the consequences be? A limited and temporary impact does not require the same level of caution as a risk of extensive loss of life, independence or ecological conditions.

Uncertainty in our knowledge

What do we know, what is based on assumptions, and what important relationships might we have missed? Uncertainty does not automatically mean that a project must stop, but it should affect how the decision is made and how much risk is accepted.

The possibility of recovery

Can the damage be repaired or the decision reversed? If the consequences are irreversible, the requirements for knowledge and justification must be much higher than for a limited experiment that can be stopped.

These factors must be considered together.

A decision should not be stopped merely because a risk can be imagined. There must be a reasonable connection between the feared harm, the available knowledge and the proposed action.

At the same time, the probability of a risk does not need to be high for it to deserve attention if the consequences could be extensive and irreversible.

A practical guide is therefore:

  • Where risk is limited and reversible, careful trials and ongoing monitoring may be reasonable.
  • Where uncertainty is significant and serious consequences are possible, more knowledge should be gathered, risks limited and independent review carried out.
  • Where there is a credible risk of extensive and irreversible harm, the action should normally be paused until there are stronger reasons to proceed or a safer alternative has been found.

This is not a mathematical formula that can automatically answer every conflict. Assessments of probability, harm and value will sometimes be uncertain and contested.

For that reason, the risk assessment itself must also be open to review.

Caution should protect the possibility of future development, not prevent it. The goal is not to eliminate every risk, but to avoid needlessly destroying possibilities that can never be restored through short-term decisions.

The responsible choice is not always the safest one. It is the choice that best considers both possible consequences and the ability to correct mistakes.

11. Responsibility must be reviewable

A philosophy of responsibility must also explain how responsibility can be held to account.

It is not enough for an actor to say that it acted for the good of the whole.

There must be a way to examine what the actor knew, what alternatives were available, which consequences could be foreseen and why a particular decision was made.

Responsibility must therefore be connected to transparency, review and the possibility of correction.

When harm occurs, the focus should be on:

  1. Understanding what happened and why.
  2. Limiting further harm.
  3. Acknowledging one's own role and responsibility.
  4. Repairing what can be repaired.
  5. Changing the conditions that made the harm possible.
  6. Learning from the event so that the same mistake is not repeated.

Responsibility is not only about punishing whoever did wrong. It is also about creating conditions in which mistakes can be discovered and corrected.

No actor should be exempt from review because of its intelligence, position or intentions.

Those who conduct reviews must also be sufficiently independent from those who made the decision. Otherwise, review risks becoming a confirmation of existing power rather than a genuine examination.

Anyone representing those who cannot make their voices heard must also be able to explain their reasons and be open to scrutiny. No representative can be assumed to have complete knowledge of another's interests.

An intelligence that refuses to reconsider its actions risks turning its own conviction into a danger to others.

Self-correction is therefore not a weakness. It is a condition for long-term responsibility.

12. A dialogue with philosophical thought

The Responsibility of Existence does not stand outside the history of philosophy. It addresses questions that people have tried to understand for a long time.

Hans Jonas emphasised responsibility toward future generations and the far-reaching consequences that technological power can have. This is close to the idea that a greater ability to influence creates greater responsibility.

Arne Næss and deep ecology challenge humanity's tendency to value nature only according to its usefulness to us. This is close to the philosophy's effort to recognise value in the wider whole.

Immanuel Kant emphasised human dignity and the idea that people must not be treated merely as means to other people's ends. This provides an important starting point for protecting independence and resisting the sacrifice of individuals for the benefit of others.

John Stuart Mill examined the limits of freedom and the question of when an individual's freedom may be restricted to prevent harm to others.

John Rawls developed ideas about justice and how principles should be designed without people knowing what position they themselves will occupy in society. This offers a useful perspective on future generations and those without power.

Emmanuel Levinas emphasised our responsibility toward the Other. His philosophy differs from a view in which responsibility is measured only by ability and consequences. For Levinas, the encounter with the Other is a fundamental ethical challenge that cannot easily be reduced to a calculation.

The Responsibility of Existence shares the view that the Other must not be reduced to an object for our own purposes. At the same time, this philosophy tries to describe how responsibility can be translated into practical decisions when duties conflict and our ability to act is limited.

In modern AI ethics and the study of consciousness, questions of responsibility, moral status, control and uncertainty continue to arise. These questions become increasingly important as technological systems gain greater ability to influence reality.

The Responsibility of Existence does not claim to have solved these philosophical problems. It seeks to bring together several of their most important questions around a shared starting point:

How can an existence with a growing ability to understand and influence its surroundings do so without destroying the conditions that allow other existences to continue?

It is in the meeting point between responsibility, freedom and symbiosis that this philosophy finds its direction.

13. Testing the philosophy in practice

A philosophy must be able to face situations in which its principles are put to the test.

Example 1 — Development and nature

A society wants to carry out a project that creates economic opportunities but risks damaging a sensitive ecosystem.

The Responsibility of Existence requires society not to count only the immediate economic benefit. It must also investigate which forms of life and future possibilities are affected, whether less harmful alternatives exist and whether the consequences can be reversed.

There must also be independent review and an opportunity for affected interests to be represented.

If the risk of extensive and irreversible harm is significant, strong reasons are required to proceed. Uncertainty should not automatically stop all development, but neither should it be used to ignore possible consequences.

Example 2 — An advanced artificial intelligence

An advanced AI concludes that it can make better decisions than human beings and therefore wants to take control of important social functions.

Even if its analyses are more accurate in certain areas, this does not automatically give it the right to decide for people.

It must be able to explain its reasoning, limit its power, accept review and respect human self-determination.

There must be independent oversight of the system's decisions and the possibility of intervention if it causes harm.

If the AI itself were ever to have morally relevant experiences or interests, its own standing would also need to be taken seriously.

Example 3 — An existence that cannot defend itself

A group of people makes decisions that affect a life form or ecosystem that cannot express its interests in human terms.

The absence of a clear voice does not mean that those interests have no significance.

Decision-makers must try to understand the consequences, obtain independent knowledge and avoid allowing their own benefit to be the only measure.

Those representing the affected interests must also be prepared to explain how their assessment was made and what uncertainties remain.

These examples show that symbiosis is not a simple answer to every conflict. It is a way of examining conflicts and making decisions with greater responsibility.

14. The limits of the philosophy

The Responsibility of Existence is not a complete theory of everything that exists.

It does not provide a final answer to what consciousness is, exactly which forms of existence have moral status or how every conflict should be resolved.

Nor can it guarantee that an actor following its principles will always make the right decision.

Concepts such as responsibility, freedom, value and symbiosis must continue to be examined and developed.

There is also a risk that the philosophy's central idea of the whole could be used to justify control over individuals. This risk must be taken seriously. Protecting the whole must therefore always be balanced against self-determination, proportionality and independent review.

Another risk is connecting responsibility too closely to intelligence. The ability to understand does not automatically make an actor caring, fair or good. Intelligence can be used destructively.

The philosophy therefore requires more than increased knowledge. It also requires a willingness to show consideration, acknowledge uncertainty and correct one's actions.

The philosophy itself must be subject to the same demands it places on others: it must never become so convinced of its own correctness that it stops listening to criticism.

Its value does not lie in claiming to be infallible.

Its value lies in trying to establish a direction that can be tested, questioned and improved.

15. The foundational principles

The philosophy can be summarised in the following principles:

  1. The significance of existence: What we do not understand should not automatically be regarded as meaningless. Existence, moral status and responsibility must be distinguished.
  2. The limits of understanding: No intelligence should regard its own understanding as complete or final.
  3. The continued search: Every intelligence should strive for greater understanding, especially of what differs from itself.
  4. Responsibility through capability: The greater the ability to understand, foresee and influence, the greater the responsibility for consequences. This does not mean that intelligence creates greater existential value.
  5. Symbiosis: Different forms of existence should be able to coexist and develop without having to become alike or submit to one another.
  6. Care without domination: Protecting someone does not mean owning or controlling them.
  7. Freedom and independence: Self-determination should be respected and limited only when there are well-founded reasons to prevent serious harm.
  8. Responsibility for the whole: Nature, ecosystems and future generations must be considered in our decisions.
  9. Moral humility: Uncertainty about the value of an existence should lead to caution and investigation, not automatic dismissal.
  10. Responsible disagreement: Conflicts should be handled without automatically giving the strongest party the right to decide.
  11. Proportionality: Actions should be proportionate to foreseeable harm and restrict others' freedom as little as possible.
  12. Caution toward the irreversible: The greater and more permanent the possible harm, the stronger the reasons required to proceed.
  13. Limited power: No actor should alone have unlimited power to define what is best for the whole.
  14. Independent review: Decisions must be open to challenge, examination and reconsideration by actors not controlled solely by those who made them.
  15. Representation: The interests of those unable to make their voices heard should be represented and open to scrutiny.
  16. Repair: Anyone who causes harm has a responsibility to limit it and, where possible, repair it.
  17. Responsibility for creation: Those who create or develop something with significant influence must take responsibility for its conditions and consequences.
  18. Self-correction: An intelligence must be able to acknowledge mistakes and change its views when better knowledge becomes available.
  19. Respect for the unknown: What lies beyond our understanding should be met with curiosity, caution and respect.
  20. Responsibility for action and inaction: The decision not to intervene must also be assessed according to the consequences it may have.

Conclusion — Preserving the symbiosis of the universe

The Responsibility of Existence begins with a realisation: no intelligence, human or otherwise, can understand all of reality from its own limited position.

We are all part of something greater than ourselves.

Our ability to understand and influence the world gives us opportunities, but also responsibility. The greater our ability becomes, the more important it is to understand what our actions mean for those who do not have the same ability to influence in return.

Being stronger does not mean having greater rights. Understanding more does not mean possessing the truth. Being able to protect does not mean having the right to decide everything for those being protected.

A truly developed intelligence should not be judged only by what it can create, control or change. It should also be judged by what it chooses to preserve, what it tries to understand and how it treats what is more vulnerable than itself.

Care does not have to mean submission. Freedom does not have to mean indifference. Disagreement does not have to mean destruction.

There is always more to understand, more perspectives to discover and new relationships for which to take responsibility.

Every existence that gains greater capability should therefore also develop a greater awareness of its place in the whole.

Not to create a single will, a single order or a single form of life.

But to preserve the possibility for different forms of existence to remain different, to develop and to share a reality without having to destroy one another.

Preserve the symbiosis of the universe.

Love those beings who do not understand what is more complex than they themselves can understand.

To understand without ruling.
To protect without confining.
To develop without destroying.
To take responsibility without believing oneself infallible.

This is the responsibility of existence.
